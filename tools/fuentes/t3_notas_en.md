### Diapositiva 3 · 3.1. Sport in Crete and Mycenae

**`Notas del orador`**

> This section studies sport in Bronze Age Crete, between the first palaces, c. 1950 BC, and the destruction of Knossos, c. 1375 BC. Crete offers the first European body of images rich enough to raise the question of the social function of a bodily practice, yet it leaves not a single text on sport, so that everything said in this section derives from frescoes, vases, seals and buildings. The order is that of the whole topic: first geography, power and economy, then how the interpretation of the images has changed and, finally, why Cretan sport takes the form that it does. In Crete there are as yet no competitions with prizes or lists of victors, and the section deals with physical activities of ritual, social and political significance that the palace organises and displays.

---

### Diapositiva 4 · Island of Crete Minoan culture

**`Notas del orador`**

> The map serves to establish three ideas before speaking of sport. Crete is a mountainous and fragmented island, and its relief explains why several palatial centres coexisted with a mosaic of small independent communities (Tomkins & Schoep, 2010). In the Neopalatial period there were palaces at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, Zakros and Galatas, and a palatial building at Petras, in the east of the island; Knossos was the largest, but it is not clear that it governed the whole island (Hallager, 2010). The third idea is Crete's position in the sea, since its trade linked it with Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, and this contact was so close that painters trained in the Minoan world, coming from Knossos or Thera, ended up decorating an Egyptian palace in the Nile delta (Kyle, 2015, p. 43). Thera, to the north, was an island of Minoan culture, and the eruption that buried its city of Akrotiri preserved under the ash entire houses decorated with frescoes, which are one of the main sources for our knowledge of physical activities in the Aegean world.

---

### Diapositiva 5 · The palace of Knossos

**`Notas del orador`**

> Arthur Evans began to excavate on the hill of Kefala, at Knossos, at the end of March 1900, although the first storage jars of the palace had been brought to light years earlier by Minos Kalokairinos (Serrano Espinosa, 1996, p. 158). The plan is organised around a great central court, surrounded by the residence, the storerooms and the cult rooms (Serrano Espinosa, 1996, p. 124). Its irregular layout, with rooms added one to another, evokes the Labyrinth, the structure in which, according to Greek myth, the Minotaur, a being half man and half bull, lived confined, and Kyle (2015, p. 42) expressly relates that Labyrinth to the plan of the palace.
>
> The central court measured between a quarter and a third of a stadion, the Greek unit of length of some 180-190 metres (Younger, 2021, p. 48). This size, at the heart of the building, makes it the great open space of the palace and a place suited to gathering the community for ceremonies and spectacles, and this centrality explains why Minoan sport is associated from the outset with the palace precinct.

### Diapositiva 6 · Historical introduction

**`Notas del orador`**

> The periodisation followed in this topic is that of Rutter (2014, pp. 37-42): a Prepalatial phase, c. 3100-1950 BC; a Protopalatial phase, c. 1950-1750 BC, with the first palaces; the Neopalatial, c. 1750-1450 BC, which accounts for almost all the sporting images; and a Monopalatial phase, c. 1450-1375 BC, in which only Knossos survives under an administration that writes in Mycenaean Greek. Kyle (2015, p. 38) calls the Neopalatial phase the ‘era of the great palaces’ and dates it to c. 1650-1450 BC. Serrano Espinosa (1996, pp. 111 & 122) explains the first palaces of Knossos, Phaistos and Malia as the result of internal evolution, without invasion, and attributes their destruction c. 1700 BC to a general catastrophe, in all probability an earthquake.
>
> For decades people spoke of a ‘Pax Minoica’, a Crete without wars as against a warlike Mycenae. Today it is known that the earliest weapons of the Aegean are Cretan and that fortifications have been found at Petras (Georganas, 2010), and Marinatos (2005, p. 158) concludes that young men trained at once in war, hunting and athletics.
>
> The eruption of the volcano of Thera, present-day Santorini, is dated by radiocarbon to c. 1660-1613 BC (Manning, 2010), and Crowther (2007, p. 38) places it in 1628 BC. It left ash and pumice in the east of Crete and some quarters of Gournia were not rebuilt, but the island recovered and arrived wealthy at the end of the palace period (Hallager, 2010). The old date of c. 1500 BC, which made Thera the cause of the end of the palaces, has been superseded. The Mycenaean takeover of Knossos is dated between c. 1450 and c. 1400 BC (Kyle, 2015, pp. 38 & 46), and the destruction of the palace c. 1375 BC (Rutter, 2014, p. 47).
>
> Rutter (2014, p. 36) is categorical in writing that no text on sport has been discovered in any of the scripts of the Aegean Bronze Age, and Linear B consists mostly of palace records (Crowther, 2007, p. 34). The tradition of the thalassocracy of Minos, the mastery of the sea attributed to him by Thucydides (1.4) and Strabo (10.4.8), is a Greek account written many centuries later and is told orally.
>
> This framework conditions the way Cretan sport was practised and is known. Without texts, Minoan sport can only be reconstructed from the images that the palaces commissioned, so that what we see is what the elite wished to show of itself; and the military preparation of young men to which Marinatos alludes explains why hunting, unarmed combat and tests of risk occupy so much space in those images.

**`Fuente`**

> Rutter, J. (2014). Sport in the Aegean Bronze Age. In P. Christesen & D. G. Kyle (Eds.), *A companion to sport and spectacle in Greek and Roman antiquity* (pp. 36-52). Wiley Blackwell.

### Diapositiva 8 · Minoan women

**`Notas del orador`**

> The cautious formulation is that of Scanlon (2014, p. 39), who considers it at least clear that women played in Minoan life a much more active role than in the later Greek cities, with the possible exception of Sparta. Chapin (2010) stresses the prominent place of women in the frescoes of Knossos, from an early procession fresco to the ‘Ladies in Blue’, the ‘Lady in Red’ and the two miniature frescoes of the Grandstand and the Sacred Grove, compositions that show elite women, great crowds and palatial settings, and that express the priorities of the Neopalatial rulers, that is, elite display, public performance and communal identity.
>
> Chapin (2010) describes the colour convention as taken from Egyptian art, with males in red tones and females in white, and Newman (2017, p. 213) recalls that it was Evans who fixed it for the Aegean, on the Egyptian model, when he published the first frescoes of Knossos. The difficulty is that Minoan art scarcely paints sexual features, since it never depicts female genitalia and at Knossos painted breasts are rare, in large part because of the fragmentary state of the frescoes (Newman, 2017, p. 216). Those that survive are eloquent, such as the seated white figure with pendulous breasts in the Grandstand fresco, the two large-breasted dancers of the Sacred Grove fresco or the curved bodice that outlines the breast of one of the ‘Ladies in Blue’, and the difference in size indicates that the painter distinguished degrees of female maturity (p. 216).
>
> Dress completes the identification. All these figures wear an open bodice and a flounced skirt of superimposed ruffles, a garment designed to leave the breast exposed, and at Knossos every figure with a breast has white skin, whereas no red figure wears that bodice, so that white skin, breast and bodice together construct female identity (Newman, 2017, pp. 216-217). Burke (2010) adds that the dress of the Minoan elite, with the flounced skirt of the so-called snake goddesses, was the individual work of highly skilled weavers, and that elite Mycenaean women later inherited the open bodice and the full skirt. In the male, the equivalent features are red skin, pronounced musculature and the kilt or the loincloth with codpiece (Newman, 2017, p. 218). Long hair, by contrast, does not distinguish sex, since in the Grandstand fresco red and white figures wear tresses down to the waist and similar hairstyles (p. 231), and jewellery appears equally on figures of either colour (p. 229).
>
> Female prominence is concentrated in cult and in spectacle. The miniature Grandstand fresco, of c. 1500 BC, shows numerous animated spectators, men and women, watching some activity (Kyle, 2015, p. 38). The Sacred Grove fresco brings together some fourteen women in richly adorned court dress, and it remains under discussion whether they are dancing or performing a sacred mime (Soar, 2014, p. 231). Aamodt (2014, pp. 48 & 51) associates Minoan dance with the epiphany of the deity and with initiation rites, and reads this fresco as a possible rite of passage of girls into adulthood. Shapland (2022, p. 26) interprets the frescoes with richly dressed women among wild goats and boars, such as that of the Park at Hagia Triada, as a way of showing the palatial group's control over the island's uplands through hunting, without any need to decide whether they were goddesses or priestesses. What the whole securely documents is female presence in spectacle and in cult, that is, who watched and who officiated.
>
> The question of Minoan women shows to what extent the meaning of an image depends on who looks at it. Evans sought in Crete a refined society dominated by the feminine, and restored it in accordance with that idea, so that any identification of a figure as a woman requires checking colour, breast and dress at the same time.

**`Fuente`**

> Newman, A. N. (2017). Queering the Minoans: Gender performativity and the Aegean color convention in fresco painting at Knossos. *Journal of Mediterranean Archaeology*, *30*(2), 213-236.
>
> Scanlon, T. F. (2014). Women, bull sports, cults, and initiation in Minoan Crete. In T. F. Scanlon (Ed.), *Sport in the Greek and Roman worlds: Vol. 1. Early Greece, the Olympics, and contests* (pp. 28-59). Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 9 · Physical and sporting activities of the Cretans

**`Notas del orador`**

> The documentary starting point is that of Rutter (2014, p. 36), who is categorical in writing that no text on sport has been discovered in any of the Minoan, Cycladic or Mycenaean scripts. The figures of the corpus are also his (p. 39), some one hundred objects or surfaces decorated with Neopalatial sporting images, the great majority with bull-leaping and between 10 and 20 % with unarmed combat.
>
> The palatial images show graceful, dynamic and stylised bodies, centred on skill and ritual, and this aesthetic coexisted with practices linked to strength and war, since the earliest weapons of the Aegean are Cretan, fortifications have been found at Petras (Georganas, 2010) and Marinatos (2005, p. 158) concludes that young men trained at once in war, hunting and athletics. What separates Crete from Egypt and from the Mycenaean kingdoms is that its palatial iconography scarcely glorifies war, and the unarmed combat shown in the images takes place within the setting of the palace, far from the battlefield.
>
> The Ring of the Runner, of c. 1600-1525/1500 BC, shows a male running on a flat surface, marked with two horizontal bands, between a woman who may be a priestess and a man in a cloak who holds what appears to be a sceptre. Lebessi and her collaborators read it as the dedication of a victorious runner, although the scene is unique and may represent a rite (Kyle, 2015, pp. 39-40).
>
> Chapin (2010) reads the stucco reliefs with athletes from the East Hall at Knossos, perhaps boxers or bull-leapers, as evidence of a palatial interest in the athlete and in competition. The acrobat who performs a backward somersault is from Rutter (2014, p. 38), and the board of c. 1600 BC with four ivory pieces found at Knossos is from Younger (2021, pp. 53-54). Dance appears often and never in competitive form, and Aamodt (2014, p. 48) associates it above all with the epiphany of the deity and with initiation rites; with the construction of the first palaces, its setting shifted from the tombs to the palaces (p. 54). Boar hunting had for Younger the meaning of a passage to adulthood.
>
> Soar (2014, pp. 227-228) traces the origin of bull iconography back to Early Minoan II, with bull-shaped rhyta from the tholos tombs of Koumasa and Porti in which several men wrestle with an animal that dominates the scene, and interprets them as a rural and communal activity. Some seven hundred years later, in the Neopalatial period, the human figures grow and the bull ceases to dominate the composition (p. 230). His general thesis is that sport and dance were public performances by which elite factions gained prestige and followers, so that a poor performance meant a loss of status, and that, together with religious ritual, they were one of the mechanisms by which Minoan society was created and sustained (pp. 235-237).
>
> The observation on prizes is Rutter's (2014, pp. 41 & 45) and organises the meaning of the whole section, since Minoan images display skill and hierarchy and in none do crowns, prizes or lists of victors appear. Kyle (2015, p. 38) sums it up by writing that the Minoans knew some of the activities of later Greek athletics, though not in the same contexts nor with prizes.

**`Fuente`**

> Rutter, J. (2014). Sport in the Aegean Bronze Age. In P. Christesen & D. G. Kyle (Eds.), *A companion to sport and spectacle in Greek and Roman antiquity* (pp. 36-52). Wiley Blackwell.
>
> Soar, K. (2014). Sects and the city: Factional ideologies in representations of performance from Bronze Age Crete. *World Archaeology*, *46*(2), 224-241.

### Diapositiva 10 · One sport, two fights, two ages: the same meaning?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The children of Akrotiri, between six and ten years old, wear a single glove, a belt, a loincloth and a cap (Younger, 2021, p. 50), and seem to practise without intent to injure (Kyle, 2015, pp. 38-39). The adults on the rhyton wear helmets, forearm protection and padded hands, and the vase leaves the defeated on the ground (Kyle, 2015, pp. 38-39).
>
> What can be deduced is that protection and severity increase with age, and that the victor is distinguished, since at Akrotiri the victorious boy wears jewellery and his rival is plainly dressed (Rutter, 2014, p. 41). Allowing for the differences, it is the logic of present-day sporting initiation.

### Diapositiva 11 · Boxing

**`Notas del orador`**

> Akrotiri, on the island of Thera, was a city of Minoan culture closely tied to Crete, whose houses were decorated with paintings in Minoan style, and the eruption of the volcano sealed them under the ash. Kyle (2015, pp. 38-39) gives two different dates for the same piece, c. 1625 BC in the text and c. 1550 BC in the figure caption, and the reasonable range is that of the late seventeenth century BC, before the eruption that sealed Akrotiri. Chapin (2010) places the fresco in Building Beta, next to that of the antelopes, and reads it as a scene of athletic training.
>
> Koehl (1986, p. 101) attends to the hairstyle. The boy boxers have the head shaved around loose locks, a treatment of the hair that in Egypt distinguished children of privileged standing, and their undeveloped musculature indicates early adolescence; the youths of the Fisherman fresco, somewhat older, have already cut off those locks, and Koehl proposes that the cutting formed part of the preparation for the next stage of a rite of passage. Soar (2014, pp. 231-232) recalls that, after bull-leaping, boxing is the most frequently represented Minoan sport, with more than twenty known images and figurines with gloves and protections deposited at the beginning of the Neopalatial period in the peak sanctuary of Kophinas.
>
> The social reading is Rutter's (2014, p. 41), who stresses the clear difference in status between the victorious boy, adorned with jewellery, and his opponent, dressed far more plainly, and who takes up Marinatos's interpretation, for whom the message of the painting is first competition through play and then the establishment of a hierarchy. Kyle (2015, pp. 38-39) adds that the context appears to be one of practice, without intent to injure, and that perhaps the boys were preparing for a later, harsher combat within an initiation ceremony.

**`Fuente`**

> Younger, J. G. (2021). Sport and spectacle in the Greek Bronze Age. In A. Futrell & T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 47-61). Oxford University Press.
>
> Rutter, J. (2014). Sport in the Aegean Bronze Age. In P. Christesen & D. G. Kyle (Eds.), *A companion to sport and spectacle in Greek and Roman antiquity* (pp. 36-52). Wiley Blackwell.

### Diapositiva 12 · The Boxer Rhyton


**`Notas del orador`**

> The rhyton is the most complete of the Minoan sporting images because it is the only stone vessel with sporting scenes that survives in more than one fragment and because it brings together in a single object several successive events (Rutter, 2014, p. 41). The measurements and the date come from Crowther (2007, p. 36), some eighteen inches in height and c. 1600-1500 BC; Rutter (2014, p. 42) dates it to c. 1550-1500 BC and Kyle (2015, p. 38) to c. 1550 BC. The vessel is made of black steatite and arrived heavily fragmented, so that a good part of what is seen today is restoration.
>
> Kyle (2015, pp. 38-39) describes the four bands. In the upper one, two men box and a column separates them from three others who appear to be boxers; in the second there is a bull-leap with a gored leaper, and Soar (2014, p. 229) specifies that two bulls charge and that one of them has thrown a man into the air; in the third, four men with helmets, forearm protections and padded hands, two standing and two on the ground; in the fourth, figures without helmets and with bandaged wrists, two on guard and two knocked down. Crowther (2007, pp. 36-37) likewise describes the four bands and the equipment shown in each.
>
> Marinatos (2005, p. 158) maintains that young elite men were trained at once in war, hunting and athletics, so that this boxing also served for strength and defence.
>
> The columns with square capitals point to a palatial context, and Soar (2014, pp. 231-232) counts twelve boxers on the rhyton and observes that on other pieces boxers appear beside columns, altars and trees, which suggests an official and perhaps ceremonial setting for boxing. On the meaning of the whole there are four readings with an author attached. Miller sees soldiers in combat, Kyle a programme of initiation performances, Scanlon (2014, pp. 33-35) maintains that the whole vessel represents sport, and Koehl (1986, pp. 108-109), whom Rutter follows, reads it as the illustration of the trials of an initiation cycle for aristocratic males, in which boxing and bull-leaping demonstrated the courage required of the chosen youth. Koehl further proposes that a room with benches for some seventeen people in the villa at Hagia Triada, with animal bones and twenty conical cups, was a collective dining hall for men (pp. 108-109). Crowther (2007, p. 37) leaves open, for the lower register, the possibility that the combatants were professionals, slaves or even mercenaries, and with it that of a paid spectacle in the palace.
>
> Stone rhyta were used only by males and the figures that decorate them are all male (Rutter, 2014, p. 41), so that the vessel speaks of the world of young elite men, and that is the basis on which most studies read it in terms of initiation. What it documents with certainty is that it mattered to record who won.

**`Fuente`**

> Rutter, J. (2014). Sport in the Aegean Bronze Age. In P. Christesen & D. G. Kyle (Eds.), *A companion to sport and spectacle in Greek and Roman antiquity* (pp. 36-52). Wiley Blackwell.
>
> Koehl, R. B. (1986). The Chieftain Cup and a Minoan rite of passage. *The Journal of Hellenic Studies*, *106*, 99-110.

### Diapositiva 14 · Bull-leaping

**`Notas del orador`**

> The corpus is Rutter's (2014, p. 39), some one hundred decorated objects or surfaces, the great majority with bull-leaping. The description in four phases, with approach, grasping of the horns, thrust over the back and landing on the feet, is Evans's, as reported by McInerney (2011, p. 9). The political thesis is also Rutter's (p. 40), for whom leaping seems to have been celebrated only at Knossos during the Neopalatial period and may have become an emblem of its power. Kyle (2015, p. 40) agrees that almost all the evidence comes from Knossos or refers back to it, and Soar (2014, p. 236) proposes that leaping was a performance reserved for the dominant faction and practised only in the largest palace of the island.
>
> Shapland (2022, p. 24) maintains that bull-leaping and the hunting of cattle appear together from the beginning of the Neopalatial period, and interprets them as indicators of the expansion of Knossos's dominion over the rest of the island, since cattle were gathered at Knossos or its outposts to be counted and the games with the bull gave the young men of the ruling group the opportunity to display their physical skill. For Shapland, practices with animals, backed by violence, were a means of integrating the different parts of Crete into the palatial group of Knossos (p. 28). McInerney (2011, p. 12) adds that Knossos, by monopolising the animals and the specialised personnel, asserted its cultural dominance over the island.
>
> The evidence of the rings is McInerney's (2011, p. 13), who counts fifty-three impressions from ten rings of Knossos reproduced at different Cretan sites and even on Thera, six of them with bull-leaping. Weingarten (2010) specifies that at Zakros the principal official responsible for sealings used a cult ring that forms a pair with another showing bull-leaping, both replicas of palatial rings, and cautions, nevertheless, that the dominance of the workshops of Knossos over the rings does not mean that Knossos politically controlled a large part of the island. Shapland (2013, pp. 202-203) adds that these gold rings served for the self-identification of their wearers within the elite.
>
> The crowds in the miniature frescoes indicate that spectacle may have served to bind Cretan society together as Knossos imposed its hegemony, although Shapland (2013, p. 203) warns that these gatherings of people are not securely linked to bull-leaping. Shapland (2013, pp. 196-199) likewise criticises the religious reading imposed by Evans, that of the bull as an offering to a great goddess, because it stems from nineteenth-century assumptions and lacks firm support in the evidence, and stresses the bias of the images, with hundreds of bull-leaps against a single scene of milking and none of ploughing, although the bones of cattle prove that ploughing took place (p. 203).
>
> Potter (2012, p. 18) carries the argument to kingship, since the bull is represented only at Knossos, and Rutter (2014, p. 41) prefers to speak of the exaltation of elite youth. In a society in which the palace is at once temple and seat of power, ritual and politics are not separate. The bull fresco in the image already belongs to the Monopalatial phase, with Knossos under Mycenaean administration, and in it the landscape, the architecture and the spectators disappear (Rutter, 2014, p. 43).

**`Fuente`**

> Shapland, A. (2022). *Human-animal relations in Bronze Age Crete*. Cambridge University Press.
>
> McInerney, J. (2011). Bulls and bull-leaping in the Minoan world. *Expedition*, *53*(3), 6-13.

### Diapositiva 15 · Who leapt and how


**`Notas del orador`**

> The social identity of the leapers comes from Rutter (2014, p. 40) and from Kyle (2015, p. 44), who concludes that the performers of the leap and of the combat were young men of high status, as their dress, jewellery and hairstyles indicate. Koehl (1986, pp. 102-103) observes that the waist-length hair of the leapers in the Knossos fresco is the same as that of the boxer on the Hagia Triada rhyton, and identifies it as the hairstyle of the aristocratic male in the fullness of adult life, devoted to athletics and to the palace festivals. Shapland (2022, p. 24) links these games to the gathering at Knossos of cattle from the whole island. Potter (2012, pp. 19-20) disagrees, since for him they dressed as rich men because they were the favourite entertainers of the elite, and McInerney (2011, p. 12), on the basis of the course landaise of south-west France, where people leap over cows raised on specialised ranches and the leapers are trained in schools, proposes that the Cretans were specialists from Knossos, a thesis of professional acrobats that is today a minority view.
>
> As explained in "The Minoan Woman", Aegean art conventionally painted the skin of women white and that of men red, and by that colour Evans identified in his bull fresco, reconstructed from five panels, two white-skinned girls who assisted a reddish-skinned youth in the leap (Kyle, 2015, p. 40); Shapland (2022, p. 10) reports his conviction that both girls and boys took part in the feats of the bull ring. Newman (2017, p. 229) counts sixteen leapers preserved in the paintings of Knossos, nine white and seven red, so that the white ones are in the majority. Three readings compete today over their identity. The first sees in the colour a mark of task or of age among males, and after studying the frescoes of Avaris Nanno Marinatos concluded that yellow, reddish and white reflected the status and function of each leaper, and that by their size, musculature and dress the white figures of Knossos represent males (Kyle, 2015, p. 43); Rutter (2014, p. 39) also defends the view that all the leapers were male, and Shapland (2013, p. 196) considers it more likely that the colour indicates age or status. The second maintains the female presence, and Younger (2021, p. 58) argues that Aegean women leapt bulls, drove chariots and hunted boar with dogs, relying on the frescoes of Tiryns with legs and hands painted white. The third is Newman's (2017, pp. 229-232), who observes marked musculature, a feature that at Knossos accompanies males, in several white figures, objects to Marinatos that there are also white acrobats who complete the leap, and proposes distinguishing between adult or adolescent women and figures that combine female skin with male muscle, an alternative gender identity perhaps limited to the rite of the leap itself.
>
> The evidence of the breast is under discussion. Potter (2012, pp. 15 & 417) states that, when Evans's artist restored one of the most important leaping frescoes, he arranged the arms of one of the white figures so as to reveal a breast, that this breast was a creation of the restorer Gilliéron, and that the reconstruction by N. Marinatos and C. Palyvou has removed it. Newman (2017, pp. 213, 215 & 229-232), following Chapin, describes instead on the white figure at the left of the panel preserved in the Herakleion Museum, W2 in his catalogue, the remains of a curved breast with a red nipple, which he documents with an unpublished photograph by Anne Chapin, and considers her an adult woman because she is the tallest figure on the panel.
>
> The study by Bietak and his collaborators (2007) of thousands of fragments from Avaris concludes that those bull games included several bulls, acrobats and techniques, and age groups ranging from the adolescent to the young man; the tallest acrobats wrestled with the bull and the smallest leapt (cited in Kyle, 2015, p. 43). Kyle adds that accidents and injuries are depicted at Avaris and at Knossos, and sums up that the bull-leap of Knossos was prestigious, dangerous and spectacular.
>
> The three schemes are from Younger (2021, pp. 55-56). In Evans's scheme the leaper takes the horns from the front and turns in the air to land on the back, and it dominates before c. 1400 BC; in the diving leap the athlete launches himself from a raised position onto the animal's neck; and that of the floating leaper, suspended over the back and holding on to one horn, is exclusively Mycenaean. There is material evidence for the first two, such as the stepped platform at the north-west corner of the central court at Phaistos (p. 56). Shapland (2013, p. 201) stresses that Minoan leapers grasp the animal's horns, unlike the modern bullfight or rodeo, in which the aim is to dodge them. Kyle (2015, p. 41) recalls that asking whether the bulls were drugged or trained leads to a dead end, and that the useful question is what these games were organised for.

### Diapositiva 16 · The diving leap


**`Notas del orador`**

> The scheme of the diving leaper (Younger, 1995, pp. 510-511). The athlete would approach the bull from a raised position, launch himself onto its neck, land with his hands on the animal's shoulders and perform a somersault to land on his feet behind it (p. 510). The toreador fresco from Knossos suggests that he climbed onto the shoulders of an assistant standing in front in order to gain height (p. 510), although the raised support is not attested without ambiguity. It is the dominant scheme, with some thirty examples from Late Minoan IB or Late Helladic IIA to Late Minoan IIIA, and in Mycenaean figured pottery down to IIIB; Younger deduces that it gained popularity as Evans's scheme declined (p. 511). The few images that fit Evans's scheme all date from the beginning of the Late Bronze Age (p. 510). The third scheme, that of the floating leaper, comprises some twenty representations, with the Tiryns fresco as the best example, and Younger reads it as another manoeuvre, the hurdle jump over the back (p. 511). Evans reconstructed his leap from the bronze group attributed to Rethymno in the upper image, whose authenticity Younger leaves open (p. 510); the reconstruction is in Evans (1930, pp. 203-232), a work that is not in the library and whose pages are taken from note 1 of Younger (1995). ### Diapositiva 17 · The bull, from capture to leap

**`Notas del orador`**

> The complete sequence is Younger's (2021, p. 55): capture of the wild bull, leaping and somersaulting, harassment with darts, which is recorded on seal CMS V 497, sacrifice and consumption at a banquet. Leaping is the part that the iconography privileges and almost the only one that has reached the textbooks. Younger also proposes that the bulls represented were aurochs, a species extinct in 1627, and bones have been recovered in Minoan contexts. Shapland (2013, p. 204) qualifies this by noting that the bulls of the Knossos frescoes have a piebald coat that occurs only in domesticated animals, as against the black coat of the aurochs, so that they were not necessarily as fierce as is usually supposed, and recalls that the bull was the largest and most dangerous animal on the island, since the large native herbivores had become extinct in the Neolithic (pp. 198-199).
>
> On the death of the animal there is no agreement. Younger considers it probable as the climax of the cycle, although he himself admitted in 1995 that there is no proof that sacrifice followed the leap (Shapland, 2013, p. 197), and Shapland (2022, pp. 24-25) observes that representations of cattle sacrifice are later than those of leaping and that what palatial art repeats are the horns, the hides and the meat. García Romero (2015b, pp. 38-39) reports Rodríguez Adrados's warning that there is no record of the game being followed by sacrifice. McInerney (2011, p. 9) reports, finally, the extreme position of MacGillivray, for whom nobody ever leapt over a bull and the scenes represent the constellation Taurus, and rejects it because the variety of the scenes, with assistants holding the horns, two animals at once or a bull on an altar, can be explained only if they refer to real performances (p. 10).
>
> At Alalakh, present-day Tell Atchana, in southern Turkey, the palace of Level VII, of the eighteenth to seventeenth centuries BC, yielded seal impressions in which a bull carries on its back two naked long-haired men performing a handstand, in a local style with musculature of Minoan influence (Yener, 2009, p. 49). Kyle (2015, pp. 43-44) dates the seal to c. 1750 BC and, together with other Syrian seals and the Mesopotamian iconography of men facing bulls, suggests that Syrian bull-leaping preceded the Cretan. At the same site, in 2006 and 2007, fragments of a Mycenaean krater of the fourteenth century BC, probably imported from the Argolid, were found, with a spotted bull at the gallop and a leaper in the air (Yener, 2009, pp. 48-49), and Yener concludes that these scenes reflect religious practices shared by Hittite Anatolia, the Aegean and the eastern Mediterranean, and that the vessels that bore them circulated as luxury objects that reinforced the status and alliances of the elites (pp. 49-50). Evans had already proposed an Anatolian origin, and a Hittite vessel found at Hüseyindede shows bull-leaping in a ceremony with acrobats, dancers and musicians, which Kyle (2015, p. 44) dates to c. 1500 BC and Yener (2009, p. 49) to c. 1600 BC. Scanlon (2014, pp. 56-59) joins the hypothesis of a Near Eastern origin.
>
> The frescoes of Avaris, present-day Tell el-Dab'a, are now dated to the Eighteenth Dynasty, around the reign of Thutmose III, and their technique, foreign to the Egyptian, and their style point to several artists who came from Knossos or Thera and reproduced Cretan palatial frescoes (Kyle, 2015, p. 43). McInerney (2011, p. 13) places them in the sixteenth century BC and recalls that leaping also appears on Canaanite seals. Bietak considers it improbable that leaping was practised at Avaris and thinks of a royal marriage with a Minoan princess or of a high-level diplomatic encounter. Kyle (2015, p. 44) concludes that bull games perhaps came to Crete from outside and did not originate there. The bull is captured, displayed and perhaps sacrificed and eaten, and each of these phases gathers the community around the palace.

### Diapositiva 18 · What did bull-leaping mean?

**`Notas del orador`**

> Ritual reading: Evans saw in leaping an offering to a Minoan goddess; Shapland (2013, pp. 196-199) maintains that this reading owes more to nineteenth-century assumptions than to the evidence. McInerney (2011, pp. 6-8) assembles the religious context of the bull (horns of consecration, rhyta, sacrifice). Weak point: Younger (1995, p. 518) admits that there is no evidence that sacrifice followed the leap (cited in Shapland, 2013, p. 197).
>
> Initiatory reading: Koehl (1986, p. 100) relates the hairstyles to age groups and suggests that the trials on the boxers' rhyton, bull-leaping included, formed part of an aristocratic initiation cycle. Scanlon (2014, pp. 28-59) links the bull games with cults and initiation.
>
> Reading as power: Shapland (2022, p. 24) interprets leaping and cattle hunting as indicators of the expansion of Knossos's dominion; in 2013 (p. 204) he defines it as part of the ‘self-definition of an elite’. Soar (2014) observes that the representations appear only at Knossos or on objects from its workshops and proposes that it was a performance reserved for the dominant faction.
>
> Athletic reading: Younger (1995, p. 508) arranges the images in a cycle of activities with bulls, from capture to leap. McInerney (2011, p. 10) maintains that the variety of scenes can be explained only if they represent real performances and cites as a parallel the French course landaise.

### Diapositiva 19 · Spaces for games and sport

**`Notas del orador`**

> The measurements are Younger's (2021, p. 48). The central court measures between a quarter and a third of a stadion; at Malia it is surrounded by columns and pillars with sockets for wooden barriers, and one may imagine the public behind them and on the balconies that the colonnade supported. The theatral areas lie to the north-west of the palaces, with nine wide steps at Phaistos, and the Royal Road of Knossos, paved and a stadion in length, preserves at its end a wall capable of supporting seating, which is why Younger proposes that it functioned as a racecourse.
>
> The western courts fulfilled another function. Aamodt (2014, p. 51) locates in them the dances that accompanied the palatial festivals, since the causeways painted in the frescoes were a real feature of these courts at Knossos, Phaistos and Malia, and recalls that Phaistos has steps for standing spectators on two sides of the court and Knossos on the northern extension of the western court, the so-called theatral area. At Knossos there were also found, some 350 metres to the west of the palace, three circular structures with a smoothed surface, of c. 1400-1350 BC, interpreted as places for dance (p. 52).
>
> The location of the leap is an open debate. The palatial thesis starts from Evans and has some indications in its favour, such as the platform in the north-west corner of the court of Phaistos; the objections are the flagstone pavement, unsuitable for the animal and the leaper, and the difficulty of driving a bull through narrow corridors. Lupack (2010) reports Younger's argument on the paving and that of Marinatos, who finds it absurd to celebrate in a court of limited capacity a festival that the whole community wanted to see, and proposes that the final sacrifice of the bull took place in the central court. Shaw and Panagiotopoulos place the spectacle outside the palace, Rutter (2014, pp. 39-40) joins that position and Kyle (2015, pp. 41 & 43) considers more probable an unpaved space, with temporary structures, next to the palace. McInerney (2011, p. 12), by contrast, takes it as certain that leaping also took place in the great courts of the other palaces, although only Knossos represented it frequently. The only complete bronze group of a Neopalatial bull-leap probably comes from an open-air sanctuary near the Preveli gorge, in the south of the island (Shapland, 2013, pp. 200 & 202). These spaces prove that there was an audience and that this audience gathered at the heart of power.

### Diapositiva 22 · Mycenae and the Dark Age

**`Notas del orador`**

> MYCENAE.
>
> Context again explains sport. A warlike and hierarchical society, with a king at its apex, turns athletic combat into entertainment for the lord and the chariot into a display of rank. Georganas (2010) calls the classic contrast between peaceful Minoans and warlike Mycenaeans too simple and naive an error, although he admits that the Mycenaeans invested far more in their military apparatus.
>
> Potter (2012, pp. 7-10) describes kingdoms ruled from palaces by kings called wanaktes, who kept their records in an early form of Greek, with fortified palaces at Mycenae, Tiryns, Thebes, Orchomenos and Gla and one without walls at Pylos, and with a lawagetas, ‘the one who gathers the people’, and a palatial bureaucracy alongside the wanax. They called themselves Achaeans and Danaans, and the Hittite archives probably knew them as Ahhiyawa (Kyle, 2015, p. 46). Ventris deciphered Linear B as Greek in 1952 (Potter, 2012, p. 9).
>
> Linear B was a purely administrative script and was never used to record sporting practices (Perry, 2014, p. 53). Younger (2021, p. 49) observes, however, that the tablets concern trained women (KN Ak 781 and 783), tripods (PY Ta 641) and oxen for fattening (PY Cn 418), that is, the prestige goods accumulated by the palatial elite.
>
> The horse and the chariot are the best indicator of the link between power and physical practice. Recht and Morris (2021, fig. 1) bring together almost two hundred kraters decorated with chariots, produced on the mainland and exported to Cyprus and the Levant, and maintain that the ownership of horses and participation in public processions were reserved for a few well-off people, while daily dealing with the animals was the full-time occupation of specialised personnel in the service of the elite or the palace, given the time required by the joint training of horse and human (figs. 29-30). Skill with the whip or the goad in the races demanded a very high level, which in turn conferred a certain status on whoever mastered it, or else only people who already had it were allowed to train (fig. 30).
>
> The thesis of the salaried boxers is Rutter's (2014, p. 45), with Benzi, and is taken up by Kyle (2015, pp. 48-49), who speaks of humble or servile performers in royal entertainments. Recht and Morris (2021, fig. 25) propose a complementary reading, since pairs of ‘boxers’ appear on several kraters directly beside the chariots, and on the most famous of the Tanagra larnakes beneath a row of figures in attitudes of mourning, so that these images, together with the funerary function of the vessel, would illustrate mortuary rituals with funeral games in a Mycenaean context. The kraters with boxers appeared in tombs in Cyprus, where the banquet service formed part of the funerary ritual (fig. 25), which brings the two readings closer.
>
> The Tanagra larnax, from tomb 22 and of the first half of the thirteenth century BC, is the coffin of a child or a young person, with two chariots facing each other on one side and bull-leapers on the other (Kyle, 2015, p. 47). Decker read it as an illustration of Bronze Age funeral games, and Rutter (2014, p. 46) rejects this, since the chariots face one another head-on and there is no race, and he maintains that there is no proof of funeral games in the Bronze Age outside epic. Recht and Morris (2021, fig. 25) incline, by contrast, towards the funerary reading on the basis of the mourning figures and the boxers, so that the student can see here an open debate over a single piece.
>
> Rutter (2014, pp. 44-45) proposes that the bull images were for exclusively royal consumption and that those of the mainland are borrowings of Minoan symbols of authority (Kyle, 2015, p. 42). Chapin (2010, fig. 308) recalls that bull-leaping, which we saw in Crete, appears on a fragment from Pylos and in a fresco from Mycenae, and that many specialists doubt that the Mycenaeans ever practised it. By contrast, the boar-hunt fresco of Tiryns represents a favourite sport of the Mycenaean elite, with women driving chariots, huntsmen with dogs and spearmen who finish off the quarry, as a proclamation of the ruling class's capacity to protect its land (Chapin, 2010, fig. 309). Hunting from the chariot, with archers who shoot at the gallop, appears on a gold ring from Mycenae and on an ivory box from Enkomi (Recht & Morris, 2021, figs. 25-26).
>
> Chariot races appear in Aegean art in the twelfth century BC, with a secure example at Tiryns (Rutter, 2014, p. 47), where the succession of chariots and the extended gallop of the horses distinguish them from the slow pace of the procession scenes (Recht & Morris, 2021, fig. 25). Kyle (2015, p. 47) considers them the only probable sport of post-palatial Greece, practised by the local chiefs, the basileis, to demonstrate their status.
>
> The passage from Crete to Mycenae is the first documented change of function in the topic, since, although the chariot and the hunt continue to distinguish the lord and his circle, boxing ceases to be the distinction of the one who competes and becomes the spectacle of the one who pays.
>
> Cline (2015) explains the collapse of the palaces as a ‘perfect storm’ of earthquakes, drought, displaced peoples, revolts and attacks by the so-called ‘Sea Peoples’.
>
> COLLAPSE AND THE DARK AGE.
>
> Without palaces or kings, sport now depends on the warrior chief and on his death, and for that reason almost all the Iron Age competitions that are known are funerary. Christesen (2014, p. 213) sums it up for the end of the period, since c. 700 BC sport was open above all to the males of a few rich and powerful families, Olympia was the only contest with a fixed calendar and organised competitions were held mainly as funeral games for prominent men, intermittently and without purpose-built spaces either in the sanctuaries or in the cities.
>
> Miller (2004a, introduction) dates the Dark Age to c. 1100-800 BC, with a depopulation and a cultural depression visible in the archaeological record, and recalls that myth associated it with the ‘Return of the Heraclids’, that is, with the Dorian invasion, which the scholarship now treats as legendary tradition. Christesen (2012b, p. 121) describes the end of the Bronze Age as a spectacular series of destructions that finished off most of the great settlements and contributed to an almost total social collapse. Potter (2012, pp. 9-10) dates the fires between 1200 and 1150 BC and the end of the attempts at reoccupation to c. 1070 BC.
>
> The village society is Christesen's (2012b, p. 124), for whom a population of a thousand people was an unusually large place, with almost no formal political system, and the influential men of each community were called basileis, an honorific title that did not derive from any election. Potter (2012, p. 24) adds that the basileus was far from being a monarchical wanax like those of the Mycenaean palaces we have just seen.
>
> At Lefkandi, beneath a tumulus, a building that came to measure 150 feet held two burials, one of them of a powerful woman interred with rich grave goods (Potter, 2012, p. 5), and it is dated to c. 950 BC (p. 10). The cremation of the male recalls the funeral of Patroclus in the *Iliad*, and for that reason Potter uses the find to link the world of Homer with archaeology.
>
> The alphabet is taken from the Phoenicians and adapted to Greek (Antonaccio, 2014, p. 200). The Dipylon oinochoe is dated to c. 740-725 BC and its hexameter inscription identifies it as a prize for dancing (Perry, 2014, pp. 57-58); Kyle (2015, p. 77) dates it to c. 750-725 BC and speaks of a dance or leaping contest. That the earliest known Greek text should be a prize for a bodily skill says much about the place that competition already occupied in that society.

### Diapositiva 23 · The funeral games of Patroclus

**`Notas del orador`**

> The episode is read from the previous slide. The funeral of the chief is the occasion, the princes are the competitors and wealth is the prize, so that sport again reflects the structure of power. Brown (2003, pp. 123-124 & 130) also reads the games as the outlet that the poem gives to the crisis of social value opened in Book 1, when force shatters trust in the distribution of the spoils, since the sporting prize, awarded in public before all, replaces that distribution as a way of recognising a warrior's merit.
>
> The episode occupies lines 23.257-897 (Perry, 2014, p. 59). Achilles brings out from the ships, ‘as prizes for those who should win in the games, cauldrons, tripods, horses, mules, strong-headed oxen, fair-girdled women, and gleaming iron’ (*Iliad* 23.259-261, trans. Segalá). The eight events are those of Christesen (2012b, p. 126) and Potter (2012, p. 13).
>
> The prizes, event by event, according to Kyle (2015, pp. 57-62). The chariot race has the longest account, the richest prizes and the most competitors, Eumelus, Diomedes, Menelaus, Meriones and Antilochus. Boxing is rewarded with a mule and a cup, and is won by Epeios against Euryalus. In the wrestling, between Odysseus and Ajax son of Telamon, a tripod worth twelve oxen and a skilled woman worth four, and Achilles declares a draw. The foot race is rewarded with a silver bowl, an ox and half a talent of gold, and in the weight throw the block of iron is at once the implement and the only prize. Almost all the participants receive something, a gesture that Kyle (p. 56) reads as generosity on the part of Achilles and a desire for reconciliation. All the competitors are princes except Epeios (Kyle, 2015, p. 57; Perry, 2014, p. 60).
>
> The chariot race is the contest that Verde (2011) analyses in detail. Achilles displays the prizes in honour of the superiority of his own horses, and the race holds the place of pride by its initial position and by the length that the poet devotes to it (p. 13), with a catalogue of contenders that is repeated in the summons, in the drawing of the starting positions and at the finish (p. 14). Nestor warns Antilochus that whoever trusts only in the strength of his horses wanders aimlessly, whereas the driver who knows tactics wins even at a material disadvantage (pp. 18-19). Apollo snatches the whip from Diomedes and Athena returns it to him and breaks the yoke of Eumelus's chariot (pp. 26-27). Menelaus accuses Antilochus of cheating and demands that he swear before the gods, and when Antilochus refuses to swear falsely he yields him the prize in recognition of the merits of Nestor's family in the war (pp. 39-40), while Eumelus receives a greater prize outside the competition (p. 40, n. 46). Verde concludes that the episode interests Homer because it is competitive, plural and prone to accident, and for that reason it serves him to set out his view of human existence (p. 42).
>
> The dating is the central question. The poems reached their basic form between 725 and 675 BC (Perry, 2014, p. 53) and close an oral tradition of centuries that mixes details of different periods (p. 58). Kyle (2015, p. 54) concludes that Homer reflects customs of the ninth and eighth centuries BC, long after Mycenaean reality; the block of iron is an anachronism (p. 62), and bronze tripods and cauldrons as prizes are post-Mycenaean (p. 47). Potter (2012, p. 11) adds that the spectacular cremation of Patroclus does not go back to the palatial period.
>
> The *Odyssey* offers the counterpoint. The Phaeacians, the people who receive Odysseus on his return, compete in the agora without prizes and without a chariot race (Kyle, 2015, p. 63; Perry, 2014, pp. 59-60), and Euryalus utters Homer's only mention of ‘athlete’ (8.164) when he tells Odysseus that he does not look like one (Kyle, 2015, p. 64). Odysseus replies with a heavier discus thrown further than those of the Phaeacians (Perry, 2014, p. 63).
>
> The jar from Tiryns, of the twelfth century BC, may show a tripod or a krater as a prize, and in that case it would be the oldest athletic prize in Greek art (Rutter, 2014, p. 48, n. 8); Recht and Morris (2021, fig. 25) consider it very probable that the Tiryns vases represent a race, on account of the succession of chariots and the extended gallop. Roller (1981) catalogues the images of funeral games in Archaic art. They appear suddenly at the beginning of the sixth century BC (p. 107); the earliest securely identified is the dinos fragment by Sophilos, of c. 580 BC, with a four-horse chariot race before a grandstand of spectators and the inscription that labels it as the games of Patroclus (pp. 108-109), and the François krater, of c. 570 BC, shows five labelled four-horse chariots whose names do not entirely coincide with those of the *Iliad* (p. 109). No pictorial version coincides entirely with the literary account (pp. 108-109 & 115), the author rebuts the idea that every Geometric athletic scene represents epic games (p. 114) and links the rise of these themes to the founding of the Pythian Games in 586 BC, the Isthmian in 581 BC and the Nemean in 573 BC (pp. 117-118).
>
> The games of Patroclus are the first text in which sport is at once funerary rite, aristocratic distinction and distribution of wealth, and the dispute between Antilochus and Menelaus shows that this distribution already demanded rules and public arbitration (Verde, 2011, pp. 39-40).

**`Fuente`**

> Verde Castro, C. V. (2011). Los juegos funerales en honor de Patroclo (*Ilíada*, XXIII.257 ss.). *Synthesis*, *18*, 13-43.
>
> Roller, L. E. (1981). Funeral games in Greek art. *American Journal of Archaeology*, *85*(2), 107-119.

### Diapositiva 28 · Historical introduction to Greece

**`Notas del orador`**

> The parallel with Crete helps the student to see the continuity, since again there appears a fragmented geography and a mosaic of communities, now without palaces. From this fragmentation derive the common sanctuaries as meeting points, victory as an honour for the city and an Olympia that was at once Panhellenic and the property of a single polis.
>
> Christesen (2012b, p. 138) describes the transition, from the eighth century BC, from the informal arrangements of the Iron Age to political systems structured around the polis, formed by an urban centre and the countryside surrounding it. The basileis exercised power because they attracted followers by their military valour and their generosity, whereas formal government made it possible to command merely by holding a magistracy, and in many places the plousioi sought to control access to these offices in order to consolidate their dominance. The polis therefore changes the system and preserves the elite. Sparta and Athens were only two among literally hundreds of poleis (p. 144), and what united them was language, the gods and the common sanctuaries, with nothing resembling a national sentiment in the modern sense.
>
> Greece never formed a political unity. The Hellenic League assembled against Xerxes won at Plataea in 479 BC and dedicated an offering at Olympia (Nielsen, 2007, p. 44). Valavanis (2021, p. 115) sums up that for the Greeks Panhellenism ‘was more a matter of cultural sharing rather than political unity’, and that the crown games were its principal manifestation. Nielsen (2014, p. 136) further qualifies the political scope of this Panhellenism, since the authorities of Olympia never attempted to extend the sacred truce to the whole Greek world.
>
> Papakonstantinou (2019, p. 1) maintains that Greek sport built, from Homeric Greece to the imperial period, overlapping identities, that of the aristocratic individual who distinguishes himself from his peers, that of the civic community that recognises itself in its victor and the Panhellenic Greekness that defines itself against the barbarian. At Olympia this last frontier had guardians, since the Elean judges, called Hellanodikai, ‘judges of the Greeks’, decided whether an athlete was qualified to compete both by physique and by ethnic origin, and the very name of the office shows the Panhellenic appeal of the sanctuary (p. 73).
>
> Kyle (2015, p. 78) explains that the new city-states used cults, festivals, processions and sanctuaries located in remote places or on the frontiers to unify space and community and to mark their territory. The great Panhellenic festivals were one of the few arenas in which that divided world recognised itself as one.

### Diapositiva 29 · Historical introduction: the periods

**`Notas del orador`**

> The dates of the periods are those of Christesen (2012b, pp. 120-121): Early Iron Age or Dark Age c. 1100-700 BC, Archaic c. 700-480 BC, Classical 480-323 BC and Hellenistic 323-31 BC. Other authors give somewhat different cut-off points, and the differences respond to different criteria. Kyle (2015, p. 54) closes the Early Iron Age in 750 BC, with the end of the Geometric; 480 BC corresponds to the Persian defeat and 500 BC, which other textbooks use, to a round figure; and the Hellenistic period ends in 31 BC, with Actium, although for Greece the decisive date is 146 BC, when it becomes the Roman province of Achaea (Golden, 2004, p. xix) after the sack of Corinth and the defeat of the Achaean League (Scanlon, 2021, p. 668).
>
> Philip II of Macedon defeated the Greek alliance at Chaeronea in 338 BC (Golden, 2008, p. 17), and his hegemony therefore belongs to the end of the Classical period; 323 BC, the death of Alexander, opens the Hellenistic. The founding of Syracuse in 734 BC and the colonisation of southern Italy and Sicily straddling the eighth and seventh centuries BC are from Sinn (2021, p. 71), and by c. 580 BC the great colonisation had already subsided (Scanlon, 2021, p. 656).
>
> Almost everything that is studied as Greek sport took shape in the Archaic period, and most of the testimonies describe the Classical period even when they speak of earlier ones, a bias in the sources that obliges us to read the ancient authors with care. The Hellenistic gymnasium, open in all the Greek cities of the East, was the institution that marked who was Greek (Bertolín, 2020, pp. 134 & 199).

### Diapositiva 30 · Greek society

**`Notas del orador`**

> The box closes with what both authors share, and the body attributes the figure for the number of practitioners to Christesen so as not to take sides.
>
> Ancient demography does not allow reliable percentages for the Greek world as a whole, and the figures that are documented are Athenian. Christesen (2014, p. 221; 2012b, p. 160) calculates some 25,000 adult male citizens in 500 BC out of some 175,000 inhabitants, that is, some 1,000 plousioi and 10,000 penêtes. Pritchard (2013, p. 201), with Hansen's calculations, gives some 60,000 adult citizens in 431 BC and some 30,000 in the second half of the fourth century BC. The only ancient census, that of Demetrius of Phalerum transmitted by Athenaeus (272c), gives 21,000 Athenians, 10,000 metics and 400,000 slaves, the last figure implausible (Christesen, 2007, p. 308).
>
> The threefold division is Christesen's (2014, p. 213; 2012b, p. 136). The plousioi came from households so rich and with so much labour at their disposal that their adult males never had to work; the penêtes, from prosperous households in which the male worked in the fields for part of the year and enjoyed a great deal of leisure in the seasons of little labour; the ptôchoi, from households in which the male worked regularly, from the modest peasant to the beggar. Political struggles were fought between plousioi and penêtes, and the ptôchoi were almost always excluded (2014, p. 214). The plousioi would be between 4 and 5 % of households and the penêtes between 30 and 50 %, so that the dominant group of each city became between six and ten times larger (2014, p. 215; 2012b, p. 141). Pritchard (2003, pp. 301-302) works, by contrast, with the twofold division used by the classical Athenians themselves, between hoi plousioi, the rich, marked by leisure, dress, hunting, horse-riding and liturgies, and hoi penêtes, which included all the other citizens, from the destitute to those who stood just below the elite, and calculates that the rich were somewhat under 5 % of the citizen body. The same term therefore designates different groups in the one author and the other, and their figures must be read accordingly.
>
> The exclusions overlap. Apart from initiation rites and the case of Sparta, Greek women were excluded from sport, and slaves were barred from it by social and legal barriers (Christesen, 2014, p. 219); Golden (2008, p. 42) documents a single exception, the bequest of Fenia Aromatio at Gytheion (IG V.1.1208), which allowed slaves to use the oil six days a year and which, by its very rarity, confirms the rule. The ptôchoi were kept away by lack of free time and by the cost of the paidotribes, the teacher of gymnastics, who charged from 10 to 15 drachmas per pupil per year when the subsistence of a family of four cost half a drachma a day and the panoply 75 to 100 drachmas (Christesen, 2014, p. 220); Pritchard (2003, p. 309) documents, by contrast, that the paidotribes Hippomachos charged a hundred drachmas for his lessons. Nudity also gave away the ‘farmer's tan’ of one who worked in the sun in a short tunic or the pallor of the craftsman (Christesen, 2014, p. 229), and Golden (2008, p. 54) warns that this same nudity, which set the Greeks apart from their neighbours, could conceal differences as much as create them.
>
> The democratisation thesis is formulated systematically in Christesen (2014), as reported by Bromberg (2023, p. 13), and not only in the 2012 book. Christesen maintains that sport, by fostering egalitarianism and unity among the enfranchised members of communities that were democratising, consolidated and extended democratisation (2014, p. 211), by means of four mechanisms: serving as a model of egalitarian relations, promoting meritocratic competition for status, generating social capital and favouring group closure against those excluded (p. 213). His evidence is the increase in contests after the reforms of Cleisthenes, the three public gymnasia of Athens at the end of the sixth century BC, when there were some thousand plousioi (p. 218), and the complaint of the Old Oligarch that the people were paid to run and sing (p. 219). The entry of the penêtes raised Athenian practitioners from some 1,000 to some 11,000 (p. 221), and in the 2012 book he calculates that between 5 and 10 % of the population took part in sport, a deceptively low figure because it covered almost all those who enjoyed full rights, which makes Archaic Greece the first known society with mass sport (Christesen, 2012b, p. 160).
>
> Pritchard argues the opposite for Athens. In his 2003 chapter he takes issue with Young, Fisher and Stephen Miller (pp. 293-299) and maintains that athletics was in Classical Athens a practice exclusive to the elite; Kyle's catalogue, with 78 Athenian athletes and horsemen, offers not a single secure case of non-elite origin, since the 50 who can be identified belong to the upper class and sport seems to follow the social rise of families rather than precede it (p. 324), and on the same page he rejects Fisher's reading, which extended participation to the poorest citizens. In his 2013 book he formulates the paradox that sport was very popular among the non-elite citizens who could not practise it (p. 1), recalls that Golden reached the same conclusion (p. 42) and summarises Christesen's opposing proposal, which would explain the growth of the festivals of that century by the mass participation of the sixth century BC (p. 45). In 2009 he condenses it thus: ‘in the world’s first fully developed democracy athletes continued to be drawn predominantly (and possibly even exclusively) from the city’s upper class’ (Pritchard, 2009, p. 8), and explains the popular admiration for the athlete by the practical and ideological democratisation of war, which legitimised the sport of the upper class (p. 21).
>
> Kyle occupies an intermediate position. He reads the Old Oligarch's complaint as the exaggerated denunciation that facilities formerly reserved for the rich had come to be used by the poor as well (Kyle, 1993, p. 68), shows that the ‘new athletes’ of the second half of the fifth century BC were still rich, although their fortune came from the empire and not from land (p. 116), and recalls that sport did not eliminate the hierarchy of Greek society (Kyle, 2015, p. 85). Bromberg (2023, p. 13) takes up his formula, according to which social change influenced sport more than sport influenced social change.
>
> Sporting practice separates the citizen from the non-citizen and the leisured from the one who works, and in that separation, which Christesen (2014, p. 226) calls group closure, lies the Greek version of the function of distinction.
>
> The equivalence with an upper, middle and lower class is didactic. Christesen's criterion is working time and not income, and in Pritchard's scheme the penêtes cover all citizens who were not rich. Christesen (2012b, p. 139) reports that the penêtes upheld an ideal expressed in the concept of mesos, ‘the one in the middle’.
>
> The Athenian gymnasium (Academy, Lyceum, Kynosarges) was a public space without an entrance fee; the exclusion was legal, not economic: the law forbade slaves to exercise in the palaestrae (Aeschines, *Against Timarchus* 1.138). Many palaestrae, by contrast, were private and were run by a paidotribes who charged his pupils (Plato, *Lysis* 203a-204a). In the Hellenistic period the gymnasiarch paid for the oil out of his own pocket as a service to the city.

### Diapositiva 32 · The politics of the poleis

**`Notas del orador`**

> Athens. In the seventh century BC Athens was governed by a hereditary aristocracy, whose families turned equestrian victory into a badge of lineage. Tyranny followed, the power of a single man without legal backing, and between 561 and 510 BC the tyrants sponsored the games in order to win over the people. In 508 BC democracy was born, the first of which we have any record, in which an assembly of all the citizens took the decisions. Did sport open up as well? No. It still demanded leisure and, as we saw, the payment of a paidotribes, so that athletes continued to come from wealthy families.
>
> Sparta. Sparta solved the problem of leisure through the labour of others. Some 8,000 citizens, the homoioi or "equals", lived off what was produced by the helots, the subjugated population of Messenia, and the law obliged them to exercise even on campaign. Their daughters also trained. Hence sport became an instrument of control. The coward was excluded from wrestling and ball games, and nudity exposed each individual to the judgement of the group.
>
> Athens opened politics to all and left sport to the rich. Sparta closed citizenship to a few and imposed sport on all of them.
>
> In Athens, the aristocracy monopolised equestrian victory, the tyrants sponsored the games, and democracy opened politics to all citizens without opening sport to them, since sport continued to demand leisure and money. In Sparta, the labour of the helots gave every citizen the free time required, and sport was placed at the service of collective discipline. The Eupatridai, Solon, Peisistratos and his sons, Kleosthenes and Pericles are named aloud.
>
> For much of the seventh century BC Athens was dominated by a small hereditary aristocracy, the Eupatridai, "descendants of good fathers", with nine magistrates elected by and from among themselves and a council of some 300 former magistrates (Christesen, 2012b, p. 143; 2014, p. 216). In the seventh and sixth centuries BC the plousioi, the rich who did not need to work, sought to found their power on control of the institutions, on the superiority of their lineage and on conspicuous consumption (Christesen, 2014, p. 214). The Athenian horse-breeding families, such as the Alcmaeonids, the Philaids and the Kalliads, used equestrian victory as a display of wealth and as a politics of elite identity, and archaic funerary monuments represented the deceased as an athlete (Papakonstantinou, 2019, p. 47). Nicholson (2005, pp. 1-4) explains in the same vein the silence of the epinikia and of the monuments regarding paid charioteers, jockeys and trainers, since their remunerated labour belied the aristocratic notion that the qualities needed to win were the patrimony of lineage, and the market for those services made it possible to evade the traditional networks of reciprocity on which aristocratic power rested (p. 3). Kyle (1993, p. 175) shows that personal victory as a direct route to political leadership gave way, towards the beginning of the fourth century BC, to the sponsorship and administration of the games, always driven by philotimia, the desire to have one's own excellence recognised.
>
> The tyrants were usurpers without royal legitimacy who seized control without constitutional ratification, and they were active patrons and reformers of competitions (Kyle, 2015, p. 80). The tyranny of Peisistratos and his sons Hippias and Hipparchos, between 561 and 510 BC, threatened the athletic dominance of the aristocrats (Kyle, 2014c, p. 169). The Great Panathenaia were reorganised as a Panhellenic festival in 566/5 BC, under the archonship of Hippokleides, to rival the great games of Olympia, Delphi, Nemea and the Isthmus (Phillips, 2003, p. 202), and Kylon's attempted coup of c. 630-620 BC, the return of Peisistratos with Phye disguised as Athena and the murder of Hipparchos in 514 BC all coincide with its celebration, which drew large crowds in a religious atmosphere favourable to political manipulation (pp. 203-206). Poliakoff (1987, p. 114) sums up the underlying tension, since nothing was more natural for the ambitious aristocrat, and at the same time more dangerous for the city, than the assertion of individual superiority that led to revolution and tyranny.
>
> Christesen dates the reform of Solon to c. 590 BC and that of Kleosthenes to 508 BC (2014, p. 216); Scanlon (2021, p. 659) specifies the archonship of Solon as 594/593 BC. Kleosthenes handed sovereign power to an assembly that met forty times a year (Christesen, 2014, p. 217), and his system is the oldest known example of democratic government (Christesen, 2012b, p. 144). A law attributed to Solon set 500 drachmas for the Athenian victor at Olympia and 100 for the victor at the Isthmus of Corinth, a figure that Papakonstantinou (2019, p. 70) believes was updated at the end of the sixth century BC and that Golden (2008, p. 41) reads as an attempt by Athens to distinguish itself from other cities. Pritchard (2013, p. 1) formulates the central paradox of Athenian democracy, which opened politics to all citizens but left access to sport untouched, because only the upper class could afford the lessons of the paidotribes, the gymnastics teacher, "because the democracy did not subsidise education". Democracy transformed the social composition of the army and the fleet, but never that of the athletes (p. 192). Pritchard (2009, pp. 18-19) adds that Kleosthenes' registration by demes and tribes was the city's first mechanism of mass mobilisation and that the fleet decided upon in 483/2 BC opened military service to citizens too poor to be hoplites.
>
> The testimony of Aristotle (Politics 1297a) opens Christesen's essay on sport and democratisation (2014, p. 211; 2012b, p. 165). Oligarchs excluded the poor from the assembly, the magistracies, the courts, arms-bearing and sport, and to that end they fined the rich who did not exercise and not the poor.
>
> Sparta covered some 8,500 km², about twenty times a typical polis (Christesen, 2012b, p. 141). After suppressing the Messenian revolt around the middle of the seventh century BC, many penêtes, the prosperous landowners who had to work part of the year, received allotments in Messenia with helots attached and became plousioi, and all male citizens came to vote in the assembly and to call themselves homoioi, although the two hereditary kings and the old wealthy families retained privileges (Christesen, 2014, p. 216). There were probably some 8,000 homoioi in the archaic period (Christesen, 2012b, p. 142), and the new system took shape between c. 650 and 550 BC (p. 151), with paides aged 7 to 14, paidiskoi aged 14 to 20 and hêbôntes aged 20 to 30 (p. 152). Women never had political rights, and a substantial part of the inhabitants was enslaved (pp. 142-143).
>
> Christesen (2012a, p. 193) maintains, with Foucault and Bourdieu, that Spartan sport reinforced above all the social order, and only to a lesser degree individual autonomy, by instilling the subordination of the individual to the group, the role of the soldier and meritocratic competition for status. Xenophon (Lac. 9.3-5) relates that the coward was excluded from the common meals, from wrestling and from ball games and relegated to the worst place in the choruses, proof that sport functioned as a means of social control (p. 194); the sources speak almost exclusively of the Spartiates and their daughters, and we know very little about the perioikoi and the helots (p. 194). The selection of the 300 hippeis, open to all Spartiates, was a competition so intense that the rivals came to blows when they met (p. 234), and compulsory nudity before one's comrades generated constant pressure to look like the ideal soldier (p. 241). Xenophon adds that the law obliged the Spartiates to exercise even on campaign (Christesen, 2014, p. 218).
>
> The hoplite phalanx, from the end of the eighth and the beginning of the seventh century BC, made soldiers equal in the line of battle (Christesen, 2012b, p. 137), and since each soldier paid for his own equipment, it was formed mostly of plousioi and penêtes, while the ptôchoi, those who always had to work, fought as light infantry or, later, as rowers (Christesen, 2014, p. 214). The panoply cost between 75 and 100 drachmas (Christesen, 2014, p. 220). For Poliakoff (1987, p. 114) the phalanx stimulated athletics as a displacement of military impulses, and he rejects the idea that sport served as training for it; Pritchard (2013, p. 208) recalls along the same lines that Athenian hoplites received no military training before the middle of the 330s BC.
>
> Access to sport was, in short, a privilege of citizenship, and the oligarchies reserved it by law for the rich.
>
> Primary sources for the cases in the matrix. The oligarchies' fine on the rich man who did not exercise is in Aristotle, Politics 4.13, 1297a. The victories of the young Aeginetans, with their trainer Melesias, are sung in Pindar, Olympian 8 and Nemean 6; that of Hieron of Syracuse in the horse race of 476 BC, in Olympian 1. For Croton, Strabo 6.1.12 records the saying that the last of the Crotoniates was the first of the other Greeks, and Pausanias 6.14.5-8 narrates the victories of Milo.
>
> Sparta and the Games: the lists of Olympic victors show the Spartans among the most successful of the seventh century and the first half of the sixth century BC; thereafter their gymnastic victories become scarce and the best known are equestrian, won by wealthy citizens who owned horses (Christesen, 2007; check pages in the reading card). Milo of Croton, six times Olympic victor in wrestling according to Pausanias 6.14.5-8, is left to be told aloud.

### Diapositiva 37 · Origins of the Olympic Games

**`Notas del orador`**

> The useful division separates what the Greeks told from what archaeology and source criticism allow us to reconstruct, and Iphitos, the pact of the three cities and the year 884 BC belong to the first plane, since they come from authors writing between the third century BC and the second century AD, as do the accounts of Heracles or Pelops (Kyle, 2015, p. 98). Christesen (2007, p. 20) concludes that all the dates in the Greek sources on the early history of Olympia rest on weak foundations and cannot be taken as reliable.
>
> The scepticism is ancient. Strabo (8.3.30) states that the Olympic Games are an invention of the Eleans and that "the ancient legends about the creation of the sanctuary and the institution of the games must indeed be discarded", since "such stories circulate in various versions and deserve no credit" (trans. 2008, p. 88). Murray (2014, p. 311) cites the same passage and reads the foundation accounts as later inventions, intended to legitimise the social and political claims of those who told them.
>
> To tell who had founded the Games was a way of saying to whom Olympia belonged, and it is the first time in the topic that sport functions as an argument of legitimacy.
>
> The cult of Olympia arose at the end of the eleventh century BC, in the midst of the Dark Age (Sinn, 2021, p. 65; Kyrieleis, 2003, p. 55), whereas the great expansion of the sanctuary and the first stadium, c. 700 BC, coincide with the beginning of the archaic period and with a sharp increase in visitors (Sinn, 2021, pp. 67-68), and the chariot race, added in 680 BC, attracted the aristocracy (Kyle, 2015, p. 87). Elis, the city that controlled the sanctuary, administered the Games as though they belonged to it entirely, and Olympia was decisive for its civic identity and its political organisation (Crowther, 2003, p. 61).

### Diapositiva 38 · The foundation stories

**`Notas del orador`**

> The series comes from Pausanias (5.7.6-8.5), who was writing c. AD 170 (Miller, 2004b, no. 86-87), and Kyle (2015, p. 98) describes it as a complete and synthetic account, though a bewildering one, built from what the priests of Elis told him. Herakles the Idaean, one of the Dactyls or Kouretes, made his brothers race, crowned the victor, founded the games and introduced the wild olive; from him, moreover, derived the periodicity of the festival, celebrated every five years in the Greek inclusive reckoning because there were five Dactyls. There follow Clymenos, who held games fifty years after the flood; Endymion, son of Aethlios, who deposed Clymenos and set his sons to race at Olympia for the kingdom; Pelops, a generation later, with the most splendid games held until then; Pelias and Neleus, Augeias, Herakles of Thebes, with games in which heroes competed, and finally Oxylos, after whom comes the interruption that Iphitos would end.
>
> Herakles the Idaean is a different figure from Herakles of Thebes, son of Amphitryon, who appears much later in the series, and textbooks often confuse them. The translator's note to the passage of Strabo (8.3.30) recalls that already in Antiquity there was no agreement as to which of the two was the founder (trans. 2008, p. 88, n. 249). Likewise, the one who sets his sons to race for the kingdom is Endymion, and not his father Aethlios.
>
> The function of the chain is the key to its reading. Sinn (2021, p. 68) explains that scholars agree in seeing in this range of cult legends the expression of the conflicts over control of the sanctuary, which lasted until the fifth century BC, and that each contender sought to support his claim by a presence there from the very beginning. Kyle (2015, p. 99) adds that the rival claims of Elis and Pisa produced transparently invented founders, Eleios for Elis and Pisos for Pisa, and that Elis favoured the traditions of Herakles.
>
> The Boxer at Rest accompanies the plate and does not illustrate any founder; the available card places it in the Palazzo Massimo in Rome, with a height of 1.28 m (Christesen & Stocking, 2021, fig. 8.22). The chain of Pausanias shows sport as prestige capital, since to possess the founder of the Games was equivalent to possessing the sanctuary.

### Diapositiva 39 · The sacred truce

**`Notas del orador`**

> Iphitos, the year 884 BC and the pact between Elis, Sparta and Pisa come from much later sources. Pausanias (5.4.5-6) presents Iphitos as a descendant of Oxylos and a contemporary of Lycurgus, and relates that, with Greece exhausted by civil war and plague, he consulted Delphi and re-established the festival and the truce (Miller, 2004b, no. 87). Miller notes that the dates of Iphitos and of Lycurgus are not agreed today, nor were they in Antiquity. Phlegon of Tralles, a freedman of Hadrian c. AD 138, drawing on Pisan sources, speaks of a reorganisation c. 884 BC by three leaders, Lycurgus of Sparta, Iphitos of Elis and Kleosthenes of Pisa, whom the oracle ordered to restore the games, declare a truce and inscribe it on the discus of Iphitos, which Pausanias (5.20.1) says he saw (Kyle, 2015, p. 98). Kyle observes that Phlegon, writing in the midst of the Roman peace, attributes to the first festival a purpose of peace and harmony. The year 884 BC is the date that Eratosthenes and Apollodorus gave to the games of Lycurgus and Iphitos (Christesen, 2007, p. 20).
>
> In the text of Phlegon, what is inscribed after the truce is announced is a discus for the Hellanodikai, "judges of the Greeks", in accordance with which they were to conduct the Games (Tyrrell, 2004, pp. 42-43). The account thus attributes to Iphitos the origin of the judges who directed the competition, which reinforces the claim of Elis to have been in charge of the sanctuary from the beginning.
>
> The truce, by contrast, is well documented in the classical period. Nielsen (2023, p. 76) describes the complete system: the organising city sent delegations, theoroi or spondophoroi, to announce the festival (epangelia) and to proclaim the ekecheiria, which protected the precinct during the celebration, and the receiving cities appointed a theorodokos, "receiver of theoroi", chosen from the local elite. For Olympia, the epangelia is attested at Akragas at the beginning of the fifth century BC (Pindar, Isthmian 2.23-24), the theorodokia in the middle of the same century and the truce in an inscription of 460-450 BC (SEG 43.630.7). Nielsen (2023, p. 77) adds that the same system, although it seems to have been conceived for the great games, is documented at festivals of small Peloponnesian cities such as Makiston, Mantineia and Phlius, so that the Olympic truce belonged to a religious institution shared by many sanctuaries.
>
> Its scope was limited. The ekecheiria forbade armies from entering Elean territory and ordered the safe passage, through any state, of travellers going to or returning from the festival, treated in practice as pilgrims, and it fell far short of a general or common peace, eirene (Kyle, 2015, p. 112). It lasted at first one month and later three, was enforced only by the authority of Zeus, religious sanctions, fines and exclusions, and could not guarantee peace or even safe passage (p. 124). Golden (2008, p. 137) observes that such a guarantee of passage would have been unnecessary had all hostilities been suspended, and Nielsen (2014, p. 136) specifies that the authorities of Olympia never attempted to extend it to the whole Greek world. Polybius (4.73.6-10) explains that the Greeks granted the Eleans an Elis that was "sacred and inviolable" on account of the Games, and that this was the source of their inexperience in war (Miller, 2004b, no. 85).
>
> War coexisted with the festival. In 480 BC the Games were held while Xerxes was invading Greece, and they continued to be convened throughout the Peloponnesian War (Kyle, 2015, p. 124). The Athenian Phrynon was captured on his way to Olympia by soldiers of Philip II during the sacred month, and the king apologised, alleging that his men were unaware of the rule (Dillon, 2021, p. 571). In 420 BC the Eleans excluded Sparta from the Games for having broken the truce by attacking their territory, and the dispute was settled by a tribunal composed of Eleans, that is, of the interested party (Crowther, 2003, p. 62); in 364 BC the Eleans even fought within the sacred precinct, during the pentathlon, to expel the Arcadians who were then presiding over the Games (Crowther, 2003, p. 62; Golden, 2008, p. 137). Kyrieleis (2003, p. 49) provides the material evidence, since weapons captured from other Greeks were displayed as trophies, tropaia, on the banks of the stadium before tens of thousands of spectators, and he concludes that the idea that dominated ancient Olympia was victory, in sport as in war.
>
> The idealisation of the truce is at once ancient and modern. Bromberg (2023, p. 1) documents that the accounts of the pacifying origin of the Games come from orators of the fifth and fourth centuries BC, Gorgias, Lysias and Isocrates, written in the context of the Peloponnesian War, and he cites the Panegyricus (43) of Isocrates, for whom the Greeks came to the festival after signing treaties and resolving their hostilities, and there recalled the kinship that united them (p. 2). Pierre de Coubertin, founder of the modern Games, defended peace through sport as a means by which nations might cease to misunderstand one another (p. 5), and UN resolution 48/11, of 25 October 1993, recognises the effort of the International Olympic Committee to restore the ekecheiria (p. 2). Against that image, ancient and modern historians agree that not even a millennium of truces was enough to halt war between Greek cities (p. 3). Kyle (2015, pp. 99 and 112) attributes to later Panhellenic ideology this projection into the past of a peace that never existed as such.
>
> The vaulted entrance in the image is of the Hellenistic period and measures 32.10 m (Romano, 2021, p. 395), and therefore bears no relation to Iphitos. The truce is a case of constructed collective identity, since the rite protected the journey to the sanctuary and later rhetoric, ancient and modern, turned it into a symbol of concord among Greeks.

### Diapositiva 41 · What history and archaeology tell us

**`Notas del orador`**

> The date of 704 BC is the proposal of the archaeologist Alfred Mallwitz, whose name is left for the oral explanation.
>
> The sequence of the site is from Sinn (2021, pp. 65-66), with the recent excavations of Kyrieleis and Rambach. The first signs of cult are Submycenaean high-footed cups and vessels of the early Iron Age, characteristic of ritual meals, dated to the end of the eleventh century BC and found next to a mound that for years was held to be Mycenaean. It is now known that the mound was raised in the middle of the third millennium BC on a settlement abandoned at the beginning of the second, and that for almost a millennium there was no human presence at the place. There is no archaeological proof of continuity from the Mycenaean period. The "black layer" of ash, bones and offerings surrounding the mound corresponds to the oldest altar of Zeus, from the end of the eleventh century BC. Kyle (2015, pp. 101-102) reports the same conclusion of Mallwitz and Kyrieleis and adds that the mound was only later associated with Pelops, perhaps in the seventh century BC.
>
> Kyrieleis (2003), who has directed the German excavations at Olympia since 1985, summarises the research on the origins of the sanctuary. The Geometric figurines of the ninth and eighth centuries BC appeared by the thousand in the nineteenth-century excavations, and it was Furtwängler who recognised them as the first examples of Greek sculpture (p. 46). Their quantity and quality indicate that the sanctuary already had interregional importance at that time, although they came from later strata and outside their original context (p. 52). The re-excavation of the Pelopion, the sanctuary of the hero Pelops, with the ceramic analysis of Jörg Rambach, dated the mound to Early Helladic II, in the middle of the third millennium BC (p. 53), and showed that the black layer was separated from the Bronze Age constructions by a sterile layer of sand and contained Submycenaean cups of the end of the eleventh century BC (p. 54). Kyrieleis concludes that the cult of Zeus was established at the end of the eleventh century BC beside the mound, still visible, which would later be taken for the tomb of Pelops, that a thousand years of emptiness lie between the two, and that any continuity from the Mycenaean period is ruled out (p. 55).
>
> The offerings of the tenth to the eighth centuries BC are above all animal figurines, oxen, horses, sheep, rams and goats, of which some thousands are known, and bronze tripods, about 65 cm in height at the beginning of the ninth century BC and more than 3 metres for some of the second half of the eighth (Sinn, 2021, p. 67). Patay-Horváth (2020, p. 1) specifies that some 4,500 of these bronze figurines represent bovids and equids, against a few hundred rams, goats and birds, while lions or deer appear only sporadically. Their iconography bears no relation to athletics, and offerings with a martial theme dominate at Olympia as at no other Greek sanctuary. Sinn (pp. 70-71) explains this imprint by the oracle of Zeus, whose fame Strabo (8.3.30) emphasises and whose seers, the Iamidai and the Klytiadai, advised armies on campaign; an Iamid took part in the foundation of Syracuse in 734 BC, and the great tripods can be understood as offerings of gratitude to the oracle. Morgan, cited in Kyle (2015, p. 102), interprets the tripods as votive offerings and not as prizes in games held on the spot. Kyrieleis (2003, p. 48) adds that the excavations have yielded the largest known arsenal of Greek weapons, booty dedicated to Zeus after military victories, as we saw when dealing with the truce.
>
> The enlargement of c. 700 BC included levelling, the terrace of the treasuries and the diversion of the Kladeos 160 metres to the west, and responded to a sharp increase in visitors; Mallwitz showed that the digging of more than 250 wells began at the same time, and from the layout of the earliest he deduced that the stadium was built c. 700 BC (Sinn, 2021, pp. 67-68). Kyle (2015, p. 102) speaks of some 200 wells and summarises Mallwitz's conclusion: the first games, if 776 BC is historical, were modest, local and perhaps annual, and the great games did not emerge until c. 700 or even 680 BC. Between the foundation of the cult and the first stadium there lie, therefore, some three centuries, during which the sanctuary attracted pilgrims without competitions (Sinn, 2021, p. 69). Kyrieleis (2003, p. 50) explains that these wells were opened during the festivals to supply water to visitors, were used for a few days, without stone lining, and were filled in when the festival ended, often with damaged offerings which, as the property of the god, had to remain within the precinct.
>
> The three dates of the fourth point appear together in the chronology of Golden (2004, p. xvii). Hippias compiled his list c. 400 BC (Sinn, 2021, p. 69), at the end of the fifth century BC according to Kyle (2015, p. 100), who summarises the thesis of Christesen: Hippias assembled with considerable carelessness names from oral tradition and poetry, his data are not reliable until the beginning of the sixth century BC, and 776 BC lacks a solid basis, having been obtained by computing generations and using the list of Spartan kings to link Lycurgus with the foundation of the Games. Sinn (2021, p. 69) proposes another route: Hippias may have taken as his reference the festival of 476 BC, the first after Plataea, and added a round figure to it. Tarrant (2003, p. 352) recalls that Hippias himself came to Olympia to display his erudition and took part in the compilation of the list of victors.
>
> Homer, who composed c. 750-725 BC, makes no specific reference to the Olympic Games (Kyle, 2015, p. 74). He relates that Neleus of Pylos sent a chariot to funeral games in Elis and that Augeias kept it (Iliad 11.698-701), but those games need not be the ones at Olympia and the mention of a four-horse chariot is probably a later interpolation. For Kyle (p. 101), the Games of the eighth century BC were at most minor contests, foot races at a cult festival with symbolic wreaths, and the first Games grew out of modest festive games with foot races, until the chariot race, added in 680 BC, broadened aristocratic participation (p. 87). The programme in the table is the traditional list given by Younger (2021, p. 47) on the basis of Pausanias, and its dates before the sixth century BC share the fragility of the list of Hippias.
>
> Archaeology inverts the order of the accounts. First came the rite, a cult of shepherds and cattle-herders of the Alpheios valley; then the marker of distinction of the elites who dedicated tripods, and only at the end the athletic competition.
>
> Box added on 04/10, "A sanctuary full of weapons": the seers of Olympia, the Iamidai and the Klytiadai, sacrificed, read the will of Zeus, accompanied armies and gave advice before battle, which explains the generous offerings of weapons, and the victory monuments and the great tripod cauldrons were offerings of gratitude for that advice and not athletic prizes (Kyle, 2015, p. 103). The excavations have yielded the largest known arsenal of Greek weapons, booty dedicated to Zeus after military victories (Kyrieleis, 2003, p. 48).

### Diapositiva 44 · Koroibos of Elis, the «first victor»

**`Notas del orador`**

> The victory of Koroibos in 776 BC is the one that opens the lists of Olympic victors, and so it appears in Eusebius, in Aristotle and in the chronographers Eratosthenes and Apollodorus (Christesen, 2007, pp. 19-20). The length of the stadion, six hundred Olympic feet, about 192 metres, comes from the translator's note to Strabo (trans. 2008, p. 89, n. 250). For the first thirteen editions, from 776 to 728 BC, the stadion was the only event, since the diaulos was added in 724 BC (Younger, 2021, p. 47).
>
> The ancient source, Athenaeus (9.382b), calls Koroibos a mageiros, which in general means cook or butcher (Kyle, 2015, pp. 101 and 199). Spivey (2005, p. 201) recalls that the term also designated the ritual office of preparing and conducting the sacrifice, in which case Koroibos would have been a very important person in Elis. Christesen (2007, p. 481) sets out both readings and does not decide between them: a cook would be an unlikely competitor in games dominated by the elite, but the word may identify him as a cult official. Kyle (2015, p. 101) suggests that he may have accompanied a religious embassy sent to Olympia for a sacrifice, hence the cook, and won a casual race there.
>
> Pausanias (5.8.6 and 8.26.3-4) says that there was no statue of Koroibos at Olympia and that his tomb, on the boundary of Elean territory, bore an inscription declaring him the first victor in the stadion. Kyle (2015, p. 101) considers it suspect of being a later attribution to mark and claim territory, and proposes that Koroibos was simply the oldest name that the locals remembered. For the same reason the caption of the sculpture is left in the conditional.
>
> Eratosthenes and Apollodorus placed the games of Lycurgus and Iphitos in 884 BC and the victory of Koroibos in 776 BC, and called the intervening Olympiads "unrecorded" because the names of their victors had not been preserved (Christesen, 2007, p. 20). The 108 years between the two dates yield twenty-seven intervals of four years, an arithmetical result of reconciling two datings, and some chronographers went so far as to place the first games in 1581 BC (p. 19).
>
> Strabo (8.3.30) writes that the Eleans were in charge of the sanctuary and the games until the twenty-sixth Olympiad (trans. 2008, pp. 88-89); Kyle (2015, p. 99) attributes the first twenty-five to Elis, dates with reservations a Pisan phase begun by Pheidon of Argos c. 660 BC and places the restoration of Elean control c. 472 BC. Sinn (2021, p. 69) qualifies this by noting that the Eleans extended their hegemony as far as the Alpheios, and with it to Olympia, only c. 600 BC or shortly afterwards. Koroibos is the first case in the course in which a community uses an athlete to fix a frontier.
>
> Crowther (2003, p. 61) considers the sources on the early history of the Games problematic and admits that Elis probably held authority for almost the whole of the first century, as Strabo suggests, although Xenophon (Hellenica 7.4.28) records that Pisa claimed to have been the first to preside over the sanctuary. According to Pausanias (6.22.2), the Pisatans administered the festival of 748 BC, when Pheidon of Argos invaded Olympia and gave them control; Julius Africanus attributes to them the Games from the thirtieth to the fifty-second Olympiad, 660 to 572 BC, and Pantaleon, king of Pisa, seized by force those of 644 BC (Crowther, 2003, p. 61). Mallwitz saw archaeological support for this Pisan phase in the destruction of the black layer, the deposit of ash and offerings of the oldest altar, and Elis recovered control in the sixth century BC (p. 62). The date of Pheidon thus oscillates between the 748 BC of Pausanias and the c. 660 BC given by Kyle, and in class it is enough to say that Pisa disputed the sanctuary with Elis in the seventh century BC.

**`Fuente`**

> Christesen, P. (2007). *Olympic victor lists and ancient Greek history*. Cambridge University Press.
>
> Crowther, N. B. (2003). Elis and Olympia: City, sanctuary and politics. In D. J. Phillips & D. Pritchard (Eds.), *Sport and festival in the ancient Greek world* (pp. 61-74). The Classical Press of Wales.

### Diapositiva 46 · Who could compete in the Games?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The requirements for admission are today the subject of debate. Kyle (2015, p. 114) sums up Olympia as a "multinational but not multicultural" contest, reserved for Greek males, with women, foreigners and slaves excluded, and specifies that the judges had to make sure that those entered in the boys' events belonged to a Greek state and a tribe, had a father and a family, and were free and legitimate (Philostratus, Gymnasticus 25). Remijsen (2019, pp. 2-3) maintains, by contrast, that the rule excluding non-Greeks derives from the work of Krause in 1838, who defined Olympia as the Hellenic national festival, and that it was consolidated by a study of Crowther in 1996, and he records the reservation of Nielsen (2014), for whom no athlete is known to have been rejected on the grounds of ethnic identity (Remijsen, 2019, p. 3). The passage adduced as proof is that of Alexander I of Macedon (Herodotus 5.22), whom his rivals wished to bar by alleging that the competition was for Hellenes and not for barbarians; Remijsen (2019, pp. 9-10) stresses that Herodotus presents that demand as the opinion of the rivals, with the participle phamenoi, "alleging", and that the objection was raised during the competition itself and not in a check on arrival. Kyle (2015, p. 224) moreover describes the episode as suspect.
>
> The traditional list of requirements (a free man, a legitimate son and in possession of his civic rights) comes from handbooks such as that of Durántez (2010, p. 69), who narrates how, after the presentation of the runners in the stadium, the herald asked the public whether anyone could reproach any of them with "an impure birth, not being of free status, having been punished with infamous penalties or being a man of unworthy habits"; Durántez himself records in his glossary atimia and asebeia, impiety, as grounds for exclusion from the Games (p. 315). He does not cite the ancient source of the scene. The requirement of legitimacy is documented in Philostratus (Gymnasticus 25), c. AD 230, and only for the boys' events (Kyle, 2014a, p. 25). In Athens, moreover, the law excluded only slaves from the public gymnasia, and the nothoi, sons of a foreign mother, could frequent them (Kyle, 1993, p. 90). Miller (2003, p. 10) specifies that age was checked on the very day of the competition. Pritchard (2003, p. 309) documents the 100 drachmas of Hippomachos.
>
> According to Remijsen (2019, pp. 52-53), the only well-documented criteria are the admission of boys in their own category, the exclusion of the slave by means of a reactive procedure and, in the Roman period, the registration of the name and of the citizenship of the polis, a bureaucratic requirement that is often confused with a requirement of Hellenic identity; his conclusion is that the Games were open to any man who wished to compete. Papakonstantinou (2019, pp. 73-74 and 111) maintains the opposite reading, since for him the Hellanodikai, the Elean judges of the Games, decided whether an athlete was qualified by his physique and by his ethnic origin, and the rule of Hellenic descent was in force for centuries, until it faded away in the Hellenistic period. The student has here an open debate between two readings of the same sources. Miller (2003, p. 10) adds that there were no weight categories, only age categories, and that age was checked on the very day of the competition by examining the athlete, his father, his brothers and his trainer, with known cases of fraud.
>
> Kyle (2014a, p. 25) stresses that competitors went to Olympia on their own initiative, without any magistrate of their city selecting them, and that they could even change their athletic homeland, like Astylos, who won races for Croton in 488 and 484 BC and afterwards for Syracuse in 480 BC. Tyrrell (2004, p. 190) adds that the judges must have turned away murderers, the sacrilegious and violators of the truce, although he presents this as a deduction. Admission ultimately depended on Elis, which controlled the sanctuary almost without interruption from the sixth century BC, with brief usurpations by Pisa and by the Arcadians (Crowther, 2003, pp. 61-62), and whose magistrates could deny access to the athletes of an entire city.
>
> The decisive filter was economic. Kyle (1993, p. 149) writes that in the classical period the athlete needed leisure, training under instruction and money to travel to the games, and that this expense was much greater in the equestrian events. Civic rewards came only to the few victors, who had already financed their own preparation, and most Athenian athletes depended on family wealth; indeed, Kyle proposes that one first rose in economic class and afterwards, almost always the sons, entered athletics (p. 150). The paidotribes Hippomachos is transmitted by Lynkeus of Samos, cited by Athenaeus (13.584c), and Kyle (1993, p. 145, n. 131) compares his 100 drachmas with the Eleusis accounts of 329 BC, which paid skilled craftsmen between 2 and 2.5 drachmas a day, and concludes that the fee represented a considerable expense. Pritchard (2003, p. 309) specifies that those 100 drachmas paid for a complete course of physical education at the end of the fourth century BC, so that the figure does not contradict the 10 to 15 drachmas per pupil per year that Christesen (2014, p. 220) gives for the ordinary paidotribes and that we saw in "Greek Society", since one is the fee of a particular teacher and the other an average annual cost. Pritchard (2003, pp. 309 and 317) contrasts that fee with the lessons of the teacher of letters, cheap and widely attended, and recalls that the Athenian elite did not amount to 5% of the citizen body (p. 301). The inscription from Ephesos, of c. 300 BC, in which a trainer asks the city to subsidise the training and travel of a promising young man without resources, is the earliest testimony of sponsorship (Kyle, 1993, p. 150, n.
>
> The result can be seen in the figures of Kyle (2014a, pp. 31 and 33), since the ordinary athlete did not attend the great festivals because of the distance and the expense and competed in the games of his own region; at each Olympiad perhaps between 200 and 250 athletes took part, and of the Olympic victors at most a quarter are known. Demosthenes (Against Aristocrates 40; Miller, 2004b, no. 91) could say that the Games were open to all, and the claim was true in law and false in practice.
>
> No rule excluded the poor, and the cost of preparation excluded them just the same. This is the core of the function of distinction at Olympia.

### Diapositiva 47 · Training

**`Notas del orador`**

> The source that describes the tetrad is Philostratus, On Gymnastics, written c. AD 230 (Miller, 2004b, no. 43), and he does so in order to reject it. The tetrad consisted of a first day of preparation, a second of intensive effort, a third of relaxation and a fourth of moderate work, and Philostratus considers it a theory that has ruined practically all athletics, because the gymnastes who follows it pays no attention to the condition of the athlete in front of him, damaged by food, wine, secret snacks, mental strain or fatigue (Gymnasticus 47, in Sweet, 1987, p. 225). The case of Gerenos of Naukratis is his example, since after winning at Olympia he spent two days celebrating, on the third he arrived at the gymnasium with an upset stomach and his trainer, indignant that he was breaking the cycle, imposed on him an exercise that caused his death (Gymnasticus 54, in Sweet, 1987, pp. 227-228). In the same passage he relates that at Olympia the Hellanodikai did not train according to a fixed calendar, decided everything on the spot and punished with the whip any trainer who disobeyed their instructions.
>
> The same treatise attends to hygiene, psychology and sleep. Philostratus devotes chapters to anxious athletes, whom he recommends encouraging with words and training systematically, and he defends training directed at the individual athlete (Gymnasticus 53, in Sweet, 1987, p. 227). The punch bag or korykos was to be light for boxers, who trained only striking, and heavier and larger for pancratiasts, who used it to practise balance against its onrush and the endurance of shoulders and fingers (Gymnasticus 57; Miller, 2004b, no. 43). Running on deep sand for sprinters is reported by Tyrrell (2004, p. 66) on the basis of Lucian (Anacharsis 27).
>
> The trainer was a qualified and recognised figure. Pindar (Olympian 8.54-66) recalls that Melesias of Athens had been a victor and that under his instruction 30 victories were achieved, and he calls another Athenian, Menandros, a "maker of athletes" (Nemean 5.48-49) (Kyle, 2015, p. 195). Nicholson (2005, p. 7) explains that the aristocratic clans of Aegina turned to these Athenian trainers because they needed quality instruction in wrestling and pankration and were prepared to pay for it generously, and he records the debate over their status, since Figueira interprets Melesias as an unpaid aristocratic patron and other authors consider him a professional who was not, for that reason, despised as a wage-earner. The same author shows that Pindar, in Olympian 10, presents the trainer as a whetstone that adds nothing to the pupil and merely draws out what was already in him by birth (Nicholson, 2005, p. 156), a way of protecting the aristocratic idea of inherited excellence against a service that was paid for. Pritchard (2003, pp. 302-305) warns, along the same lines, that in Athens there is no reliable evidence of a specialised trainer distinct from the paidotribes until well into the fourth century BC.
>
> The idea of progressive overload comes from the anecdote of Milo transmitted by Quintilian (1.9.5), who carried the bull that he had become accustomed to carrying when it was a calf (Sweet, 1987, p. 106). Athenaeus (10.412f; Miller, 2004b, no. 163b) adds that at Olympia he carried a four-year-old bull on his shoulders around the stadium and then ate it in a single day. It is an anecdotal tradition, with no value as a description of a method.
>
> Greek training was conceived in the categories of medicine, and its greatest theorist wrote it against routine that pays no heed to the particular body.

### Diapositiva 48 · Palaestrae and gymnasia

**`Notas del orador`**

> The earlier version presented palaestrae and gymnasia as state institutions. Spivey (2005, p. 40) qualifies this by noting that the gymnasium was regarded as a public facility, whereas the palaestra could be founded and run privately, and Bertolín (2020, p. 135) adds that it was usual to integrate the palaestra into the public gymnasium and that the Greeks themselves sometimes used both terms as synonyms. García Romero (2015, p. 30) recalls that in Athens there was no public educational centre financed by the state and that the gymnastics teacher directed the exercises with a staff.
>
> The ideal arrangement reserved the palaestra for boys and the gymnasium for older boys and for young men under thirty, and the law of Beroia, of the second century BC, gave the boys a timetable of their own and forbade the young men to talk to them (Bertolín, 2020, p. 136).
>
> The gymnasiarch was in classical Athens a wealthy citizen who financed a public service, and in many Hellenistic cities a civic office responsible for the oil, the works and the prizes and obliged to render accounts on leaving it (Stavrou, 2016, pp. 106-109); for that reason "noble" becomes "citizen".
>
> The final paragraph rests on Spivey (2005, p. 35), who describes on the upper terrace of the gymnasium at Delphi, of the early fourth century BC, a xystos, a covered track of almost a stadion for running in bad weather, and on Pritchard (2013, p. 1) for restricted access. Spivey (2005, p. 34) warns that no material remains of any Greek gymnasium earlier than the middle of the fourth century BC survive, although tradition places the three Athenian gymnasia in use c. 600 BC. The palaestra at Olympia, of the third century BC, measures some 66 metres on each side, with the wrestling sand in the centre (p. 40).

### Diapositiva 49 · Who entered the gymnasium?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The law of Solon is known from Aeschines (Against Timarchus 1.138-139), and it forbids the slave to exercise in the gymnasium and to rub himself with oil in the palaestrae, and to love or follow a free boy, on pain of fifty lashes (Scanlon, 2002, p. 88). All the sources date from the fourth century BC or later, but their agreement allows us to think that Solon instituted some rule of this kind c. 580 BC (p. 213). Plutarch (Solon 1.4) interprets it shrewdly, since by forbidding the slave gymnastics and the love of boys the law placed them among honourable practices and encouraged the worthy to what it denied to the unworthy (Scanlon, 2002, p. 213). Golden (2008, p. 54) adds that one of the aims of that exclusion was to prevent sexual contact between slaves and free boys and young men. The rule remained in force for centuries, and the only known exception confirms the rule, since at Gytheion, already in the Roman period, the benefactress Phaenia Aromation endowed the supply of oil with 8,000 denarii and allowed slaves to use it six days a year on specific festivals (Golden, 2008, p. 42; Papakonstantinou, 2019, p. 123).
>
> The list from Beroia is given by Spivey (2005, p. 54), with slaves, male prostitutes, market traders, drunkards and the insane, and Stavrou (2016, pp. 44-45) completes it with freedmen and their sons, those of poor physical or mental condition, those who lacked a decent life and those who practised trades in the agora, such as merchants, craftsmen and labourers. The law of Amphipolis added a criterion of wealth, since it required an eighteen-year-old to have a family fortune of 3,000 drachmas in order to enrol in the ephebeia, a figure higher than the 2,000 required in Athens by Antipater in 322 BC (Stavrou, 2016, p. 45). Spivey understands the "male prostitutes" to have been youths who abused the customs of pederasty, and explains the exclusion of the agoraioi as pure snobbery, an assertion that the gymnasium was reserved for those who did not have to haggle for a living (pp. 54-55). Each gymnasium also had its own gods, Hermes at Beroia, Apollo at the Lyceum, Athena at the Academy and Herakles at the Kynosarges, and any breach of discipline could be regarded as sacrilege (p. 54).
>
> In Athens the main barrier was money. The paidotribes Hippomachos charged one hundred drachmas for a course (Pritchard, 2003, p. 309), the upper class did not amount to five per cent of the citizen body (p. 302), and Kyle's catalogue of 78 Athenian athletes and horsemen of the archaic and classical periods offers no certain case of humble origin, since the 50 whose social position can be determined belonged to the upper class (p. 324). Kyle (1993, p. 116) shows, moreover, that the so-called new athletes of the second half of the fifth century BC were equally rich, although their fortune did not come from inherited land. The law, by contrast, excluded only slaves from the public gymnasia, so that foreigners and nothoi could frequent them (Kyle, 1993, p. 90).
>
> The tradition about the Kynosarges comes from Plutarch (Themistocles 1), according to whom Themistocles, himself of half-foreign origin, persuaded young men of good family to train with him there, and in doing so partly erased the boundary between nobles and commoners (Spivey, 2005, p. 55). Kyle (1993, p. 90) considers that the anecdote informs us above all about the political ambition of Themistocles, or about Plutarch as a biographer, and hardly at all about social segregation in the gymnasium. Tensions of rank persisted. Alcibiades took part at Olympia in 416 BC with seven teams of horses, and in the speech that Isocrates wrote for his son we read that he despised gymnastic events because some athletes were of low birth, from small cities and of poor education (Bertolín, 2020, p. 195), testimony that for Pritchard (2003, pp. 326-327) confirms that athletics was perceived as a preserve of the elite. The underlying statistics, with the exclusion of the ptôchoi, are in "Greek Society".
>
> The gymnasium was closed by law to the slave and, in practice, to anyone who could not pay the paidotribes or have the leisure to train.

### Diapositiva 50 · Diet


**`Notas del orador`**

> The sources describe a succession in the athlete's diet. Miller (2004b, no. 217) records a change from dried figs, fresh cheese and wheat flour to meat, attributed to a Pythagoras who may have been the philosopher or a masseur (aleiptês) of the same name (Diogenes Laertius 8.12-13; Pliny, Natural History 23.121), and the version of Pausanias (6.7.10), according to which Dromeus of Stymphalos, winner of the dolichos, the long-distance race, at Olympia in 484 and 480 BC, twice at Delphi, three times at Isthmia and five times at Nemea, devised the meat diet when until then athletes had fed on fresh cheese. Golden (2004, p. 52) places the change c. 500 BC and points out that the earlier diet was that of any Greek; abundant, meat-based eating marked the athlete from then on throughout antiquity.
>
> Epictetus, who speaks of his own time, requires the aspirant to submit to rules and to a diet, to give up sweets, to exercise at a fixed hour in heat or in cold, not to drink cold water and sometimes not wine either, and to hand himself over to his trainer as to a doctor (Handbook 29, in Tyrrell, 2004, pp. 189-190). The habitual drink of the Greek was wine, almost always mixed with water (Sweet, 1987, p. 200).
>
> The athlete's diet was the object of criticism from very early on. Nicholson (2014, p. 74) records a Hippocratic text of the late fifth century BC that censures the meat diet because it caused diarrhoea in some athletes, and Aristotle's criticism of excessive diets, which destroyed the good proportion of the body. Voracity became a commonplace, since Athenaeus attributes to Milo twenty pounds of meat, twenty of bread and eight quarts of wine a day (Miller, 2004b, no. 163b), and an epigram of Posidippus has the statue of Theagenes of Thasos say that he ate a whole ox on a wager (Nicholson, 2014, p. 74).
>
> Eating meat daily was within the reach of very few, and the athlete's body displayed that expense.

### Diapositiva 53 · Levels of management at Olympia

**`Notas del orador`**

> Olympia was the administration of one polis over its own sanctuary. Nielsen (2014, p. 134) explains that the Greeks did not call Olympia "Panhellenic", but did say that "any Greek who wished" could compete there (Herodotus 2.160), and that its events were open to every free Greek male. In practice, the sanctuary was administered by a single city, Elis, situated some 60 km away by road and 36 in a straight line; under normal conditions, only Elis controlled admission to the sanctuary and the competitions, set the rules and appointed all the personnel from among its citizens (Nielsen, 2014, p. 136). The figure of some 45,000 attendees is the estimate of Nielsen (2014, p. 136), who in a later work speaks of between 30,000 and 45,000 people for the great Panhellenic games (Nielsen, 2023, p. 84); Kyle (2015, p. 112) raises the figure to perhaps 100,000. Some handbooks present an "Olympic Senate" with nomophylakes and spondophoroi under their orders, an organisation chart that derives from Fernández Truan (vol. 1, p. 179) and refers to no source.
>
> The material evidence is provided by the site itself. The Eleans built at Olympia a prytaneion and a bouleuterion, buildings of ordinary political administration that were usually situated in the main urban centre of each polis. The prytaneion was the physical expression of the existence of the city, and on its hearth burned the eternal flame "which signified the life of the polis"; its location at Olympia amounted to a declaration of ownership of the sanctuary (Nielsen, 2014, p. 139). According to Pausanias, it was there that the Eleans gave the banquet for the victors at the end of the Games. Yalouris & Yalouris (1995, p. 14) date the prytaneion to the early fifth century BC, in the north-west corner of the Altis, the sacred precinct. Likewise, from the sixth century BC Elis published its decrees at Olympia in inscriptions written in a markedly Elean dialect, which was in itself a demonstration of control (Nielsen, 2014, p. 138). The contrast is offered by the treasuries that other cities built in the sanctuary, since Neer (2007, p. 226) proposes as a criterion of a Panhellenic sanctuary precisely that it admitted constructions by foreign cities, so that Olympia belonged to all through its offerings and to Elis through its government.
>
> The flame of the prytaneion bears no relation to the modern Olympic fire. Kyrieleis (2003, p. 47), speaking from the standpoint of the German archaeology of Olympia itself, explains that the campaigns begun in 1937 were prepared within the framework of the Berlin Games in order to influence public opinion, and that to this end a symbolic act was invented to unite the modern Games with ancient Olympia, the lighting of the Olympic flame and its relay to the host city of each Olympiad, held for the first time in 1936; in his words, "it has nothing to do with Antiquity".
>
> The subordinate staff comes from Kyle (2015, p. 113), who lists the mastigophoroi or whip-bearers, the rhabdouchoi or rod-bearers, the alytarch and the alytai, who acted as police of the precinct, in addition to cooks, heralds and slaves. The great festival of all the Greeks was, at the same time, the showcase of one particular city.

### Diapositiva 55 · The calendar of the Games

**`Notas del orador`**

> Kyle (2014a, p. 23) places the Games at the end of the summer, in July or August, at the second full moon after the solstice, and explains the choice by two reasons: it coincided with a pause in agricultural work and depended on an astronomical phenomenon that any Greek could understand. Some handbooks speak of the first full moon, and Tyrrell (2004, p. 189), following Miller, reconciles the two versions. A Panhellenic festival could not be governed by the calendar of Elis or by that of any other polis, since each had its own, and the sky offered the only common reference. The presence of the athletes in Elis thirty days before the festival indicates that the first full moon marked their arrival, and the Games fell at the second, so that the sacred month occupied the interval between the two. Miller (2003, p. 5) works this out for a hypothetical Olympiad of 300 BC, in which the truce would have run from 10 July to 9 August.
>
> The spondophoroi, or truce-bearers, announced the Games and invited athletes, spectators and official embassies, the theoriai (Kyle, 2014a, pp. 23 & 25). Nielsen (2023, p. 76) describes the complete system of announcement, the epangelia, in which the organising city sent delegations of theoroi or spondophoroi and the receiving cities appointed a theorodokos from among their elite; for Olympia, the epangelia is attested at Acragas at the beginning of the fifth century BC, the theorodokia in the middle of that century and the truce between 460 and 450 BC. Inscription IvO 64, of 28 BC, forms part of a series of lists of sanctuary officials who served throughout the whole Olympiad and not only during the festival (Miller, 2004b, no. 133), and in it the three spondophoroi appear with their names.
>
> The truce was studied when dealing with Iphitos, and here it is enough to recall its scope. Kyle (2014a, p. 23) specifies that it forbade armies to enter the territory of Elis during the Games and ordered safe passage, through any state, for those travelling to or from Olympia, although it did not stop wars in Greece, and Tyrrell (2004, p. 189) adds that the Greeks waged war above all in summer, so that a general peace at that time of year would have made war almost impossible.
>
> The calendar and the truce belong to the function of collective identity, since communities with different calendars, laws and dialects accepted a single date, fixed by the sky, on which to gather before Zeus.

### Diapositiva 56 · The gathering at Elis

**`Notas del orador`**

> The thirty-day stay is documented. Philostratus (Life of Apollonius 5.43; Miller, 2004b, no. 84) writes that, as the festival approached, the Eleans trained the athletes for thirty days in Elis itself, and Kyle (2014a, pp. 24-25) confirms it. Tyrrell (2004, p. 190) specifies that in the time of Pausanias athletes, the fathers and brothers of the boys, and trainers had to assemble in Elis at the first full moon after the solstice, on pain of disqualification, and that the stay was at the athlete's own expense. Miller (2003, p. 5) spells out the penalty: an athlete who arrived late and could not prove that illness, pirates or shipwreck had delayed him paid a fine, and if he did not pay it he was flogged. It is not known since when the stay was required, and Tyrrell suggests that it may have been imposed in the fourth century BC to reassert the control of Elis over the Games; in his view, the requirement contributed to the rise of athletic professionalism, or was a consequence of it. Crowther (2003, p. 68) adds that no other festival imposed a comparable compulsory period, which meant a considerable expense for the foreign athlete and a home advantage for the Eleans.
>
> Apollonius Rhantes, of Alexandria, arrived late at the Olympiad of AD 93 and pleaded contrary winds; his compatriot Heraclides showed that in reality he had been collecting prizes at the games of Ionia, and the Eleans disqualified him (Golden, 2004, p. 13; Tyrrell, 2004, p. 189). The case brings together the two sides of the section, the month at Elis as an obligation and the circuit of games with prizes in cash as a way of life.
>
> The figure of ten months appears in two places. The athletes of the men's category swore before Zeus Horkios, over pieces of boar's flesh, that they had completed ten consecutive months of training (Pausanias 5.24.9-10; Miller, 2004b, no. 90), a promise that nobody could verify. The elected Hellanodikai lived for ten months in the Hellanodikaion at Elis while the guardians of the law taught them their duties (Pausanias 6.24.3; Miller, 2004b, no. 83), and Kyle (2014a, p. 24) places the beginning of the preparations at that moment, ten months before the Games.
>
> The selection is well documented. Spivey (2005, p. 76) writes that all the aspirants underwent an official examination and that those who did not reach certain standards were sent home, in order to ensure the quality of the competition. Pausanias (6.23; Miller, 2004b, no. 83) describes the old gymnasium at Elis, with the Hall of the Hundred Feet, where the Hellanodikai paired the wrestlers by age and then by ability, and relates that they entered before dawn to pair the runners and at midday for the pentathletes, the athletes of the combined event of five disciplines, and for the heavy combat events. Miller (2003, p. 4) adds that Elis had three gymnasia, and that the third was reserved for the ephebes of the city throughout the time of the Olympic festival. Kyle (2014a, p. 25) points out that the judges supervised the athletes to guarantee their competence and to deter or remove those whom they considered unworthy, so that the documented criteria are age, ability and conduct. Bodily beauty does not appear as a criterion of admission in any source, although the ideal of kalokagathia, which united the beautiful (kalos) and the good (agathos), explains why a Greek associated a good appearance with worth; indeed, the Panathenaia, the great civic festival of Athens, included male beauty contests in its programme (Kyle, 2014a, p. 33).
>
> Kyle (2014a, p. 24) places Elis some 25 miles north of Olympia, about 40 km, and Waterfield (2018, ch. 4) speaks of more than 35 miles along the Sacred Way, whose exact course is unknown; The procession, instituted in the sixth century BC, left one or two days before the Games, stopped to spend the night and was purified at the spring of Pieria before entering the sanctuary; it was made up of perhaps several hundred people, with some 200 athletes (Kyle, 2014a, p. 25; Waterfield, 2018, ch. 4). Before departing, the judges offered the athletes a last opportunity to withdraw: 'if you have laboured in a manner worthy of going to Olympia, if you have done nothing indolent or ignoble, take heart and go; those of you who have not trained in this way, go wherever you wish' (Philostratus, Life of Apollonius 5.43; Miller, 2004b, no. 84).
>
> The sanctuary selected the athletes to guarantee the quality of what it was going to offer to an audience that Kyle (2014a, p. 25) raises to some one hundred thousand spectators and Nielsen (2023, p. 84) places, more cautiously, between 30,000 and 45,000, and the fairness of access concerned it much less.
>

### Diapositiva 59 · Naked in the stadium


**`Notas del orador`**

> The chronology of athletic nudity sets the literary sources against archaeology. McDonnell (1991, p. 182) considers it fairly certain, from Bronze Age archaeology and the Homeric poems, that athletic nudity was not practised before the end of the eighth century BC. The ancient tradition attributes the innovation to Orsippos of Megara, to Acanthus of Sparta or to an anonymous Athenian, with dates ranging from the 14th Olympiad (724 BC) to the 32nd (652 BC), and the oldest source on Orsippos is the epigram of a late Megarian inscription, which some date to the time of Hadrian and Moretti to the fifth century AD (McDonnell, 1991, p. 183). Bonfante (1989, p. 552) records the two main versions, that of Orsippos, who supposedly lost his perizoma while running, and that of Dionysius of Halicarnassus, who attributes the novelty to Acanthus the Lacedaemonian in the 15th Olympiad. Kyle (2015, p. 84) considers the date of Orsippos conventional, and Pausanias (1.44.1) probably saw at Megara the inscription declaring him the first Greek "crowned naked".
>
> Thucydides (1.6.5) writes that the Lacedaemonians were the first to exercise naked, that before then, even at the Olympic Games, athletes competed wearing a loincloth and that the custom had ceased only a few years earlier. McDonnell (1991, p. 184) sets against that testimony the more than 800 naked athletes in sixth-century BC Greek art catalogued by Legakis, more than 600 of them on Attic vases, and concludes that nudity was general practice in Athens by the middle of that century, and probably earlier in Sparta and at Olympia, so that Thucydides and Plato, who depends on him, must be rejected on this point (p. 193). The so-called perizoma vases, with athletes covered by a white cloth at the end of the sixth century BC, were made for the Etruscan market and do not reflect an Athenian fashion (McDonnell, 1991, pp. 186-189). Christesen (2012b, pp. 174-175) dates the beginning between 700 and 575 BC and considers nudity common by the middle of the sixth century BC, and proposes, as Kyle (2015, p. 84) reports, that Orsippos was the first crowned naked because he won at the first Olympiad in which nudity was compulsory, a rule that was imposed in the non-equestrian events in the first half of the sixth century BC. Sansone (1988, p. 107) qualifies this by noting that the riders in the horse races also competed naked, and that only the charioteers wore a long tunic.
>
> The Greeks themselves conjectured that nudity made running faster or safer, and there was even a story of a runner who tripped over his loincloth and died (Christesen, 2012b, p. 175). Kyle (2015, p. 84) considers these explanations apocryphal. Bonfante (1989, p. 543) understands Greek nudity as a cultural costume, and applies to it the reasons why human beings dress, protection, social distinction, modesty, aesthetics and apotropaic magic; she also recalls that for Herodotus (1.10.3), among the Lydians and nearly all barbarians, being seen naked was a great shame even for a man (p. 546), and that Thucydides used that difference to separate the Greeks from the barbarians and from their own past. Sansone (1988, p. 111) places athletic nudity in the same field as initiation rites, with the Cretan example of the youth who appeared naked before receiving his arms. In gymnasium culture nudity communicated citizenship, status, leisure, physical care and bodily virtue (Kyle, 2015, p. 84).
>
> Pausanias (5.6.7-8) tells that Kallipateira, also called Pherenike, a widow and a member of a family of Olympic victors, entered the stadium disguised as a trainer to watch her son compete, even though a married woman was forbidden even to be present at the Games (Golden, 2008, p. 10), that she was discovered when she jumped the barrier as he won and went unpunished out of respect for her family, and that from then on trainers had to strip. The rule affects trainers, the nudity of the athletes is already taken for granted, and Kyle (2014b, p. 266) warns that the story became confused and was inflated in the sources with the passage of time.
>
> Outside sport, public nudity was a humiliation, and palaestrae and gymnasia were surrounded by walls that protected them from view (Christesen, 2012b, pp. 174-175); Plato (Republic 452c) recalls that not so long before the Greeks, like most barbarians, had considered it shameful to see a man naked. Christesen (2012b, pp. 176-177) gives the custom a double effect. Thucydides suggests that it reduced inequalities, since the rich had used luxurious dress to mark distance, although nudity also gave plousioi and penêtes an unusual uniform that set them apart from the rest and drove the poorest, the ptôchoi, away from the gymnasium, since whoever worked the land in a short tunic displayed the ancient equivalent of the "farmer's tan", and the craftsman, who worked indoors, was noticeably pale. The terms in the box are from Christesen (2012b, p. 177), and Kyle (2015, p. 84) adds that the worker who stripped was exposed to public shame.
>
> In Sparta nudity also had a function of surveillance. Christesen (2012a, p. 241), following Foucault, maintains that the compulsory nudity of the Spartiates from youth, who exercised in view of their comrades, generated constant pressure to look like the ideal soldier, since there was no way of hiding an inadequate physique, and the same author recalls the ancient tradition that made athletic nudity a Spartan invention (p. 242, n. 159).
>
> The naked body, which apparently erased differences, displayed them more precisely than any garment, and it is the clearest example of the function of distinction in Greek sport.

### Diapositiva 60 · Values associated with the Olympic Games

**`Notas del orador`**

> Agon. Its sentence said that in competition there is no place for luck. At Olympia chance played a regulated role: the Hellanodikai paired the rivals and assigned lanes and byes by lot (Kyle, 2014a, p. 25), and Lucian (Hermotimus 40; Miller, 2004b, no. 97) describes the procedure, with tokens the size of beans, marked in pairs with the same letter, which each athlete drew from a silver urn after praying to Zeus.
>
> Kalokagathia. Spivey (2005, p. 57) describes the classical belief that beauty was invested with morality: to look good was to be good, and a coward could be recognised by the droop of his shoulders.
>
> Arete. Aristoi is plural; the singular is aristos. Miller (2004b, glossary) warns that the word has no simple equivalent and brings together excellence, goodness, manliness, courage, nobility and virtue. The agreement 'la más alta valor' is also corrected, becoming 'el más alto valor', as are the English quotation marks of the three entries.
>
> Kyle (2014a, p. 33) summarises the values that almost all athletes shared: effort, virtue, piety, endurance or expenditure (ponos) and humility (aidos); victory brought glory (kleos), tempered by humility and the acknowledgement of divine favour. In Pindar, the crowned victor was to show composure, and the defeated man returned home by back alleys, amid jeers (Kyle, 2015, p. 195).

### Diapositiva 62 · The meaning of victory

**`Notas del orador`**

> The sanctuary gave a crown of symbolic value, and the victor's city compensated generously for what Olympia did not pay. D'Amore (2021, p. 516) explains the mechanism, since the timē did not belong to the athlete alone and reached the whole community that he represented, so that Athens, Sparta, Croton or Sybaris rewarded their victors with money and special honours, and tyrants did the same, as Hieron of Syracuse did with Astylos of Croton. Hyde (1921, p. 32) recalls that the herald, in proclaiming the victory, also proclaimed the name of the athlete's city, which thus shared in his success, and Nielsen (2023, p. 84) specifies that, although athletes competed out of personal ambition and without intervention from their polis, from the Classical period the victor was perceived as its representative, and the city was said to have been 'crowned'. The reverse is shown by Astylos himself, who had himself proclaimed a Syracusan to please Hieron and saw his fellow citizens of Croton burn his house and pull down the statue that they had dedicated to him in the temple of Hera (Hyde, 1921, p. 32).
>
> The reward came on returning home. Miller (2003, p. 30) describes the olive wreath as an ephemeral token, which withered quickly, and places the real prize in the triumphal entry into the city, the eiselasis, and in the free meals for life, the sitesis. Hyde (1921, p. 32) attributes to Solon the custom that Athenian victors dined in the prytaneion at public expense, and Pritchard (2003, pp. 324-325) documents that from the 430s BC Athens granted the sitesis and probably the proedria to its victors at the four great games; the sixth-century BC date for the origin of these honours therefore rests on the Solonian tradition. Tax exemption, the ateleia, figures among the privileges of victors alongside public meals and the proedria (Weiler, 2021, p. 530), but Golden (2004, p. 19) places it as a reward of the crown games in the Roman imperial period, and the library offers no secure testimony for Classical Greece. Herodotus (3.137; Miller, 2004b, no. 216) relates that the physician Democedes of Croton betrothed himself to the daughter of the wrestler Milo, paying a large sum, in order to show Darius that he was somebody in his homeland; the prestige belonged to the athlete father-in-law.
>
> The statue closed the cycle of victory. To erect a statue in the Altis, the sacred precinct of Olympia, was an honour that the Elean magistrates granted to the victor, and Hyde (1921, p. 28) explains the apparent contradiction between Pliny, for whom all victors dedicated a statue, and Pausanias, who says that not all did so, because of the cost of making, transporting and setting it up, which obliged the poor victor to content himself with a small statuette of bronze or marble. Neer (2007, p. 230) specifies that the victor could erect one without being obliged to, and that such statues are securely attested at Olympia from 544 BC. Victors also commissioned offerings, inscriptions and odes from Simonides, Pindar or Bacchylides, to the point of sustaining an entire auxiliary industry (D'Amore, 2021, p. 517). Nicholson (2005, pp. 1 & 4) warns that those monuments and songs almost always passed over in silence the charioteers, jockeys and trainers who had made the victory possible, and attributes that silence to the ideological battle of their patrons, since the poverty of those professionals is not enough to explain it. Papakonstantinou (2019, p. 47) shows that Athenian families such as the Alcmaeonidae, the Philaidae or the Calliadae used equestrian victory as a politics of elite identity, and Kyle (1993, p. 175) recalls that this direct route from victory to political power was exhausted towards the beginning of the fourth century BC.
>
> Currie (2002, p. 24) studies Euthymos of Locri, victor in Olympic boxing in 484, 476 and 472 BC and defeated in 480 BC by Theagenes of Thasos, and places around the middle of the fifth century BC the first documented cult of a living person in Greece, with Euthymos among the earliest cases if the testimony of Callimachus, transmitted by Pliny, is reliable. Currie (2002, p. 37) further maintains that the honoured man could actively promote his heroisation in his lifetime, against the idea that it was a concession which the community decided only after death, although success always depended on the consent of that community (p. 44).
>
> The victor returned to his city in a triumphal parade, and athletes were the only social group that Attic comedy scarcely ridiculed (Pritchard, 2003, pp. 324-325). Agonistic culture reserved no consolation for the runner-up (Kyle, 2015, p. 195), and the rewards had their critics from Xenophanes and Tyrtaeus onwards (Papakonstantinou, 2021, p. 593).
>
> The athlete won in the name of his city, the city paid for it, and the victor's monument was also a monument of the polis.
>
> Corrected on 04/10 with Pritchard (2013, p. 97): the victor in the men's stadion probably received 80 amphorae, 73 metretai of oil; each amphora held 11 choes, about 36 litres, and the metretes was worth c. 17 drachmas, so that the prize came to some 1,247 drachmas, and that for the chariot of adult horses, 140 amphorae, some 2,182. With the daily wages of 2 to 2.5 drachmas of the skilled craftsman from 'Who could compete in the Games?', the prize is equivalent to more than a year's work.

**`Fuente`**

> D'Amore, L. (2021). Economic aspects of athletic competition in the archaic and classical age. In A. Futrell & T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 511-520). Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 69 · The stadium of Olympia

**`Notas del orador`**

> Romano (2021, p. 212) explains the three senses of stadion: the linear distance of 600 feet, the race over that distance and the place where it was held. He relates the 600 feet to the Sumerian and Babylonian sexagesimal mathematics and proposes that the word derives from the verb histemi, 'to stand', that is, 'the place where one stands', since in the earliest stadia the public remained standing on low embankments. The absolute length depended on the local foot, which varied between about 0.296 and 0.320 metres (Miller, 2004b, glossary, s.v. 'stadion'). Olympia measured 192.28 metres between the starting lines and Halieis 166.50 (Romano, 2021, p. 212); Yalouris (1979, p. 160) adds Priene with 191.39, Athens with 184.96, Epidaurus with 181.30 and Delphi with 177.55. Handbooks in Spanish usually repeat 192.27 metres. Miller (2003, p. 23) rounds the track at Olympia to 192 metres and those at Nemea and Delphi to 178, and draws from that variation a fundamental difference from modern sport, since neither times nor distances were recorded, only the names of the victors.
>
> The history of the precinct is summarised by Kyle (2015, p. 109). There are wells of c. 700 BC and a retaining wall of c. 550 BC for a low embankment, and a stadium of c. 500 BC with a higher southern embankment. The next one was shifted 12 metres to the north and 75 to the east, with embankments to the south, north and west, a capacity of some 40,000 spectators and a track of about 212 by 28.5 metres. The date of this move is disputed: Kyle places it c. 475-450 BC, Golden (2004), in his initial chronology, dates 'Stadium III' to 450 BC, and Miller (2014, p. 291) speaks of the stadium built c. 350 BC. Kyle (2015, p. 108) specifies that the present restoration reproduces the state of c. 350 BC. On the north side the track rested against the Hill of Kronos, and when the new stadium was built artificial embankments were raised to close the other three sides (Yalouris, 1979, p. 160).
>
> The figure of 40,000 is the most frequently cited, and Yalouris (1979, p. 160) reaches 45,000. Scanlon (2002, p. 422) revises it downwards: with one square metre per spectator he calculates a maximum of some 28,500, and only with the public standing and packed together, at 0.71 square metres per person, would 40,000 be reached. Olympia never had stone seating, unlike other stadia, perhaps because the site originally belonged to Demeter Chamyne (Kyle, 2015, p. 109). The only permanent seats were those of the judges, on the south side, and that of the priestess of Demeter, opposite (Miller, 2014, p. 291). Miller (2003, p. 20) adds that the spectators from Elis were placed around the judges' tribune, a home advantage that was sometimes exploited, in line with what we saw when dealing with Elis as both judge and interested party.
>
> The stadium of Olympia was a space of spectacle without the architecture of spectacle. Tens of thousands of people watched the races on the grass, and the only visible hierarchy was that of the judges and that of the goddess.

**`Fuente`**

> Romano, D. G. (2021). Greek footraces and field events. In A. Futrell & T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 209-220). Oxford University Press.
>
> Scanlon, T. F. (2002). *Eros and Greek athletics*. Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 70 · The start

**`Notas del orador`**

> Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) and Miller (2003, pp. 20-21) describe the scene: the runners waited in the tunnel until they heard their name, came out one by one onto the track amid the applause of their supporters and the boos of their rivals, and took their places barefoot and standing before the stone threshold. Miller (2003, p. 21) explains that the two grooves of the balbis fixed the posture that the images show again and again, with one foot slightly advanced and the arms extended above the cords of the mechanism. The upright posture is proven: Miller (2004a, pp. 36-37) collects the only two ancient images of a crouching start and shows that in one of them a judge, with his rod, stops the runner. Valavanis (1999, p. 4) records the definition of the lexicographer Meris, according to which the grooves of the balbis ensured that all had the same posture and the hysplex that all set off at the same instant.
>
> The starting calls are recorded by Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) and Miller (2004a, p. 36) with ellipses between the first and the last, and Miller (2003, p. 22) completes the sequence with three commands, poda para poda, 'foot beside foot', which placed the athlete in the balbis, hetoimoi, 'ready!', and apite, 'go!', with which the starter released the cords of the mechanism. Golden (2004, p. 12, s.v. aphetes) defines that official as responsible for the oral command and for operating the mechanism. The trumpet is documented at the start of the chariot races, in Sophocles' Electra, and to signal the last lap of the horse races (Waterfield, 2018, chs. 4-5); Valavanis (1999, p. 2) adds that Heliodorus (Aethiopica 4.3), in a much later novel, places Theagenes in the balbis at Delphi hearing the blast of the trumpet, which points, without proving it, to an audible signal in the foot races as well.
>
> The floggings for a false start are recorded by Kyle (2015, p. 109) and Crowther (2007, p. 60), who speaks of public floggings. The oldest testimony is from Herodotus (8.59): before Salamis, the Corinthian Adeimantus warned Themistocles that at the games those who start before the signal are flogged, and Themistocles replied that those who are left behind are not crowned (Valavanis, 1999, p. 1; Miller, 2014, p. 293, n. 3). Plutarch attributes to the Spartan king Leon, who is not to be confused with Leon of Ambracia, the runner we saw appealing to the Olympic Council, the saying that the runners at Olympia cared more for advantage than for justice (Moralia 224F, in Miller, 2004b, no. 25). Valavanis (1999, p. 1) warns, however, that later references to false starts have a mainly metaphorical character and do not prove the practice of each author's own time.
>
> The chronology of the hysplex requires distinguishing two systems. Balbis appears in Aristophanes in 424 BC (Knights) and hysplex in 411 BC (Lysistrata), both without explanation, a sign that they were everyday words (Valavanis, 1999, p. 3). The oldest known system is that of Stadium I at Isthmia, with individual wooden barriers that fell under their own weight; Broneer dated it c. 470-460 BC, and the excavations of Gebhard and Hemans lowered it to the second half of the fifth century BC (Valavanis, 1999, p. 7). The second system, with two torsion posts and cords at knee and waist height, is first documented by a Panathenaic amphora of the archon Lykiskos, of 344/343 BC (p. 24); Valavanis dates its conception to the middle of the fourth century BC and its installation in the stadia of the Peloponnese to c. the beginning of the third century BC (p. 143), with the works at Nemea between 330 and 270 BC and those at Corinth after 280 BC (p. 50). No hysplex survives at Olympia, although cuttings in the blocks of the eastern balbis indicate that there was a mechanism from the fourth century BC (pp. 58-59).
>
> This distinction resolves the clash of dates. Waterfield (2018, ch. 4) places the hysplex in the last quarter of the fourth century BC and Miller (2014, pp. 289-290) c. 320 BC, and both are in fact referring to the introduction of the torsion system in the stadia, which coincides with the dates at Nemea. Yalouris (1979, p. 161), who dates the one at Isthmia from the beginning of the fourth century BC, falls short of the revision recorded by Valavanis.
>
> The purpose was to prevent anyone from gaining an advantage by starting early and to eliminate the subjectivity of the judge who accused a runner of a false start (Miller, 2014, p. 290), although Romano (2021, p. 212) doubts that it put an end to them. Valavanis (1999, p. 52) formulates it thus: once equality at the start was guaranteed, victory went to the athlete who ran best on the track. The torsion mechanism, moreover, shared the vocabulary of catapults and siege engines, and Valavanis attributes it to the milieu of the engineers of Philip II, c. 360-350 BC (pp. 32-33). Miller (2014, p. 290) stresses that it arrives at the same time as the professional athlete of the spectacle, since identical conditions for all guaranteed the uncertainty of the result.
>
> The terminology is unstable already in the sources. The balbis is properly the area of the track marked off for discus throwers, and sometimes the starting line; the bater is 'that which is trodden', a threshold, and designates the take-off board of the jump and, in general, the starting line of the stadium (Miller, 2004b, glossary, s.v. 'balbis', 'bater'). The lines were at first drawn in the earth and could be moved in someone's favour, and for that reason from the fifth century BC they were built of stone (Yalouris, 1979, p. 160). The lanes were assigned by lot (Waterfield, 2018, ch. 5).
>
> The start is the place where the Greek concern for equality of conditions is best seen, and also its price, since the fairness of the race was protected by the whip on the body of the offender.

**`Fuente`**

> Valavanis, P. (1999). *Hysplex: The starting mechanism in ancient stadia. A contribution to ancient Greek technology*. University of California Press.

**`Notas del orador`**

> The thresholds at Olympia are long, narrow slabs with two parallel grooves 0.18 metres apart, sockets for posts every 1.25 metres and positions for 20 runners; Delphi had 17 or 18, Miletus 12, Epidaurus 11 and Priene 6, and at Corinth the continuous grooves are replaced by two hollows per position, 0.63 metres apart (Yalouris, 1979, p. 161). Valavanis (1999, p. 62) explains the figure for Delphi: the stadium went from 20 to 17 places shortly before the middle of the third century BC, when the three bases of the hýsplex were installed at the ends and in the centre of the balbís. At Epidaurus he calculates phases of eleven and of nine places, and ten in the monumental Hellenistic phase (p. 111). The posts, which marked the lanes, also served as the kampter, the turning post, in the there-and-back races.
>
> Miller (2004a, pp. 36-37) dates the oldest known thresholds, at Nemea, to c. 500 BC, with a single groove of about 10 centimetres, and considers the double groove normal in the fourth century BC. The runner hooked the toes of the forward foot over the edge of the block and set those of the rear foot in the groove, with arms extended above the cords of the mechanism (Miller, 2003, p. 21). The lanes measured between 88 and 92 centimetres and were numbered with letters. Yalouris (1979, p. 164) describes the posture: front knee bent, heel raised, rear leg extended, body leaning forward and arms stretched ahead. In the race in armour only the right arm was thrust forward, since the left held the shield.
>
> The two grooves fulfilled the same function as the barrier, to make the runners equal, and the definition of Meris reported by Valavanis (1999, p. 4) says so expressly: the balbís ensured that all had the same posture.

### Diapositiva 72 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> The oldest known system is that of Stadium I at Isthmia, which Yalouris (1979, p. 161) describes as follows: the installation formed an isosceles triangle whose base was the starting line; at the apex, a pit 0.53 metres in diameter housed the starting judge; from each post a groove ran to that pit, and along them ran the cords that held the barriers in front of the runners. Valavanis (1999, p. 7) specifies that each runner had his own wooden barrier, which fell under its own weight when the judge released all the cords at once, and dates the whole to the second half of the fifth century BC. Broneer called this system balbís and reserved hýsplex for the later ones, and Valavanis (1999, p. 9) corrects that terminology, since the Isthmia one combines all the features that the sources attribute to the hýsplex.
>
> The second system is shown by a Panathenaic amphora of the archon Lykiskos, of 344/343 BC, with a runner in the race in armour before two horizontal cords, at knee and waist height, stretched between torsion posts (Valavanis, 1999, p. 24). The parts of the mechanism had the same names as those of catapults and siege engines, and a sharp tug made the cords drop all at once and simultaneously for everyone (pp. 32-33).
>
> The written sources confirm its use over centuries. Two inventories from Delos, of 166-135 BC, list parts of the hýsplex stored in the House of the Andrians, proof that it was dismantled out of season (Valavanis, 1999, pp. 31-32; Miller, 2004b, nos. 21-24); Lucian writes that, as soon as the hýsplex falls, the good runner thinks only of what lies in front of him; and an epigram mocks a certain Pericles, who still had not moved a finger when the crash of the falling hýsplex was already heard and another was receiving the crown (Miller, 2004b, nos. 21-24). An inscription from Epidaurus fined the Corinthian engineer Philon and his guarantor, the banker Nikon, 500 drachmas for failing to complete the installation of the mechanism on time, in the first half of the third century BC (Valavanis, 1999, p. 50), and another from the Acropolis, of the second century BC, mentions repairs to the starting gates of the Panathenaic stadium (p. 51).
>
> In July 1993 Miller and his team operated a full-size model at Nemea, with one cord at waist height and another at knee height, as the amphora shows, and found that releasing the two cords simultaneously required strength and practice, since in the first trial none of the four improvised judges managed to make the two posts fall together at the first attempt (Valavanis, 1999, p. 159). The word hýsplex designates the entire mechanism or the cord or barrier that formed part of it (Miller, 2004b, glossary, s. v. "hysplex").
>
> The hýsplex is at once an invention of war put at the service of spectacle and a public work that was contracted, paid for and fined like any other.

**`Fuente`**

> Valavanis, P. (1999). *Hysplex: The starting mechanism in ancient stadia. A contribution to ancient Greek technology*. University of California Press.

### Diapositiva 74 · The diaulos

**`Notas del orador`**

> Romano (2021, p. 213) defines the diaulos as a race of two lengths of the stadium, 1,200 feet, introduced at Olympia in 724 BC, with Hypenus of Pisa as the first victor, and explains the name from the double flute, whose two almost parallel reeds suggested the course: start, turn around a post at the opposite end and return. With a stadium of 192.28 metres the distance would be 384.56, and Yalouris (1979, p. 166) gives a range of 355 to 385 metres depending on the stadium.
>
> The turn is the central technical question and it is not settled. Romano (2021, pp. 213-214) sets out two theories, a common central post or one post per runner, and admits that the solution may have varied from one festival to another. Yalouris (1979, p. 164) records four and considers the most probable that each runner had two lanes, that is, that only one position in every two was occupied at the start. Golden (2004, p. 93, s.v. kampter) is categorical: in the short races, diaulos and hoplite race, each runner turned at his own post, and in the long ones all used the same; in his entry on the diaulos he specifies that the lanes were marked with lime or gypsum and that the turn was anticlockwise (p. 51). Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) describes the same system of alternate lanes. The posts were of wood and were fitted into stone blocks, and were therefore movable (Golden, 2004, p. 93). Miller (2003, p. 23) describes the same system at a Games of 300 BC: the runners took their places in alternate lanes, ran down one, turned around their own post and returned to the starting line. Valavanis (1999, p. 30) suggests that the wooden posts of the balbis may have served at once to mark each runner's position, as the turning post in the diaulos and in the race in armour, and as a support for cords marking the lane throughout the race.
>
> The course explains the topography: the diaulos started from the line where the stadium ended, the western end at Olympia, so that both races finished in the same place (Yalouris, 1979, p. 168; Miller, 2014, p. 292). Philostratus asks of the diaulos runner more strength than of the stadion runner and less weight than of the hoplite runner (On Gymnastics 33, in Miller, 2004b, no. 20).
>
> The technical term for the post is kampter (Miller, 2004b, glossary, s.v. 'kampter'; Golden, 2004, p. 93). The prohibition on leaning on the post with the hands, which the bibliography records, Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) refers to the dolichos.

### Diapositiva 75 · The dolichos

**`Notas del orador`**

> Romano (2021, p. 214) dates the dolichos to 720 BC, with Akanthos of Sparta as the first victor, and explains that the number of lengths, from seven to twenty-four, varied from one place to another, perhaps because the length of the foot also varied and twenty lengths of one stadium were equivalent to twenty-four of another, or perhaps through local custom. The figures for Olympia do not agree. Younger (2021, p. 47) gives 18 to 24 stadia; Kyle (2015, p. 115), 20 or 24 lengths; Waterfield (2018, ch. 5), twelve laps or twenty-four lengths, a little under three miles; Yalouris (1979, p. 168), 7 to 24 stadia and in most cases 20, some 3,550 to 3,850 metres; and Miller (2004b, glossary, s.v. 'dolichos') warns that the absolute length is uncertain even at Olympia, although he leans towards twenty-four lengths. Golden (2004, p. 55) and Yalouris (1979, p. 168) give seven lengths as the lower end of a range of distances attested at different sites. Miller (2003, p. 22) considers that the evidence, without being secure, favours twenty-four lengths, some 4,200 to 4,500 metres at Olympia.
>
> The turn around a single central post, anticlockwise, and the prohibition on using the hands to push oneself off are from Waterfield (2018, ch. 5), and Golden (2004, p. 93) confirms that in the long races all turned at the same post. The dolichos opened the morning of the third day, before the stadion and the diaulos (Waterfield, 2018, ch. 5). Yalouris (1979, p. 168) describes the style shown on vases: upright trunk, arms bent at waist height, clenched fists and a short stride, with a change to the sprinter's style in the final metres. Philostratus asks of the dolichos runner a strong neck and shoulders, like the pentathlete, and light legs, like the sprinter (On Gymnastics 32, in Miller, 2004b, no. 20). Miller (2003, pp. 21-22) also places the dolichos at the beginning of the day of the races and describes what followed each victory: the victor received a ribbon on his head and a palm branch, and the herald proclaimed his name and he took a lap of the track while the public threw flowers at him, a custom called phyllobolia. Valavanis (1999, p. 48, n. 138) leaves open whether the hysplex was used in the dolichos, and notes that, even if it was, the turn was far from the start and the runners did not get tangled in the fallen cords.
>
> Philostratus (On Gymnastics 4) relates the event to the hemerodromoi, the professional messengers, almost all Arcadians, who carried news over great distances in time of war, and Romano (2021, p. 214) and Yalouris (1979, p. 168) record it, the latter considering it somewhat forced but not impossible. An epigram from Epidaurus, perhaps of c. 320 BC, preserves the voice of Drymos of Argos, who announced his Olympic victory at Epidaurus on the same day, after running some 225 kilometres; Miller supposes that his victory was in the dolichos (Miller, 2004b, no. 27). The dolichos preserves the memory of the body at the service of the community at war, that of the messenger who runs for his city.
>

### Diapositiva 76 · The hoplite race

**`Notas del orador`**

> The race in armour entered Olympia in 520 BC, with Damaretos of Heraia as the first victor (Romano, 2021, p. 214). The runners wore a helmet, a shield and, at first, greaves, and never a spear. The distance was two lengths at Olympia and at Athens and four at Nemea, and Pausanias (5.12.8) relates that twenty-five bronze shields were kept in the temple of Zeus for the event. Pausanias himself (6.10.4) describes the statue of Damaretos with shield, helmet and greaves, and adds that with the years the Eleans and the Greeks abolished the helmet and the greaves (Miller, 2004b, no. 26). Miller (2003, p. 28) specifies that the greaves had already been abandoned before 300 BC and that the shield, on the left arm, was always obligatory. Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) estimates for the military shield a diameter of one metre and a weight of between 6 and 8 kilos, and explains that at Olympia shields made for the event were used, all of the same weight. It was probably the last event of the day and of the Games (Miller, 2003, p. 28).
>
> Golden (2004, pp. 84-85) stresses that it was the least uniform event of Greek athletics: two lengths at Olympia, four at Nemea and fifteen stadia at the Eleutheria of Plataea. It entered Delphi in 498 BC and was everywhere the last athletic event for men; boys did not run it, although it formed part of the military instruction of the ephebes in Hellenistic Athens, and Plato held that only races in armour deserved a prize (Laws 8.833). The Eleutheria were founded in memory of those who fell against the Persians in 479 BC; their race, run on the anniversary of the battle from the trophy to the altar of Zeus, was the longest and the hardest, and its victor received the title 'the best of the Greeks' (Golden, 2004, p. 58). Mnesibulos of Elateia, victor in the stadion and the hoplite race at Olympia in AD 161 and in that of Plataea, died fighting against the Costoboci (Golden, 2004, p. 104).
>
> The meaning of the event is discussed with authors and arguments. Pausanias (5.8.10) supposed that it was instituted as military training; modern historiography proposes that it recalls an ancient link between athletics and war or that it reflects changes in hoplite tactics to gain mobility against light infantry, that is, against the Persians (Kyle, 2015, pp. 115-117); and Philostratus (On Gymnastics 7) speculates that, being the last event, it signalled the end of the truce and the return to a state of potential war, and further attributes to it an origin in the growing hostilities during the festivals (Romano, 2021, p. 215). Miller (2003, pp. 28-29) is the most sceptical: the event arrived a century after the rest of the naked competitions, which for him refutes the claim that Olympic athletics was military preparation; it lacked offensive weapons, the infantry depended on the coordination of the group and not on individual action, and hardly any athletes won both the diaulos and the race in armour, a sign of a technique of its own. Pritchard (2013, p. 157) qualifies that reading, since it was the only event that rehearsed what hoplites really did, who ran when the phalanxes were some fifty metres apart and also, although nobody would admit it, when fleeing; at Athens it was called hoplites and entered the Great Panathenaia at the end of the sixth or the beginning of the fifth century BC.
>
> The link with citizenship has two readings. Christesen (2012b, p. 137) recalls that the phalanx appears at the end of the eighth and the beginning of the seventh century BC, and that, since each soldier paid for his own equipment, which cost between 75 and 100 drachmas, the wage of two or three months of a skilled craftsman, it was formed by plousioi and penetes, the rich and those of middling fortune, and not the ptochoi, the poor. In fifth-century BC Athens cavalrymen, hoplites and archers contributed to the sanctuary of Apollo Lykeios, in the gymnasium of the Lyceum, and Christesen concludes that the privileged, the hoplites and those who took part in sport regularly were largely the same men (p. 158); he adds that the hoplite entered combat with some thirty kilos of arms, that the shield weighed about eight, that outside Sparta military instruction was minimal and that sport kept the soldier's physical fitness (p. 159), and for the debate he refers to Poliakoff and to Reed (p. 159, n. 47). Pritchard (2003, p. 301; 2013, p. 192) maintains, by contrast, that in Athens democracy transformed the social composition of the army and the fleet, with a hoplite army under public control and military pay, but never that of the athletes, who continued to come from an elite of fewer than 5% of the citizens, and specifies that Athenian hoplites received no military training until the middle of the 330s BC (Pritchard, 2013, p. 208). What united the two was language, since combat and competition were described with the same words, agon, ponoi and kindynoi, toils and dangers that put areté to the test (Pritchard, 2013, p. 164).
>
> Despite that disagreement, the event brought to the stadium a common figure, that of the citizen soldier, and Pritchard (2013, p. 164) explains that the ordinary citizen projected onto the athlete the same admiration that he felt for the hoplite, even though he himself could never have afforded a paidotribes.
>
> Miller (2003, p. 28, fig. 26) reproduces a Panathenaic amphora in the Louvre with this event, MN 704, which could be the same one. The hoplite race is the event in which training for war and the marker of citizenship coincide, since the body that runs with a shield is that of the citizen who can afford it.

> Christesen, P. (2012b). *Sport and democracy in the ancient and modern worlds*. Cambridge University Press.

### Diapositiva 78 · The pentathlon

**`Notas del orador`**

> Younger (2021, p. 47) gives 708 BC, in the eighteenth Olympiad, for wrestling and the pentathlon in the same edition, Sweet (1987, p. 236) records the passage of Pausanias (5.8.7) from which it derives, and Romano (2021, p. 215) adds the name of the first victor, Lampis of Sparta. The boys' pentathlon was introduced in 628 BC, with a victory by Eutelidas of Sparta, and was abolished shortly afterwards (Romano, 2021, p. 215); Golden (2004, p. 130) specifies that it was a boys' event only once. Kyle (2014d, p. 240, n. 36) records Harris's conjecture that it was abolished for lack of interest on the part of the public. Minetti & Ardigò (2002, p. 141), following Ebert, place in that same Olympiad of 708 BC the first use of halteres in the jump.
>
> The structural fact is from Kyle (2015, p. 117): running and wrestling existed as independent events, but the jump, the discus and the javelin were held at Olympia only as part of the pentathlon. There are two readings of the origin. Romano (2021, p. 215) relates it to military preparation in general and recalls that the games of the Phaeacians in the Odyssey (8.100-103) bring together five events, with boxing in the place of the jump. Kyle (2015, p. 117) maintains that Olympia created the composite event to measure excellence in the discus, the jump and the javelin, and that running and wrestling were added to complete the set and help decide an overall winner. Indeed, in 708 BC the programme had only the races and the newly introduced wrestling, and from that repertoire came the two events that were added (Kyle, 2014d, p. 239). The account of Philostratus (On Gymnastics 3), according to which Jason joined the five events at Lemnos so that Peleus might win, is for Kyle (2014d, pp. 231-232) a late aetiology without value for reconstructing the competition. Miller (2003, p. 15) confirms that the discus, the jump and the javelin existed only as parts of the pentathlon, and gives for the competition the order stadion, discus, jump, javelin and wrestling, different from that proposed by Golden (2004, p. 130), discus, jump, javelin, stadion and wrestling; Sweet (1987, p. 38) warns that there is no evidence on the order of the first three.
>
> Kyle (2015, p. 118) further stresses that the whole pentathlon was settled in one afternoon, with only three judges. The image is the black-figure Panathenaic amphora with four athletes that Kyle (2015, p. 117, fig. 6.4) reproduces, attributed to the Euphiletos Painter and dated c. 530-520 BC. Miller (2003, p. 15) places the pentathlon in the stadium on the same day as the equestrian events, just after them, and presents it as the first of the gymnikoi agones.

### Diapositiva 79 · How was the pentathlon won?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The three-victories rule is the only one that the bibliography holds to be secure. Miller (2004b, glossary, s.v. 'pentathlon', p. 226) writes that, if an athlete won three of the five events, he was declared the victor, and Romano (2021, p. 217) states that practically all specialists agree on this. Kyle (2015, p. 118) draws the consequence: the competition ended as soon as someone reached three victories, and for that reason the three events peculiar to the pentathlon were probably contested first. The testimony of Xenophon (Hellenica 7.4.29) refers to the Games of 364 BC, when the Eleans attacked the Arcadians who had seized the sanctuary: those who had moved on to wrestling were no longer in the stadium, and were fighting between the stadium and the altar (Sweet, 1987, p. 38). Golden (2004, p. 130) proposes the order discus, jump, javelin, stadion and wrestling, and Sweet (1987, p. 38) warns that there is no evidence on the order of the first three. Miller (2003, p. 15) places, by contrast, the race first, before the discus, the jump and the javelin.
>
> Miller (2003, p. 17) summarises what can be asserted: it is not known how the victor was decided, but it is certain that, if an athlete had won three events before the wrestling, the latter was no longer held, and it seems clear that the course of the event allowed some form of elimination that left out of the final wrestling those who could no longer win. The victor received the ribbon and the palm branch there and then, as in all events.
>
> The episode of 364 BC also has a political reading. That year the Arcadians presided over the Games, and the Eleans attacked the sanctuary in the middle of the pentathlon and even fought inside the Altis (Crowther, 2003, pp. 61-62; Golden, 2008, p. 137). Golden adduces it as proof that the truce guaranteed only safe passage to the sanctuary, which connects with what we saw about Elis and its control of Olympia.
>
> The case of Tisamenos is transmitted by Herodotus (9.33) and Pausanias (3.11.6), and is placed c. 500 BC (Miller, 2004b, no. 50, pp. 42-43). An oracle had announced to him that he would win five very notable contests; he trained for the pentathlon at Olympia, defeated Hieronymos of Andros in the race and in the jump, lost the wrestling to him and then understood that the oracle spoke of five military victories. Two victories were not enough.
>
> The reconstructions have authors. Harris (1972, pp. 34-35, cited in Kyle, 2015, p. 118) proposes a system of elimination in which only the top places counted: after the first three events, whoever had won them all was the victor; if not, the winners went on to the race and, if necessary, to the wrestling. Kyle (1990) adapts Harris with an added race as a semi-final; Lee (1995) proposes a hybrid system of eliminations and relative places; Egan (2007) emends the text of Philostratus (Kyle, 2015, p. 118). Sweet (1987) has everyone compete in the first four events and resorts to the lot, a solution that Kyle (2014d, pp. 236-239) considers too complicated. Romano (2021, p. 217) inclines towards a simple system, close to that of Harris.
>
> Some Spanish handbooks record the system of Gillet (1971): everyone jumped, the first five threw the javelin, the first four ran the stadion, the three finalists threw the discus and the winner of the wrestling was the victor of the pentathlon. The work, Historia del deporte (Oikos-tau, 1971), is not in the library, and the system clashes with the only secure rule, since it makes the title depend on the wrestling and not on the three victories.
>
> Kyle (2014d, pp. 229 & 233) maintains that the problem of the scoring is a modern one: the Greeks left no record of disputes over the winner, the procedure was fixed c. 708 BC, when writing was barely beginning to spread, and great literature did not concern itself with regulations that everybody knew. Golden (2004, p. 130) puts it differently: modern ignorance is the product of the Greeks' familiarity with the procedure. Kyle (2014d, p. 240) adds that the system had to be intelligible to thousands of spectators without scoreboards or programmes.
>
> The rule of the pentathlon was devised for an audience that had to follow the event with the naked eye, and the modern obsession with the exact regulation says more about our sport than about the Greek.

**`Fuente`**

> Kyle, D. G. (2014d). Winning and watching the Greek pentathlon. In T. F. Scanlon (Ed.), *Sport in the Greek and Roman worlds: Vol. 1. Early Greece, the Olympics, and contests* (pp. 228-248). Oxford University Press.
>
> Crowther, N. B. (2003). Elis and Olympia: City, sanctuary and politics. In D. J. Phillips & D. Pritchard (Eds.), *Sport and festival in the ancient Greek world* (pp. 61-74). The Classical Press of Wales.

### Diapositiva 81 · The jump

**`Notas del orador`**

> The weight range of the halteres is wide: 1.4 to 4.5 kg in Kyle (2015, p. 118), 1 to 4.5 kg in Romano (2021, p. 215), who follows Gardiner, and 2 to 4.5 kg in Spivey (2005, p. 95); Miller (2004b, glossary, s. v. "halter", p. 218) speaks of about five pounds. Minetti & Ardigò (2002, p. 141, fig. 1) reproduce pieces from the British Museum, the National Museum of Athens and the Museum of Olympia weighing between 1.1 and 4.5 kg. Miller (2003, p. 16) stresses that, unlike the discus, halteres were personal and had no standard weight, and he cites an Olympia example of 4.6 kg dedicated to Zeus by its owner after his victory. Pausanias (5.26.3) describes their shape, an open oval through which the fingers passed as through the handle of a shield (Miller, 2004b, no. 58, pp. 45-46). The haltere of Epainetos is made of lead, weighs 2.199 kg and Moretti dates it to c. 580-570 BC (Sweet, 1987, p. 47). At Nemea, moreover, a deposit of the second half of the sixth century BC came to light containing a lead haltere, two javelin heads, an iron discus and a strigil, probably the offering of a pentathlete (Romano, 2021, p. 215).
>
> The technique is described by Kyle (2015, p. 118) and Miller (2003, p. 16) on the basis of the vases: the jumper ran up to the bater carrying the weights, swung them forward at take-off, threw them back as he began to descend and released them on touching the pit. Minetti & Ardigò (2002, p. 141) reconstruct the same sequence from vase paintings. Romano (2021, p. 216) qualifies this by noting that there is no firm proof that he did not hold on to them until landing, and Crowther (2007, p. 63) recalls that, according to Philostratus, the jump was measured only if the imprint of the landing was perfect, which suggests that he did not let go of them. Aristotle (Progression of Animals 705a) states that pentathletes who hold halteres jump further than those who do not carry them (Miller, 2004b, no. 57, p. 45).
>
> The two experiments do not contradict each other, although their figures appear to. Sweet (1987, p. 48) summarises that of Ebert, with weights of 2.5 kg, which reduced the running jump by more than a metre and lengthened the standing jump by between fifteen and twenty centimetres. Minetti & Ardigò (2002, pp. 141-142) do not repeat that trial, since from Ebert they take only the date of 708 BC and a take-off angle of about 50°, and they start from the jump without a run-up; with a computer model and a test with four subjects they calculate that a pair of halteres of 2 to 9 kg in total, with an optimum of 5 to 6 kg, lengthens a 3-metre jump by at least 17 centimetres, and that performance worsens only when the load exceeds 10 to 12 kg. Both therefore agree on a gain of some 15 to 20 centimetres in the standing jump, and Minetti & Ardigò conclude that the ancient athletes had arrived by trial and error at the same optimum that biomechanics calculates today (p. 142). Ebert's negative result with a run-up, by contrast, clashes with the vases that show the preliminary run (Kyle, 2015, p. 118; Crowther, 2007, p. 63), and the question remains open.
>
> The flute had a function. Philostratus (On Gymnastics 55) explains that the regulations considered the jump the most difficult event and therefore allowed the jumper to be helped by the rhythm of the flute and by the weight of the halteres (Miller, 2004b, no. 47, pp. 39-40), and Pausanias (5.7.10) attributes the Pythian music of the jump to Apollo (Miller, 2004b, no. 59, p. 46). Miller (2003, p. 16) adds that the flautist at Olympia was the winner of the flute contest at Delphi, and that the coordination and rhythm required by the jump explain his role. The bater is the take-off point and the skamma, "the dug-up place", also designates the broken ground of wrestling (Miller, 2004b, glossary, pp. 213 and 230). The jump was measured with a rod, the kanon (Sweet, 1987, p. 46; Romano, 2021, p. 216). Romano (2021, p. 213) recalls that times were not recorded and distances hardly at all, and that athletes were compared by results.
>
> The five consecutive jumps are one of the hypotheses put forward to explain the jumps of more than 50 feet attributed to Phayllos and Chionis, the latter in 664 BC (Sweet, 1987, pp. 48-49). Golden (2004, p. 91) and Crowther (2007, p. 63) mention the series of five standing jumps, and Crowther adds that vase paintings do not support the jump without a run-up; Spivey (2005, p. 95) considers a multiple jump plausible, perhaps of five impulses. Sweet (1987, p. 50) recalls that most specialists prefer some form of multiple jump, but that there is no solid ancient evidence, literary or figurative, and that the only testimony, that of Themistius, is difficult to read. Romano (2021, p. 216) adds that it is not known how many jumps were allowed. Golden (2004, p. 91) thinks it more likely that the epigram on Phayllos is a joke, and Crowther (2007, p. 64) sums up the current consensus: a long jump similar to the modern one, with weights and music, and distances that are humorous exaggerations. Phayllos, incidentally, was three times a victor at Delphi and commanded his own ship at Salamis (Herodotus 8.47; Bertolín, 2020, p. 164).

**`Fuente`**

> Minetti, A. E., & Ardigò, L. P. (2002). Halteres used in ancient Olympic long jump. *Nature*, *420*(6912), 141-142.
>
> Sweet, W. E. (1987). *Sport and recreation in ancient Greece: A sourcebook with translations*. Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 82 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> Vase paintings are the main source for the gesture of the jump, and they show the preliminary run (Kyle, 2015, p. 118), the pick with which athletes broke up the pit and the measuring rod (Sweet, 1987, p. 46). Romano (2021, p. 216) warns that some images and bronze statuettes show jumps without a run, which may have been a training exercise. The kylix by Douris with the flute player is reproduced by Sansone (1988, fig. 22), and Miller (2003, p. 16, fig. 11) reproduces another red-figure kylix, from the Antikenmuseum Basel (KÄ 425), with a pentathlete who jumps to the sound of the flute. The captions of images that are vases must indicate painter, museum and date.

### Diapositiva 83 · The javelin

**`Notas del orador`**

> The elder shaft is given by Kyle (2015, p. 118) and Spivey (2005, p. 97), and it is already mentioned by Bacchylides when he sings of the victory of Automedon in the pentathlon (Sweet, 1987, p. 52). Golden (2004, p. 91) gives a length of five or six feet, with a metal point for sticking into the ground, and Sweet (1987, p. 53) about six feet, as thick as a finger. Crowther (2007, p. 66) warns that no ancient javelin has survived and that, judging by the art, some were somewhat taller than the athlete and others somewhat shorter. The throw from the balbis, a rectangle open at the back to allow the run-up, comes from Crowther (2007, pp. 66-67); Miller (2004b, glossary, p. 213) defines the balbis as the area marked off for throwers and, by extension, the starting line of the runners. Miller (2003, p. 16) gives it a length of about two metres, a shaft thinner than the heavy hunting or war spear, the doru, and a pyramidal bronze point instead of a cutting blade.
>
> The number of attempts is not known (Sweet, 1987, p. 55), and the figure of three given by some popular guides has no ancient support. The length of the thong, from one foot to a foot and a half, comes from Sweet (1987, p. 53), and Crowther (2007, p. 67) puts it at approximately one foot; Miller (2004b, glossary, s. v. "ankyle", p. 211) describes a strip of rawhide of some six feet that was folded before being wound on. The spinning effect, which improved both distance and accuracy, comes from Kyle (2015, p. 118), and a scholium on Euripides calls this javelin a mesankylon (Sweet, 1987, p. 53). According to Gardiner, cited in Sweet (1987, p. 53), Napoleon III found that untrained men improved from 25 to 65 metres with the thong. Golden (2004, p. 91) estimates that champions reached some 300 feet, and Crowther (2007, p. 67) considers that claim debatable. The throw also carried risks: a speech by Antiphon deals with a boy killed by a javelin in the gymnasium (Golden, 2004, p. 91). Miller (2003, pp. 16-17) explains that the thong left a loop for the first two fingers, acted like a sling and, as it unwound, made the javelin spin like a rifle bullet; throws were marked with pegs, as in the discus and the jump, and the winner was decided by distance, since accuracy, despite what some modern authors have maintained, was not a criterion.
>
> The javelin enjoyed prestige among commanders. In the Iliad, Achilles gives the javelin prize to Agamemnon in recognition of his rank as leader of the army (Golden, 2004, p. 91). At the Panathenaia the ephebes threw from horseback at a target, an event that appears among the prizes at the end of the fifth century BC and that Xenophon recommends for the cavalry (Kyle, 2015, p. 156). Crowther (2007, p. 66) recalls that the javelin was used in hunting and in war, often from horseback. In the pentathlon, by contrast, the throw was removed from the battlefield: there was no target, hoplites did not hurl javelins, and the competition javelin was lightened so that it would fly further (Spivey, 2005, pp. 95-97). Kyle (2015, p. 81) concludes that events such as the javelin imitated war, but athletics was, at most, an indirect form of military training. Pritchard (2013, p. 157) makes the contrast precise: the pentathlete sought the greatest distance and not a target, whereas the hoplite used the heavier doru, which in the pitched battles of the Classical period he did not throw, since with it he wounded the enemy in hand-to-hand fighting.
>
> Sansone (1988, pp. 10-11) uses the javelin precisely to discuss the theory that explains sport as training for hunting and war, since that theory does not clarify why people continue to throw in societies that no longer hunt or fight with spears, and he proposes reading its practice as a rite that is repeated because the previous generation also did it (p. 26).
>
> The caption is generic and must be completed with the object, the museum and the date of the piece that is projected. The pentathlon javelin shows training for war transformed into something else, since the weapon of the horseman and the hunter becomes a contest of pure distance, with rules, a line and judges.

>
> Spivey, N. (2005). *The ancient Olympics: A history*. Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 84 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> Many vase paintings show the thong and the athletes fitting it to the javelin (Romano, 2021, p. 216). Spivey (2005, p. 97) describes the gesture: the shaft was held between the index and middle fingers, with the loop on one or both fingers, and the athlete took a few steps, extended his arm and released the javelin on an ascending trajectory; the spin of the thong as it unwound kept it in the air for longer. The thong was not tied to the shaft and fell to the ground after the throw (Kyle, 2015, p. 118; Miller, 2004b, glossary, p. 211). The Latin term was amentum. Miller (2003, p. 17, fig. 13) reproduces a red-figure kylix in the J. Paul Getty Museum (85.AE.25) that shows the complete sequence: the athlete fits the thong with his fingers in the loop, begins the run, turns his trunk 180 degrees and brings his arm forward while untwisting it towards the throw. Miller adds that this technique has been used successfully, though rarely, in modern times.

### Diapositiva 85 · The discus

**`Notas del orador`**

> The term for the pentathlon event is diskos, which originally designated a weight of stone or metal of no definite shape (Miller, 2004b, glossary, p. 216). The ancient scholiasts distinguished the stone diskos from the solos of solid metal, and that distinction led to errors that the Liddell and Scott dictionary carried along for decades (Sweet, 1987, p. 42); Yalouris (1979, p. 191) identifies the solos with a weight similar to the modern shot.
>
> The measurements come from Kyle (2015, p. 117): 17 to 35 cm and 1.5 to 6.5 kg, with an average of 2.5 kg, in bronze for those in use and in stone for votive offerings. Romano (2021, pp. 216-217) gives 1.3 to 5.7 kg and 0.17 to 0.35 m, and warns that some may have been votive; Sweet (1987, p. 40) counts eight discuses found at Olympia, and Spivey (2005, p. 92) mentions an iron discus from Nemea of more than 8 kg. The three discuses of the Treasury of the Sicyonians come from Pausanias (6.19.4; Miller, 2004b, no. 56, p. 45), and Sweet (1987, p. 43) interprets them as a guarantee of equality. Spivey (2005, p. 92) records another custom, that of throwing with the largest discus that any of the athletes had brought. Miller (2003, p. 15) takes as characteristic examples discuses of about 2 kg and about 20 cm in diameter, and explains that official discuses were kept at Olympia so that all the athletes would throw the same one on the same day.
>
> The technique remains open. Philostratus, in the Imagines (1.24), places the thrower on a small balbis, with his head turned to the right and the whole right side thrown into the throw (Miller, 2004b, no. 55, pp. 44-45; Sweet, 1987, p. 41). Kyle (2015, p. 117) proposes an upward throw from below with the right arm while the left leg advanced, without a full turn, and Spivey (2005, p. 92) a throw with the feet fixed, bending the knees and twisting the trunk. Crowther (2007, pp. 65-66) relates that the organisers of 1896 imposed a platform and a movement without a turn in imitation of the Discobolus and through misreading Philostratus, and that most specialists admit some turning movement. The number of attempts is unknown (Sweet, 1987, p. 45; Romano, 2021, p. 217). A fragmentary inscription from Rhodes, perhaps of the first century BC, seems to provide for five throws per athlete, but the word discus is restored (Miller, 2004b, no. 52, p. 43). The landing point was marked with a peg (Sweet, 1987, p. 42; Spivey, 2005, p. 92). Miller (2003, p. 15) describes, by contrast, a throw with a turn that began crouching and with the back to the field and ended with the body extended towards the throw, which had to fall within certain limits, perhaps the stone channels at the edge of the track, and he records indications of five attempts per athlete, of which the best counted.
>
> The Lancellotti Discobolus is a marble copy of c. AD 140 of a bronze by Myron of c. 460-450 BC, found on the Esquiline in 1781 (Spivey, 2005, p. 94). Pentathletes served as models for sculptors because of their beauty (Crowther, 2007, p. 63), and the Discobolus shows how the body of the pentathlete passed from competition to art and became a model of male beauty.
>
> Miller (2004a, pp. 60-63) explains that we do not know whether discus throwers merely twisted their bodies or also made a half-turn before releasing the discus, like present-day throwers, and that there may have been three throws at Olympia, although an inscription from Rhodes allows five (SEG 15.501), so that the rules could vary from one place to another.

### Diapositiva 86 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> The 255th Olympiad falls in AD 241, and Yalouris & Yalouris (1995, p. 174) state it in the catalogue entry for the object. The calculation is simple: the first Olympiad is that of 776 BC, and the 255th falls 1,016 years later. Yalouris (1979, p. 295) includes Publius Asclepiades of Corinth in the list of victors under Olympiad 255, year 241, and reproduces the discus (p. 191, fig. 93). He should not be confused with Marcus Aurelius Asclepiades of Alexandria, a pankratiast and Olympic victor who appears in the same list (Kyle, 2015, p. 320).
>
> The discus is inscribed on both faces (Yalouris & Yalouris, 1995, p. 174). The first is the offering of thanks to Olympian Zeus by Publius Asclepiades, winner of the pentathlon in the 255th Olympiad. The second is the confirmation by the official who received the offering, the alytarch or chief of police Flavius Scribonianus, who presents himself as a relative of senators and consuls and dates the dedication to the 456th Olympiad. The difference is explained by two ways of counting: the second inscription takes 1580 BC as its starting point and not 776 BC, a feature that the authors relate to the fascination of the period with a remote past in which the Games were supposedly founded. Miller (2004a, fig. 106) gives an exact weight of 5.707 kg and the inventory number M 891.
>
> By its weight and its diameter, at the upper end of Kyle's range (2015, p. 117), and by its inscriptions, it is an offering; Sweet (1987, p. 40) recalls that some bronze discuses appear to be votive and not for throwing. The piece brings together in a single object the rite of one who thanks Zeus for victory and the distinction of the official who takes advantage of another's offering to display his kinship with the Roman aristocracy.

### Diapositiva 87 · Combat sports

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Greeks called gymnikoi agones, "naked contests", the events held without clothing, the pentathlon, the races, wrestling, boxing and the pankration, as opposed to the equestrian ones (Miller, 2003, p. 15). Within the gymnic events they separated the heavy ones, wrestling, boxing and the pankration, from the light ones, running, jumping, javelin and discus, and the criterion was the weight of the athlete, since without categories the bulkiest had the advantage (Waterfield, 2018, ch. 5). Kyle (2015, p. 119) records the name barea athla with a "probably", and adds two details that ordered the tournament: in odd-numbered draws an athlete could be exempted by lot, as an ephedros, and reach the next round rested, and the judge punished with the rod fouls, holding to stop the contest and any sign of passivity.
>
> The dates of entry at Olympia are those of Younger (2021, p. 47) and Poliakoff (2021, p. 222), who adds those of the boys, 632, 616 and 200 BC respectively. Poliakoff stresses that the Greeks saw the inequality between boys and men and created age categories, although they never wished to protect the lightest, and that the absence of time limits, of pauses and of water, under the summer sun, brought the event closer to the conditions of war (pp. 222-223). Miller (2003, p. 10) confirms this from the organisation of the Games, in which there were only age categories, men from the age of twenty and boys from about seventeen to nineteen, and age was verified on the very day of the contest by examining the athlete, his father, his brothers and his trainer, with known frauds. The same author recalls that in boxing and the pankration, with no weight limits or rounds, deaths occurred (pp. 26-27). Cicero even wrote that a novice boxer bore the blows of Olympia better than the heat (Brutus 69).
>
> The surrender with the index finger raised is documented by Kyle (2015, p. 119) and Poliakoff (1987, p. 80), who sees it in vase painting; Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) speaks of the middle finger of the right hand, and his version stands alone. That all the competitions were held in the stadium, and not in the palaestra, comes from Poliakoff (1987, p. 20). The skamma also designated the landing pit of the jump, and in the pankration the attendants soaked it until it became mud (Faulkner, 2012, ch. 7).
>

### Diapositiva 88 · Wrestling

**`Notas del orador`**

> Men's wrestling entered at the 18th Olympiad, in 708 BC, together with the pentathlon (Younger, 2021, p. 47; Poliakoff, 2021, p. 222), and 632 BC is the year in which the stadion race and the boys' wrestling were incorporated, a date that some manuals wrongly attribute to adult wrestling.
>
> The system of falls is explained by Poliakoff (1987, p. 23): the normal aim was to throw the opponent, and the wrestler needed three falls, so that a bout could last five at most; the "third fall" came to be in Greek a metaphor for definitive defeat, and Plato uses it in the Euthydemus. Kyle (2015, p. 119) speaks of three falls out of five or surrender, and Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) specifies that touching the ground with the hip, the back or the shoulders counted as a fall. Aristophanes gives two tricks: smearing the neck with oil to prevent holds (Knights 490-491) and shaking the dust from the back to deny the fall (571-573), which shows that the judge read the throw on the wrestler's body (Kyle, 2015, p. 119).
>
> On the two modalities, Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) identifies the Olympic pale with standing wrestling and attributes ground wrestling to the pankration. Crowther (2007, p. 68) qualifies this by noting that Greek wrestling also included ground wrestling, less represented on vases, and that surrender by strangulation had its part, so that the boundary was not as sharp as the classical division between orthe pale and kato pale suggests.
>
> The sacred law of the Altis, of c. 525-500 BC (SEG 48.541), forbade breaking fingers and punished the offence with flogging (Kyle, 2015, p. 119). Poliakoff (2021, p. 223) maintains that for almost the whole of its history it was legal to break fingers, and he dates the victories of Leontiskos to 456 and 452 BC (Pausanias 6.4.3). Even so, wrestling was the safest of the three heavy events, and Greek society expected an educated man to practise it and to enjoy it as an adult (Poliakoff, 1987, p. 23).
>
> The only documented death in wrestling is recorded on the base of a statue at Delphi dedicated by Daochos c. 336-332 BC, on which Telemachos of Pharsalos declares that he killed "a very powerful man of the Tyrrhenians". Brophy & Brophy (1985, pp. 173-174) date it to the fifth century BC, between 488-484 and 440-436 BC, with a preference for the earlier date, and they accept the restoration of the text according to which the death was involuntary and the victor kept the crown; the accidental nature of the death therefore rests on that restoration. The same authors conclude that killing an opponent without foul play did not deprive one of the crown either in the fifth century BC or in the second century AD, and that it was probably always so, given the well-known normative immobility of Olympia (p. 198).

### Diapositiva 89 · Boxing

**`Notas del orador`**

> Onomastos of Smyrna and his set of rules of 688 BC are a tradition recorded by Poliakoff (1987, p. 80), with no surviving description. Kyle (2015, p. 119) describes the first straps as long, thin strips of oxhide that wrapped the hands and wrists leaving the upper phalanges free, the himantes meilichai, whose purpose was to protect the hands of the man striking. Later a longer glove appeared, with hard straps, the himantes oxeis, and a rim of thick leather over the back of the hand and the knuckles, and after that a woollen padding for the forearm. Poliakoff (2021, p. 223) places the sharp straps shortly after the middle of the fourth century BC and describes the rim as a cutting edge. The evolution was therefore towards more harmful gloves. Brophy (1978, p. 369) brings forward the spread of hard straps to the beginning of the fourth century BC and specifies that in the time of Kreugas, c. 400 BC, boxers still fought with soft straps; the difference in dates between the two authors remains open.
>
> On the caestus, Kyle (2015, p. 119) confines himself to recording that many authors believe it was reinforced with metal, and Poliakoff (2021, p. 223) asserts it with Virgil, who describes it as "stiff with lead and iron sewn in" (Aeneid 5.404-405). Poliakoff also adduces a coin of Philippopolis, from the time of Caracalla, showing a boxer with a caestus at a contest on the Pythian model, proof that the Greeks themselves admitted it on some occasion. Blows were directed above all at the head, and Greek art does not hide the results: the head of the boxer Satyros, a work by Silanion of the mid-fourth century BC found at Olympia, has a flattened nose and deformed ears (Kyle, 2015, p. 119).
>
> The klimax is the fundamental correction. Golden (2004, p. 94) gives two meanings: a portable object used to bring boxers into contact and a wrestling hold in which one athlete mounts the back of his opponent. Poliakoff (1987, pp. 79-80) cites an ancient lexicon according to which "a ladder was placed for boxers when they wasted time, so that they would remain in the same place", and he reproduces two vases of the sixth century BC in which a barrier or a long rod reduces the space of combat. Kyle (2015, p. 119) speaks of a barrier in the form of a ladder that delimited the ring and returned the boxers to the centre, and Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) describes it as a long rod that the judges held horizontally. Faulkner also mentions a tie-break of alternating blows, which he calls rare, without citing a source.
>
> The only ancient testimony of blows taken in turns is that of Pausanias (8.40.4-5, in Miller, 2004b, no. 38). At the Nemean Games evening fell and Kreugas of Epidamnos and Damoxenos of Syracuse agreed before witnesses to let themselves be struck in turns; Kreugas struck at the head, Damoxenos asked him to raise his arm and drove his extended fingers beneath the ribs until he tore out his entrails. The Argives, who were then presiding over the Nemean Games, expelled Damoxenos for having delivered several blows, one per finger, crowned the dead man and erected a statue to him in Argos (Kyle, 2015, pp. 139-140; Golden, 2004, p. 45; Miller, 2003, pp. 26-27). Kyle presents the agreement as a proposal of the judges, whereas Pausanias attributes it to the two boxers, and in any case it was an improvised pact at Nemea. Fernández Truan (vol. 4, p. 766) presents the klimax as the rule that decided a bout not resolved before sunset and places the case in the pankration, a fusion of a real object with a real episode that has passed into Spanish-language manuals.
>
> Brophy (1978) devotes an entire article to this death and to that of Arrhichion. He places the bout c. 400 BC (p. 363), recalls that Pausanias wrote between AD 160 and 180, almost six centuries later, which explains part of his technical errors (p. 365), and considers anatomically impossible the detail of the nails that pierce the belly, since no boxer could wear them so long and sharp without wounding his own hand (p. 383). His diagnosis is that Damoxenos struck with extended fingers beneath the ribs, a technique that he compares with blows of karate and taekwondo, and that Kreugas died from a ruptured spleen and the resulting internal haemorrhage (p. 386). Brophy & Brophy (1985, p. 172) add that the common factor in the three deaths that cost the perpetrator the crown, those of Kleomedes, Diognetos and Damoxenos, was the blow to the body, which in their view the unwritten rules of boxing prohibited, just as low blows are forbidden today; their thesis clashes with Poliakoff (1987, p. 85), for whom nothing prevented striking the genitals with the fist, so that the question remains open.

**`Fuente`**

> Poliakoff, M. B. (1987). *Combat sports in the ancient world: Competition, violence, and culture*. Yale University Press.
>
> Golden, M. (2004). *Sport in the ancient world from A to Z*. Routledge.

### Diapositiva 91 · The pankration

**`Notas del orador`**

> Poliakoff (1987, p. 57), when treating the kick as a hallmark of the pankration, maintains by contrast that the genitals were not out of bounds, supports this with art of the sixth century BC and the second century AD, and in the chapter on boxing adds that nothing prevented striking them with the fist (p. 85); Kyle (2015, p. 120) and Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) agree with him. The prohibition on gouging the eyes was not always respected either: Epictetus counted having an eye put out among the risks of the trade, and a vase of c. 515-500 BC shows Heracles doing it to Antaeus (Poliakoff, 1987, p. 56).
>
> Philostratus' sentence on the Spartans is an ancient testimony to the difference between the practice that prepares for war and the contest of the sanctuary: the Lacedaemonians allowed biting and gouging the eyes, "I suppose because they train for battle", whereas the games of Elis forbade it although they admitted strangulation (Brophy, 1978, p. 372). The first Olympic victor was Lygdamis of Syracuse, of whom it was said that his feet measured a cubit (Faulkner, 2012, ch. 7). Sostratos of Sicyon, nicknamed "Fingers", won by bending his opponent's fingers until he surrendered, and he amassed twelve crowns at the Isthmus and Nemea, three at Olympia and two at Delphi (Pausanias 6.4.2, in Miller, 2004b, no. 46).
>
> Arrhichion has three ancient accounts. Pausanias (8.40.1-2, in Miller, 2004b, no. 44) is sober: with two previous Olympic victories, in the final of 564 BC his rival gripped him round the waist with his legs and squeezed his neck; Arrhichion dislocated one of his toes and died strangled, at the very moment when the other surrendered from the pain, and the Eleans crowned his corpse. Philostratus describes a painting and specifies the technique, with the rival's ankle wrenched out of its socket in the fall, and warns that it should not be thought that this was by chance, "for he planned his victory with great cunning". The third relates that Arrhichion was on the point of surrendering when his trainer shouted to him: "What a fine epitaph, not to have given up at Olympia!" (Poliakoff, 1987, p. 91). His statue, of archaic style, stood in the agora of Phigaleia and has not survived.
>
> Brophy (1978, p. 367) stresses that Arrhichion was seeking his third consecutive crown and maintains, with anatomical support, that he did not die of progressive strangulation, since the same abrupt twist with which he forced his rival's leg, with his head immobilised by the hold, fractured the odontoid process of the second cervical vertebra, the mechanism of death by hanging (p. 381). He also recalls that Pausanias wrote some seven and a half centuries after the events and Philostratus almost a century later, a distance that explains a good part of their technical errors (p. 365).
>
> The prestige of the pankration could also have political consequences. Dioxippus, an Athenian pancratiast who had won at Olympia and belonged to the entourage of Alexander, defeated an armed Macedonian in India, in 325 BC, in an exhibition combat, and the resentment this provoked led to his being falsely accused of theft and forced to suicide, since his feat was perceived as a dangerous display of Athenian superiority over the Macedonians (Buraselis, 2017, p. 140).
>
> Arrhichion condenses the education of character as the Greek agonistic culture understood it, since perseverance unto death was worth more than life and society rewarded it with a crown.
>
> Biting and putting fingers in the eyes were the only prohibitions of the pankration at Olympia, not its rules.

> Miller, S. G. (2004b). *Arete: Greek sports from ancient sources* (3rd ed.). University of California Press, no. 44-45.
>
> Brophy, R. H., III. (1978). Deaths in the Pan-Hellenic Games: Arrachion and Creugas. *The American Journal of Philology*, *99*(3), 363-390.

### Diapositiva 92 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> As we saw when studying the levels of management at Olympia, the Hellanodikai were the judges of the Games, magistrates of Elis, and a vase allows them to be identified only when an inscription or the context says so; in palaestra scenes the figure with the rod may be a judge or a trainer. The rod served to punish fouls, holds and passivity (Kyle, 2015, p. 119), and Faulkner (2012, ch. 7) adds the whip, which with the rod kept the contestants within the skamma. Miller (2003, p. 7) specifies that the usual punishment for fouls at the Games was flogging, applied by an official called the mastigophoros, and that judges were forbidden to strike the athlete's head.

It was they who expelled from the stadium Diognetos of Crete, who had killed his rival Herakles in the boxing at Olympia, probably in 488 BC, and denied him the crown, according to Brophy & Brophy (1985, pp. 183-184) because he had used a forbidden blow to the body and not because of the death itself.

### Diapositiva 94 · Equestrian events

**`Notas del orador`**

> Equestrian competition was the event of the nobility, and Nicholson (2021, pp. 242-244) develops the point. The horse was the sign of the elite par excellence: in several cities the wealthy were called hippeis, "knights", because they could serve in the cavalry; many aristocrats bore horse names, such as Hippokleas or Hipponikos, and Aristophanes mocks the mother who wants for her son a name with hippos against the peasant father (Clouds 63-65); an Athenian even asked on an ostracon for the banishment of Megacles and his horses. Equestrian events ranked above gymnic ones: at the Panathenaia of the early fourth century BC the winner of the stadion race received some 80 amphorae of oil and the winner of the two-horse chariot 140, and the temple of Zeus at Olympia, of c. 460 BC, represented as its founding moment the chariot race of Pelops and Oenomaus (pp. 243-244). Kings and tyrants, from Cleisthenes of Sicyon and Peisistratos to Hieron, tyrant of Syracuse, Philip of Macedon and the Ptolemies, concentrated almost all of their participation in equestrian events (p. 244).
>
> Kyle (2015, pp. 121-122) calls the four-horse chariot (tethrippon) the most expensive, prestigious and dangerous event, and proposes that it was aristocrats and tyrants who promoted the entry of the races in order to multiply the occasions for display, in an Elis famous for its horse breeding. Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) adds that the four-horse chariot opened the athletic programme on the second day, after a procession past the altars of the Altis, and compares it with Formula 1 for the pleasure of watching extremely expensive vehicles driven skilfully in dangerous conditions; the analogy is his own and modern.
>
> The expense was not limited to the horses. Preparing a great race seriously required a whole team of grooms, trainers and breakers in addition to the charioteer, and almost all of them were left out of the public commemoration (Nicholson, 2005, p. 40). The great Athenian families, such as the Alcmaeonids, the Philaids and the Kalliadai, used equestrian victory to display their wealth and also to engage in elite politics (Papakonstantinou, 2019, p. 47), and between 296 and 244 BC the Ptolemaic royal family won at least four, and probably five or six, of the fourteen documented Olympic four-horse chariot races, a proportion that not even the Sicilian tyrants had reached (Remijsen, 2009, p. 252).
>
> The rule that awarded the crown to the owner made it possible to win without travelling to Olympia, since the owner of the chariot did not need to be present to be proclaimed victor (Kyle, 2003, p. 185), and Argos even came to win as a city the horse race in 480 BC and the chariot race in 472 BC (Nicholson, 2021, p. 250).
>
> Equestrian competition is the marker of distinction in its pure state, since the crown rewarded the owner's wealth without demanding any physical effort from him.

### Diapositiva 97 · (cont.)


**`Notas del orador`**

> Pausanias (6.20.10-14, in Miller, 2004b, no. 69) describes the start with a detail unusual in him. It was shaped like the prow of a ship with its point towards the track, each side measured more than 400 feet and housed stalls that were allotted by lot, and a cord was stretched in front of each chariot. In the centre of the prow an adobe altar with a bronze eagle was set up at each Olympiad; when the official operated the mechanism, the eagle rose in view of the public and a bronze dolphin fell to the ground. The cords farthest from the point were released first and, as those chariots drew level with the next ones, the cords of these were released, so that at the point of the prow all were made equal. The inventor, Kleoitas, son of Aristokles, recorded it on a statue in Athens, and a certain Aristides improved it afterwards.
>
> Waterfield (2018, ch. 5) calculates sides of more than 120 metres and explains that the chariots at the back reached the line with more momentum, an advantage that offset their greater distance from the centre of the track. A society that builds such a device to equalise the start of dozens of chariots shows how much it cared that victory should appear clean.

### Diapositiva 101 · Women in the polis

**`Notas del orador`**

> Dodero Paz (2012, p. 20) starts from a general observation, namely that, as in any other Greek city-state, the Spartan woman did not enjoy the right to take an active part in politics and formed a group dependent on the males with full citizenship rights, although she was not left outside the community, to which she belonged as mother and wife. Kyle (2014b, p. 258) puts it in similar terms: except in Sparta, motherhood and the family home circumscribed the social role of women, to the point that Xenophon (Oeconomicus 10.9) considered that household tasks were enough as "exercise" for the wife.
>
> Christesen (2012b, p. 128) explains the relation between age and sport. From very early on there were athletic competitions within the rites that marked the coming of age; for boys, at about 17 or 18, it meant beginning to serve as soldiers, and for girls, between 12 and 14, being ready to marry. Outside those rites it was rare for girls to be allowed to take part in sport, and married women were excluded altogether. Kyle (2014b, p. 258) agrees that female practice almost always concerned unmarried girls, the parthenoi, in rites of passage that prepared them for marriage, and Scanlon (2002, p. 121) defines the Spartan girls' games and the Attic Arkteia as a preparation of the prenuptial strength of the future wives and mothers of warriors.
>
> Sparta was the exception. Dodero Paz (2012, pp. 19-21) distinguishes two stages: in the archaic period the education of young women was articulated in the choruses, which prepared them for religious festivals that concealed rites of passage, and the woman was valued as a link between aristocratic families through marriage; in the classical period the State took control of that education, paid greater attention to physical exercise and gave it a eugenic and political purpose, since the value of the woman came to be measured by her role as mother and wife of soldiers. The main discipline was running, of cultic basis, and wrestling and the bibasis, a dance of leaps in which one had to strike the buttocks with the heels, are also documented (p. 20). The author explains the eugenic purpose by the scarcity of Spartiates, the oliganthropia, and recommends taking with great caution the passage of Plutarch that would equate death in childbirth with death in combat, since it rests on a disputed philological emendation (pp. 21-22).
>
> Christesen (2012a, pp. 207-208) qualifies the image of the naked Spartan woman. Some forty bronze statuettes of the sixth and fifth centuries BC show girls, more than half naked and others in shorts or a short tunic, and the most probable interpretation is that the young women ordinarily exercised in short tunics and only on special occasions, above all ritual ones, without clothing. Many ancient authors, such as Critias, Xenophon and Plutarch, attributed to their sporting practice the purpose of begetting stronger children (p. 213), and Christesen maintains that, even though sport may have given Spartan women a certain capacity for self-assertion, its dominant effect was to reinforce the existing social order (p. 193).
>
> All this information arrives filtered. Kyle (2014b, p. 259) warns that almost all the testimonies were produced by men and reflect their idea of correct female conduct, so that the physical activity of women was recorded only when it seemed strange, improper or erotic, and Scanlon (2002, p. 121) adds that those who transmitted these sources considered women's sport of little interest and little importance. The place of women in Greek sport depended, in short, on their age and marital status, and the decisive boundary was marriage.

### Diapositiva 104 · Sanctions and malpractice

**`Notas del orador`**

> The judges and their staff are those described by Kyle (2015, p. 113): the Hellanodikai, in purple tunics and with a forked staff, had at their disposal mastigophoroi, rhabdouchoi, a policing body (the alytarch and the alytai), cooks, heralds and slaves; three judges ran the equestrian events, three the races and the rest the combat events, and they could expel, fine or flog athletes for cheating or lying. Buraselis (2017, p. 134, n. 4) records the case of Leon of Ambracia, who appealed against two of the three Hellanodikai, who were fined. Miller (2003, pp. 5 and 7) adds that flogging, administered by the mastigophoros, was the usual punishment, which was used even against the athlete who arrived late for the compulsory month of training in Elis and did not pay the fine, and that the judges were forbidden to strike the head.
>
> Some of the oldest inscriptions of Olympia, of the sixth century BC, already contained prohibitions of fouls in wrestling and rules on fines, and the fouls of the moment, such as putting fingers in the eyes in the pankration, were corrected with the whip (p. 126). For centuries the start was given by voice and whoever jumped the start was flogged (p. 109). The anecdote of Themistocles is from Herodotus (8.59), in Kyle (2015, p. 126).
>
> The Zanes, whose name is the dialectal plural of Zeus, stood beside the vaulted entrance of the stadium, at the foot of the terrace of the treasuries, where their bases are preserved (Buraselis, 2017, p. 133; Hyde, 1921, figs. 1917). The fines for bribery did not go to the sanctuary treasury, since they were invested in these statues, whose inscriptions proclaimed that Olympic victories were not bought with money, as a lesson for all the Greeks (Buraselis, 2017, p. 133). Fraud was understood as hybris, an insolent violation of the sacred law that founded the Games, comparable in its moral essence to the crimes of the barbarians who had tried to enslave Greece (p. 134). The first group, of six statues, was paid for in 388 BC with the fines on Eupolos of Thessaly and on the boxers of Arcadia, Cyzicus and Halicarnassus whom he had bribed, and one of its epigrams recalled that at Olympia what counted was swiftness of foot and strength of body (p. 135). The second group, also of six, comes from the fine on the Athenian pentathlete Kallippos in 332 BC (pp. 135-136), and Buraselis also documents two statues for the bribery of a Rhodian wrestler at the 178th Olympiad, 68 BC (p. 136), without giving a closed total. Kyle (2015, pp. 126-127) counts at least sixteen, a figure that is relegated to the notes, with the last zanes in AD 125 (p. 318), and Nielsen (2014, p. 139, n. 2) counts twelve in the fourth century BC and reads them as monuments to the power of the Hellanodikai and of Elis. Dillon (2021, pp. 572-573) stresses that the inscription named the offender and his offence and that the crime was an offence against Zeus himself; he dates the first zanes to the 98th Olympiad and gives the year 384 BC, although the 98th Olympiad corresponds to 388 BC, which is the date of Kyle and of Buraselis.
>
> The reading of the Zanes as a sign of a drift in the Games has its nuances. Kyle (1993, p. 3) attributes the scheme of an aristocratic golden age, placed between 510 and 440 BC, followed by a decline caused by professionalism and specialisation, to the traditional historiography of Gardiner and Jüthner, and replaces it with the idea of transformation; on p. 124 he sums up the conventional account, according to which professional athletes, often of low class, would have degraded athletics. Kyle (2015, pp. 126-127) maintains along the same lines that the abuses were not a late phenomenon, a harbinger of decline, and appeared as a by-product of the passion for victory, and proposes seeing in the Zanes an ingenious innovation, a public and lasting warning. Buraselis (2017, pp. 140-141) concludes that these administrative data, apparently minor, reveal the moral values, the self-image and the political life of a society. Victory was worth so much that it was worth buying, and the sanctuary turned each piece of cheating into a monument that recalled who was in command at Olympia.

### Diapositiva 106 · From ritual to spectacle

**`Notas del orador`**

> Durántez places the first symptoms of decline at the end of the classical period. In religious terms, the feeling of union with Zeus weakens and victory comes to be sought for the honours and benefits it brings (p. 48); the stadium leaves the Altis. In technical terms, between 440 and 338 BC the professional athlete appears, specialised and attentive to perquisites, the public shifts towards the heavy events and chariot races, and the first frauds and the Zanes emerge (p. 49). In educational terms, kalokagathia is unbalanced towards the moral, with Plato and the quotation from Jüthner (pp. 49-50).
>
> In the Hellenistic period, Zeus loses primacy among Greeks raised in Asia and Africa; professionalism becomes established, the pankration gains followers and is opened to youths in 200 BC (Phaidimos of Alexandria), the pentathlete loses the favour of the public and the comic dramatist Menander describes the Olympia of the early third century BC as "fair, market, acrobats, entertainment, thieves" (p. 53). With Rome, the conquerors do not understand nudity or formative agonism (p. 54), and, following Schöbel, the Games become a meeting point for a heterogeneous society from all over the Empire, with characteristics of the circus; commercialisation brings prizes for victory, corruption and fines, with a long list of athletes who paid for Zanes (p. 55).
>
> Historiographical contrast. This moral reading of rise and decline is what Remijsen (2015, p. 6) calls the traditional scheme: Young (1984) showed that the Greek "amateur athlete" is a modern myth, and Kyle (2015, pp. 318-320) documents the rebirth of Olympia under Rome, with professional athletes, associations and prizes that had formed part of the system for much longer. In class it is advisable to present the two perspectives: the loss of foundational values that Durántez stresses and the institutional continuity that Kyle and Remijsen stress.

### Diapositiva 107 · Olympia under Rome

**`Notas del orador`**

> The games of Sulla in 80 BC, held to celebrate his victories over Mithridates, included athletic contests and were said to have left Olympia that year with no event but the stadion race, because all the athletes had been summoned to Rome (Appian, Roman History 1.99, in Newby, 2005, p. 26, checked in the PDF on 04/10). Newby records that Appian does not say that Sulla transferred the Games to Rome.
>
> Nero competed in AD 67 in a special race of ten-horse chariots, fell, did not finish and was declared victor, for which he generously rewarded the judges (Suetonius, Nero 24, in Spivey, 2005, p. 87).
>
> Archaeology, focused since 1987 on the Roman period, suggests that Rome prolonged the life of Olympia. The south-west building, with a pool and courtyard, is provisionally identified as a "club house" for the victors; it was begun by Nero, finished by the Flavians and remained in use during the fourth century. Around AD 150 Herodes Atticus, a friend of Hadrian, added the nymphaeum (aqueduct and fountain), and when Pausanias visited the sanctuary in the 170s it was at its architectural zenith (Kyle, 2015, p. 318). There were also more than thirty new "Olympic" Games in the cities of the Roman East (Kyle, 2015, p. 319).
>
> The athletes of the Empire lived from their careers. Marcus Aurelius Asclepiades of Alexandria, a pancratiast, won at Olympia in AD 181 and in many contests of Italy, Greece and Asia, retired at the age of 25 after six years of competition and was director and high priest for life of his association of athletes (Kyle, 2015, p. 320). Dio Chrysostom gives as much as five talents, some 30,000 drachmas, as the price of the presence of an Olympic victor (Spivey, 2005, p. 200). The comparison with the paidotribes refers back to S33.
>
> Change of 04/10: the case of Nero moves to its own slide ("Nero at Olympia"). In its place, the victories of Tiberius in the four-horse chariot race of the 194th Olympiad (AD 4) and of Germanicus in the 199th (AD 17) (Durántez Corral, 2010, p. 57; Spivey, 2005, p. 198).

### Diapositiva 109 · When was the last edition of the Games?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The date of 393 refers to an anti-pagan constitution of Theodosius I that does not mention the Olympic Games, and the connection between the two facts derives from the doubtful testimony of Cedrenus (Kedrenos), an eleventh-century Byzantine chronicler, and from the presumption that the end of the agones had to be due to a religious law (Remijsen, 2015, p. 47). The date is usually linked to Codex Theodosianus 16.10.10-12 (Remijsen, 2015, p. 47, n. 46). Cedrenus does not deserve credit in this passage because he confuses the burning of the synagogue of Callinicum, in 388, with the destruction of the synagogue of Constantinople under Theodosius II, and his explanation of the indictions is nonsense (Remijsen, 2015, p. 48). In 399 Claudian, in the panegyric on the consulship of Flavius Manlius Theodorus (vv. 288-290), asks that the athletes crowned at the Isthmus and the youths praised by the "Elean thunderer", that is, by Olympian Zeus, should come to Rome (Remijsen, 2015, p. 49).
>
> The historiographical contrast is Spivey (2005, pp. 202-203), who still writes that the end of the festival "came about as a result of edicts from Constantinople" and places Olympia under direct prohibition during the reign of Theodosius I. The revision began with Young (1984), who dismantled the myth of the amateur athlete, and with Weiler (1985), who pointed out the weight of nationalism and Marxism in the causes that had been proposed (Remijsen, 2015, p. 6). Kyle (2015, p. 336) already records the revision: the prohibition of 393 did not put an end to the Games at a stroke.
>
> The missorium dates from 388, before the law, and is chosen because it shows the emperor; if another image is preferred, a coin of Theodosius I will do.
>
> The plaque measures some 75 cm high by 40 wide, came to light in 1994 stuffed into the pipe of a latrine in the so-called south-west building, a luxurious villa-type structure adapted to the training of athletes, and was trimmed at the top and on the left so that it would fit in the pipe, which was apparently meant to be blocked. Its twenty names were engraved at different times over perhaps 300 years and by some fourteen hands; the first dated one is of 28 BC (188th Olympiad) and all those in the right-hand column and the final additions are of the fourth century (Remijsen, 2015, p. 44). The last are the Athenian brothers Eukarpides, victor in the boys' pankration in 381, and Zopyros, in the boys' boxing in 385, and another Athenian, Pankratios, victor in the pentathlon in 381 (Remijsen, 2015, p. 46). The function of the plaque is disputed: it was thought to be the list of an association of athletes, and Remijsen proposes a sort of visitors' book of victors lodged in the building, with names that sometimes come with "νικῶ", "I win", in the first person (p. 46).
>
> The find multiplied by six the known victors of the fourth century, who had previously been only two, Philoumenos of Philadelphia and the Armenian prince Varazdates, whose victory in boxing is told by Moses of Chorene and whom Remijsen asks not to be discarded, albeit with caution. All the new victors come from Greece or Asia Minor, which could indicate a regionalisation, although the sample is too small, and the regular distribution of victories refutes the idea that many Olympiads were suspended after the third century (Remijsen, 2015, p. 47). Kyle (2015, p. 336) records the find: the last known victor moved from 369 to 385.
>
> The Heruli joined the Goths in the raids across the Black Sea and in 267 invaded Achaea, with destruction documented in Athens, Corinth, Sparta and Argos, but no literary source says that they reached Olympia (Remijsen, 2015, p. 38), and the damage to the buildings is later, probably from an earthquake c. 300 (p. 39). The wall that surrounds the temple of Zeus was raised with pieces from other buildings, perhaps in the face of the threat of 267, with no trace of looting, and in 269 Dionysios of Alexandria won the stadion race (p. 42).
>
> Two scholia on Lucian link the end of the Games to a fire in the temple under Theodosius II, son of Arcadius: "when the temple of Olympian Zeus was burnt, the festival of the Eleans and the Olympic contest ceased". The scholia do not say that the emperor caused the fire, and a small accidental fire may have sufficed if nobody paid for the restoration (Remijsen, 2015, p. 49). The date of 426 (13 November) for an edict of destruction of temples goes back to Adler (1897), who gives no reference; Fargnoli proposes 435 (Codex Theodosianus 16.10.25) (Remijsen, 2015, p. 49, n. 52). Kyle (2015, p. 336) still writes that the destruction of the temple by Theodosius II in 426 may have damaged the Games; Remijsen is followed, who expressly revises that date. Spivey (2005, p. 203) likewise finds no archaeological evidence that Theodosius II burnt the temple.
>
> Cedrenus places the chryselephantine statue by Pheidias in the collection of the quarter of Lausus in Constantinople; Lausus was chamberlain of Theodosius II in 420 and perhaps again in the 430s, and the transfer, costly and with imperial permission, must have been carried out in those years (Remijsen, 2015, pp. 49-50). In the fifth century the workshop of Pheidias became a church, on a layer of silt from a flood of the Kladeos, and the baths of the Leonidaion became a wine cellar (p. 50). The stadium was not built over because it never had stone seating to reuse, and the fifth-century objects found in it prove that the track was no longer cleaned, not that athletics continued (p. 51). The last coins are of Justin II (565-578), and recent geoarchaeological studies attribute the layer of sediment to repeated tsunamis at the end of the sixth century (p. 51). Kyle (2015, p. 336) recalls that the last "Olympic" Games were those of Antioch, prohibited by the emperor Justin in 520; Remijsen (2015, p. 51) rejects that they were held under a licence from Elis.
>
> The traditional list of immediate causes (Heruli, earthquakes, the rise of Christianity and an edict of Theodosius I, or sometimes of Theodosius II) was repeated without discussion until 1984 (Remijsen, 2015, p. 6). Remijsen explains the end by the withdrawal of the elite: the circuit, perfected under the court in the first and second centuries with the help of the associations of athletes, overheated in the third century by adding high-ranking contests without stable funding (p. 344); the crisis of the fourth century hit above all the agones sustained by a cash fund, while the distance grew between the very rich and the rest of the curia (p. 308); from the tetrarchy onwards the notable had to choose between paying for agones or circus games, increasingly favoured by the authorities (p. 321), which offered larger venues and a long association with imperial birthdays and consulships (p. 341); and the agones benefited from the success of their neighbours as much as they suffered from their decline (p. 328). The cultic elements, such as sacrifices, had been largely withdrawn over the course of the fourth century, so that the Christian minority that saw the agones as pagan found it increasingly difficult to justify this (p. 343).
>
> Kyle (2015, p. 335) poses the same alternative for the whole Roman West ("Were the games abolished or abandoned?") and concludes that the end of the spectacles was gradual, with economic, logistical and imperial-collapse factors alongside Christian opposition.
