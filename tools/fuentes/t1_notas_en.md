### Diapositiva 4 · What Is History?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The definition offered by the Diccionario de la lengua española, "a set of political, social, economic, cultural, etc. events or facts of a people or a nation" (Real Academia Española, 2014), describes what History narrates, but not what History does, and for that reason it proves insufficient for anyone who is going to practice it as a university discipline. The misunderstanding consists in reducing History to a chronicle, when its true object is the method by which it is decided which facts of the past matter and why.
>
> Edward H. Carr established this idea with a formula that has become classic: there exist millions of facts about the past, and only a few become historical facts because the historian selects them according to the questions he brings with him, so that History ends up defined as "a continuous process of interaction between the historian and his facts, an unending dialogue between the present and the past" (Carr, 1961/2010, p. 40). From this arise the three temporal planes announced on the screen, the past being studied, the present from which it is studied, and a third plane, the future, which enters because the questions a historian formulates depend on the concerns of his own moment, concerns that change with the time to come. Recognizing that the historian looks from somewhere does not open the door to any arbitrary interpretation, since that outlook is always subjected to the test of the sources.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Carr, E. H. (2010). *¿Qué es la historia?* (J. Romero Maura, Trad.). Ariel. (Trabajo original publicado en 1961).
> Real Academia Española. (2014). *Diccionario de la lengua española* (23.ª ed.). Espasa.

### Diapositiva 5 · History as a Tool

**`Notas del orador`**

> That History functions as an analytical tool extensible to other fields depends on its applying a method and not merely a narrative intuition. That method starts from a prior renunciation, that the past can no longer be observed directly, so that the historian works on its traces, documents, objects, images, and subjects them to a test of coherence that admits error and its correction.
>
> Leopold von Ranke established in nineteenth-century Germany the first model of that discipline with the aim of showing the past "as it actually was" (Ranke, 1824), a decisive advance over unverified legend, although his weak point lay in believing that facts speak for themselves once the researcher steps aside and lets them speak. The correction comes later from Carr, for whom knowing a fact, interpreting it within a question and drawing from it a reasoned conclusion are the three operations that truly constitute the historical method, and not the mere accumulation of verified data (Carr, 1961/2010, p. 60).

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Carr, E. H. (2010). *¿Qué es la historia?* (J. Romero Maura, Trad.). Ariel. (Trabajo original publicado en 1961).
> Ranke, L. von. (1824). *Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker von 1494 bis 1514*. Reimer.

### Diapositiva 6 · Object and Subject of Study in History

**`Notas del orador`**

> The subject of History is always the human being, considered both in its individual and its collective dimension, and its object has no thematic boundaries because it encompasses everything surrounding that subject that can be read through facts of relevant significance. Sport belongs to that object in its own right, although it took time to be accepted as a serious matter of research.

### Diapositiva 8 · 1928 Amsterdam Olympics

**`Notas del orador`**

> The women's 800 meters flat event made its debut at an Olympic Games in Amsterdam in 1928 amid an atmosphere of explicit opposition from coaches, physicians and even the International Olympic Committee, who considered it harmful to the female organism (English, 2019). The account that has traveled through textbooks for nearly a century holds that several runners collapsed exhausted at the finish, apparent proof that the distance exceeded women's strength, an account that led to the ban on events longer than 200 meters until their reinstatement at Rome in 1960.
>
> The scholarship qualifies that account considerably. Roger Robinson went back to the original material, the chronicles of the period and the official results, and reconstructed what happened with precision: nine athletes took the start, all nine finished the race, none withdrew, and the winner, the German Lina Radke, broke the world record (Robinson, 2018). The collapse repeated in textbooks was invented by the press of the time, led by the American reporter John Tunis, and the International Olympic Committee later made use of that journalistic hoax to justify the ban, which was not lifted until the 1960 Rome Games (World Athletics, s. f.). The slide rightly notes that all the athletes finished the event, a fact confirmed by the scholarship, with the added nuance that the collapse itself, rather than a proven fact, was a media construction with very real institutional consequences.
>
> For the essay, the episode documents clearly the function of distinction, in its variant of exclusion by sex, and further shows how a groundless account can sustain an institutional barrier for decades.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> English, C. (2019). "Beyond women's powers of endurance": The 1928 800-meter and women's Olympic track and field in the context of the United States. *Sport History Review*, *50*(2), 187-204.
> Robinson, R. (2018). *When Running Made History*. Syracuse University Press.
> World Athletics. (s. f.). *Evolution of Olympic Women's Athletics, 1928 to the present day*. https://worldathletics.org

### Diapositiva 9 · From Boston to Beijing

**`Notas del orador`**

> The contrast sets two moments of the same race against each other. Switzer completed Boston in 1967 in four hours and twenty minutes, running without official support and under the hostility of the organizers (Switzer, 2007). Forty-one years later, in the women's marathon at the 2008 Beijing Games, the Romanian Constantina Diță-Tomescu won with a time of 2h 26min, and did so moreover as the oldest Olympic champion in the history of the marathon, men and women included, at 38 years of age, taking that symbolic record from the Portuguese Carlos Lopes (Fernández Truan, s. f., p. 340). The gap between the two times, nearly double, should not be read merely as a difference in athletic level, since Switzer was running under conditions of training, footwear and institutional recognition that had nothing to do with those enjoyed by a professional elite athlete eighty years after the women's marathon existed even as a recognized category.
>
> For the essay, the comparison measures in a single figure the distance separating exclusion from institutionalization, and sustains the same function of distinction as the neighboring slides, already overcome in its most explicit form.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Fernández Truan, J. C. (s. f.). *Historia de los Juegos Olímpicos Modernos* (Manual de Historia de la Actividad Física y del Deporte, Tomo III). Universidad Pablo de Olavide.

### Diapositiva 10 · The other pioneers

**`Notas del orador`**

> Before Switzer there is a genealogy of women who ran the marathon without permission or official recognition, and whom feminist sport historiography has gradually rescued from oblivion. Stamata Revithi ran nearly the entire course of Athens 1896 the day after the men's race, without being allowed to enter the stadium and without her time being registered officially, so that her feat remains outside the official record by a decision imposed on her and not for lack of merit. Dale Greig completed the Isle of Wight marathon in 1964 with a time of 3h 27min, and a good part of athletics organizations recognize her as the first woman to run a certified marathon with a formal timed record. Bobbi Gibb ran and finished Boston in 1966, a year before Switzer, hidden in the bushes near the start and without a bib number, after the organization had rejected her entry on the grounds that women "were not physiologically capable" of that distance (Jutel, 2003). Switzer's own testimony acknowledges Gibb's priority (Switzer, 2007).
>
> The pattern that connects all three is identical to that of Amsterdam 1928, the invocation of a supposed female physiological incapacity to sustain endurance effort, an invocation that the empirical evidence of each of these races immediately contradicts.
>
> For the essay, this series of pioneers documents the function of distinction in its purest form, restricted access to a practice on grounds of sex alone, sustained by a biological argument without support, and its progressive erosion over seven decades.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Jutel, A. (2003). 'Thou dost run as in flotation': Femininity, reassurance and the emergence of the Women's marathon. *The International Journal of the History of Sport*, *20*(3), 17-36.
> Switzer, K. (2007). *Marathon Woman: Running the Race to Revolutionize Women's Sports*. Da Capo Press.

### Diapositiva 11 · Sources for the knowledge of History

**`Notas del orador`**

> The screen distinguishes sources according to their intention, direct when the society that produced them meant to record its own history, indirect when it had no such intention and their historical value depends entirely on the historian's subsequent interpretation. Marc Bloch worked with a related, though not terminologically identical, distinction between voluntary testimonies, written for posterity such as a funerary inscription or an official chronicle, and involuntary testimonies, which no one conceived as a historical record, such as a tomb painting or the remains of a grandstand. Bloch warned that the latter tend to lie less, precisely because they were not made to convince anyone (Bloch, 1949/2001, pp. 84-93), an observation that reinforces the usefulness of the category of indirect source proposed on screen.
>
> Every source, direct or indirect, undergoes the same methodical interrogation, who made it, when, where, for whom and what it conceals, a procedure that Julio Aróstegui systematizes as source criticism (Aróstegui, 2001, pp. 381-420). An object carved in bone or a scene painted on a wall often admit several simultaneous readings, and the honest historian keeps them open as long as the available evidence does not decide between them.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Aróstegui, J. (2001). *La investigación histórica: teoría y método*. Crítica.
> Bloch, M. (2001). *Apología para la historia o el oficio de historiador* (M. Jiménez y D. Zaslavsky, Trads.). Fondo de Cultura Económica. (Trabajo original publicado en 1949).

### Diapositiva 12 · Example of a direct source

**`Notas del orador`**

> The official reports of each edition of the Olympic Games, summer and winter, are the textbook example of a direct source, since they are drafted by the organizing committee itself with the explicit intention of leaving a record for posterity of what happened in the competition. That character of voluntary testimony, in Bloch's terminology, does not make it a neutral document, because whoever narrates the events is also the one with an interest in presenting them in a certain way, so that the source criticism Aróstegui demands, asking who writes, for whom and what is omitted (Aróstegui, 2001, pp. 381-420), applies to this type of material with the same rigor as to any other. The Amsterdam 1928 report is, moreover, one of the documents that allows a precise reconstruction of what happened in the women's 800 meters event discussed in the previous slides.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Aróstegui, J. (2001). *La investigación histórica: teoría y método*. Crítica.

### Diapositiva 13 · Examples of indirect sources

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Tomb of the Augurs, in the necropolis of Monterozzi, in Tarquinia, is one of the oldest and most important among the Etruscan funerary paintings that document sporting practice, and the literature dates it to around 540-530 BCE, a range somewhat higher than the one shown on screen (Bevagna, 2014, p. 397). Its burial chamber combines three sporting scenes, two naked boxers, a pair of wrestlers next to whom three large vessels are stacked serving as a prize, and a masked and bearded figure, wearing a pointed cap, who moves energetically in a gesture that the scholarship itself acknowledges as ambiguous, perhaps a dance, perhaps a race (Bevagna, 2014, p. 397), precisely the doubt captured in the slide's title. This is an involuntary testimony in Bloch's sense, painted to accompany the deceased and not to inform a future historian, and it is that undeliberate character that gives it documentary value (Bloch, 1949/2001, pp. 84-93).
>
> The giraffe scene from the Tomb of Rekhmire, in Egypt, is an example of the same class of source. This course's working material places it around 1450 BCE, a somewhat earlier date than the one on screen, within the biography of a high official who had no intention whatsoever of recording sporting events and who, for that very reason, in Bloch's logic, proves more reliable than any courtly chronicle written with that purpose.
>
> For the essay, both scenes originate in a funerary context, and that circumstance duly supports the function of rite, that of sport in the service of the sacred and of accompanying the deceased.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Bevagna, G. (2014). Etruscan sport. En P. Christesen y D. G. Kyle (Eds.), *A companion to sport and spectacle in Greek and Roman antiquity* (pp. 395-411). Wiley Blackwell.
> Bloch, M. (2001). *Apología para la historia o el oficio de historiador* (M. Jiménez y D. Zaslavsky, Trads.). Fondo de Cultura Económica. (Trabajo original publicado en 1949).

### Diapositiva 15 · Examples

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Book of Games that Alfonso X the Wise finished in Seville in 1283, also known as *Libro del acedrex, dados e tablas*, is the oldest European treatise devoted to chess and constitutes a primary source of the first order for late medieval leisure, since it was produced in the very period it documents (*Libro del acedrex, dados e tablas*, 1283). José Luis Salvador's book, *El deporte en Occidente*, is by contrast a secondary source, written seven centuries later with the intention of interpreting that past from the distance of academic research (Salvador, 2004).
>
> The example carries added methodological interest, because it shows how the same primary source can generate, over time, a growing number of secondary sources that reinterpret it under different questions, without any of them replacing the other in documentary authority. This manual, in fact, draws in the last instance on Salvador's doctoral thesis, which the screen identifies as one of the most complete compendiums on the history of sport in Spanish, and which in turn constitutes an essential secondary source of reference for this course.

### Diapositiva 16 · Divisions of History

**`Notas del orador`**

> Ordering the past requires cutting it into periods, an operation that the European tradition resolves with the usual sequence of Prehistory, Ancient Age, Middle Ages, Modern Age and Contemporary Age. This is a convention that is convenient to handle, although strictly European, since its milestones, the invention of writing, the fall of Rome, 1492 or the French Revolution, belong to the history of the Mediterranean and say very little about societies that kept their own clock, such as China or pre-Columbian America. The Sumerian tablet with cuneiform writing shown in the slide refers precisely to the first of those milestones, the appearance of writing, which the classic periodization takes as the boundary marking the end of Prehistory.
>
> Sport, however, does not advance at the pace of those ages. Physical practices with a military, ritual or social distinction function span millennia without interruption, whereas modern sport has barely two centuries of existence. Allen Guttmann established the set of traits that distinguish that modern sport, secularization, equality of opportunity, specialization, rationalization, bureaucratization, quantification and the obsession with the record, and showed that they are only found together in the contemporary period (Guttmann, 1978, p. 16). Hence sport requires, within the general European framework, its own periodization, which this manual will build chapter by chapter.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Guttmann, A. (1978). *From Ritual to Record: The Nature of Modern Sports*. Columbia University Press.

### Diapositiva 18 · Auxiliary sciences of history

**`Notas del orador`**

> The historian does not work alone, and recourse to the so-called auxiliary sciences, which source criticism requires in order to access testimonies that would otherwise remain mute (Aróstegui, 2001, pp. 381-420), proves decisive in the study of ancient sport. Archaeology has brought back to light stadiums, hippodromes and ballcourts in cultures very different from one another, while numismatics allows a whole ideology of the body to be read, condensed into a few centimeters of metal, on an ancient coin. Palaeography makes legible the medieval manuscript presented in the slide on primary sources, and epigraphy recovers lists of victors engraved in stone. Anthropology and ethnology compare the physical practices of different cultures with one another, and geography explains why a society of horsemen arises on the steppe and not in the marsh.
>
> Each of these seven disciplines resolves a different problem of the ancient source, material dating, the symbolic value of an object, the reading of an extinct script, the engraved record, cultural comparison and the constraint of territory, and it is their combination that allows some rigorous reconstruction of societies that left no explicit text about their bodily practices, as will happen with Crete in the following unit.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Aróstegui, J. (2001). *La investigación histórica: teoría y método*. Crítica.

### Diapositiva 20 · Approaches to the study of History

**`Notas del orador`**

> Every historical source admits two classic and complementary perspectives. The diachronic view follows a specific phenomenon over time, for example how wrestling passes, across successive societies, from an initiation rite to a regulated sport. The synchronic view, by contrast, freezes a single moment and studies its internal connections, for example what the gymnasium meant in fifth-century BCE Athens in its simultaneous relation to the education, politics and religion of that same society.
>
> Neither perspective suffices on its own, and the historian alternates between them according to what they wish to understand at each point, without declaring either one permanently superior to the other. This manual will draw mainly on the synchronic view within each chapter, in order to establish what a given society needed from a given practice, although it will turn to the diachronic view whenever a practice runs across several of the civilizations studied and its transformation from one to another deserves to be traced step by step.

### Diapositiva 24 · Terminological aspects

**`Notas del orador`**

> The five definitions on screen distinguish concepts that everyday conversation frequently confuses and that the course needs to keep clearly separate, because each refers to a distinct historical reality. The specific formulations of physical activity, physical exercise, physical education and gymnastics given on screen are not found verbatim in this manual's working library, and their most probable origin is the kinesiology literature, of which the library holds related though not identically worded developments.
>
> The definition of play does find direct support. Johan Huizinga formulated it as «una acción libre ejecutada "como si" y sentida como situada fuera de la vida corriente, pero que, a pesar de todo, puede absorber por completo al jugador, sin que haya en ella ningún interés material ni se obtenga en ella provecho alguno, que se ejecuta dentro de un determinado tiempo y un determinado espacio, y que se desarrolla en un orden sometido a reglas» (Huizinga, 1972, pp. 26-27), a formula from which the slide faithfully takes freedom, temporal limitation and submission to rules. Kretchmar, Dyreson, Llewellyn and Gleaves later refine this same idea by correcting it with Suits, who judged Huizinga's definition too broad, and they fix play as an attitude or a way of approaching an activity that is «autotélico y temporal» (Kretchmar et al., 2017, cap. 1), that is, an activity whose end lies within itself and not outside it, a precision that clearly distinguishes play from exercise planned with an external objective, such as the physical improvement the slide itself pursues in defining exercise.
>
> The distinction among these five terms will prove operative in the following units whenever a given ancient bodily practice must be classified as a rite, as training or as mere entertainment.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Huizinga, J. (1972). *Homo ludens: El juego y la cultura* (E. Imaz, Trad.). Alianza. (Trabajo original publicado en 1938).
> Kretchmar, R. S., Dyreson, M., Llewellyn, M. P., y Gleaves, J. (2017). *History and philosophy of sport and physical activity*. Human Kinetics.

### Diapositiva 25 · What are the limits of sport?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The closed approach given on screen, sport as a regulated, competitive and institutionalized physical activity oriented toward maximum performance, and the open approach, which broadens the category to any form of activity, organized or not, aimed at expression or at physical or psychological improvement (Consejo de Europa, 1992; Rey, 2014), reproduce a tension that sport theory has been discussing for decades under other terms.
>
> Allen Guttmann proposed resolving it through a chain of successive dichotomies, play, organized play, competitive play and, at the last step, physical contest, which is what he calls sport in the strict sense, defined as a non-utilitarian, playful physical contest (Guttmann, 1978, pp. 7 y 9). That deliberately narrow scheme is the academic version of the closed approach on screen, and it excludes from the category of sport, among other cases the author himself discusses, chess, for lacking a sufficient physical component, or whale hunting, for its utilitarian purpose. In the same passage, Guttmann also discusses Johan Huizinga's proposal, which had dissolved the boundary between play and contest by treating every rule-governed confrontation as a form of play, and he recalls Roger Caillois's reply, for whom Huizinga's work is in fact an inquiry into the ludic spirit in culture and not a rigorous study of games themselves (Guttmann, 1978, p. 7).
>
> The discussion is not merely academic for this course, because choosing between a closed and an open approach determines whether an ancient practice with no institutionalization, no record and no formal competition can be called sport, a question that will resurface in each of the following units.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Consejo de Europa. (1992). *Carta Europea del Deporte*.
> Guttmann, A. (1978). *From Ritual to Record: The Nature of Modern Sports*. Columbia University Press.
> Rey, A. (2014). *Deporte y motricidad. Epistemología de las ciencias de la actividad física y el deporte*. Dykinson.

### Diapositiva 26 · So then... what is sport?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The etymological reconstruction on screen closely follows the one offered by Olivera-Betrán and Torrebadella-Flix, although the library allows one date to be refined with greater precision. According to Corominas, cited by these authors, the Castilian word depuerto is already documented in the thirteenth century, and from it derives the verb deportarse, meaning to amuse oneself or to rest, around 1260, from the Latin deportare, to carry away or transport (Olivera-Betrán y Torrebadella-Flix, 2015, p. 64). The slide places that same verb around 1200 CE in the Cantar de Mio Cid, some sixty years earlier than Corominas's date, and that particular work is not cited in the article that documents the etymology in the working library.
>
> The Provençal origin noted on screen is confirmed, since the word deport circulated in that language between the eleventh and twelfth centuries with the meaning of amusement and pleasant pastime, and from it derive at once the Catalan deport, the Italian disporto, the Portuguese desporto, the French desport and the Castilian deporte (Olivera-Betrán y Torrebadella-Flix, 2015, p. 66). The route into English described on screen, desport turned into disport around 1300 and simplified to sport in the fifteenth century, matches the one reconstructed by the same source.
>

### Diapositiva 27 · Sport: a living concept

**`Notas del orador`**

> The ancient Greeks, who invented the Olympic Games, never had a word equivalent to sport, and instead used four terms that the slide lists accurately, agón, the contest, athlos, the prize-bearing trial, gymnastiké, exercise practiced naked, and paidiá, play, each of which captures a different portion of what is today gathered under a single word (Miller, 2004). This lexical absence is not a mere curiosity: it constitutes proof itself that sport, in its present-day sense, is a concept dated to the nineteenth century and permanently open in scope, under which activities with very unequal degrees of physical involvement coexist today, from Olympic athletics to e-sports.
>
> The question that must be asked of an ancient bodily practice does not ask whether that was sport by a criterion the past did not have: it asks what it was for and why that society gave it precisely that form. Four tools allow this second question to be answered throughout the manual, Marcel Mauss's techniques of the body, which understands every physical gesture as a learned social fact, Norbert Elias's civilizing process, which sees in sport a way of containing and sublimating violence, Pierre Bourdieu's theory of distinction, which reads sporting practice as a class marker, and Allen Guttmann's traits of modern sport, already presented in the slide on periodization. None of the four defines what sport is, and the four together instead make it possible to analyze any bodily practice, in any period and in any culture, with the same functional question.
>
> For the essay, this slide works as the hinge of the entire course, installing the four tools with which each of the eight functions of the sporting fact that structure the final paper will be read, chapter by chapter.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Miller, S. G. (2004). *Arete: Greek sports from ancient sources*. University of California Press.

### Diapositiva 28 · Where does the sporting impulse come from?

**`Notas del orador`**

> In Homo Ludens, Huizinga puts forward the thesis that "play is older than culture" (Huizinga, 1972, pp. 10-11), given that societies with no recognizable state organization already practice identifiable forms of play. The conceptual instrument that sustains that priority is the magic circle, a space and a time set apart from ordinary life and governed by absolute rules that would have no value outside it (Huizinga, 1972, pp. 24-25); within that enclosure the player voluntarily withdraws from necessity, and for the author that gratuitousness precedes any form of society.
>
> Elias and Dunning reverse the order of the explanation: sport does not precede society, it is a product of society, datable and locatable. The introduction to the volume reconstructs the case of English boxing, where the progressive replacement of the legs by the fists, the introduction of gloves and their padding, and the classification of boxers by categories document a growing sensitivity toward violence that developed first in England, a country that other societies imitated along with the sport itself (Elias and Dunning, 1992, pp. 33-34 and 36-37). Both readings draw on sources of a different nature, the phenomenology of playing in one case and the documentary reconstruction of historical regulations in the other, and this difference in method largely explains why they do not admit a simple synthesis. For the essay, Elias's reconstruction of English boxing serves to treat sport as the training of conduct and as the controlled decontrolling of violence, in the function as social marker and spectacle that this same process of regulation ends up producing.

> Huizinga, J. (1972). *Homo ludens: El juego y la cultura* (E. Imaz, Trad.). Alianza. (Obra original publicada en 1938)
> Elias, N., y Dunning, E. (1992). *Deporte y ocio en el proceso de la civilización* (P. Ducher, Trad.). Fondo de Cultura Económica.

### Diapositiva 29 · The seven traits of modern sport

**`Notas del orador`**

> Guttmann lists the seven traits in a single paragraph of From Ritual to Record: secularism, equality, specialization, rationalization, bureaucratization, quantification, and the quest for records (Guttmann, 1978, p. 16), and then devotes a chapter to each. Equality is developed as equality of opportunity to compete and in the conditions of competition (p. 26); rationalization, as the replacement of custom by calculation (p. 40); quantification, as the conversion of every result into a comparable number (p. 47); and the record, as its logical consequence, since the very fact of quantifying generates the need to compare the present with the past (pp. 52-53).
>
> The textbook by Kretchmar, Dyreson, Llewellyn, and Gleaves provides a highly instructive fact: a large part of British sporting quantification arose linked to horse race betting, since the need to arbitrate monetary disputes required drafting written rules, founding clubs to act as a regulating authority, and, toward the mid-eighteenth century, using stopwatches that already existed, originally devised for physicians to take the pulse, in order to record the horses' times (Kretchmar et al., 2017, chap. 6). For the essay, this origin linked to betting allows quantification and the record to be read as formal traits of modern sport and, at the same time, as an early chapter in its conversion into a commodity.

> Guttmann, A. (1978). *From ritual to record: The nature of modern sports*. Columbia University Press.
> Kretchmar, R. S., Dyreson, M., Llewellyn, M. P., y Gleaves, J. (2017). *History and philosophy of sport and physical activity*. Human Kinetics.

### Diapositiva 30 · One society, several forms at once

**`Notas del orador`**

> Eichberg does not deny that modern sport differs from what preceded it; he questions the criterion by which that distinction is measured. Against Guttmann's linear model, he proposes a method he calls trialectic, which describes three patterns of body culture present at the same time within a single society: achievement sport, built on the production of results, measurement, and comparison, whose experts are coaches, performance scientists, and physiologists; fitness sport, oriented toward health and well-being under the guidance of physicians and educators; and a third pattern, less easy to delimit, made up of bodily experience and social sensuality, visible in dance and in popular forms of play, with a different kind of specialist, the teacher, the juggler, or the acrobat, unconnected to the function of giving orders (Eichberg, 1997, pp. 122-123).
>
> Eichberg himself points out that no real phenomenon fits neatly into just one of the three categories, and that what he offers is a set of analytical categories rather than a closed catalog of contents (Eichberg, 1997, p. 123). For the essay, this caveat requires asking, of every ancient bodily practice, whether something is truly missing relative to the achievement pattern or whether it simply belongs to a different body culture, tied to a group's identity rather than to its mark or its record.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Eichberg, H. (1997). *Body cultures: Essays on sport, space and identity* (J. Bale y C. Philo, Eds.). Routledge.

### Diapositiva 33 · The emergence of bipedalism

**`Notas del orador`**

> The textbook by Kretchmar, Dyreson, Llewellyn, and Gleaves devotes the opening of its first chapter to showing that upright walking responds to a set of adaptive advantages that reinforce one another, rather than to a single cause. Bipedalism made hominins the savanna's best thermoregulators, since it exposes less body surface to the overhead sun and facilitates heat dissipation through sweating, which combined with other physical traits made them the most effective endurance predators of that environment (Kretchmar et al., 2017, chap. 1). ### Diapositiva 35 · Was there sport in Prehistory?

**`Notas del orador`**

> Prehistory occupies, by an enormous margin, the longest part of the time the human species has existed, and yet it is the worst documented, because it lacks writing and because the materials that may have been used in play or in hunting, wood, plant fiber, or cord, survive only very rarely. The textbook by Kretchmar and his co-authors itself warns that this material scarcity should not be confused with cultural simplicity, since the political, family, religious, and creative systems of hunter-gatherer groups are as complex as those of any modern society, even though their material culture is more limited (Kretchmar et al., 2017, chap. 1).
>
> Faced with this scarcity of direct sources, research has turned to three complementary approaches developed in the following slides: the formulation of historical theories and hypotheses, supported by reason and analogy; the anthropological method, which studies societies without writing or a state that still exist or existed until recently; and the archaeological method, which directly interrogates material remains. None of the three allows a certain reconstruction of what prehistoric man did or what doing it meant to him; each, instead, offers a different degree of plausibility.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Kretchmar, R. S., Dyreson, M., Llewellyn, M. P., y Gleaves, J. (2017). *History and philosophy of sport and physical activity*. Human Kinetics.

### Diapositiva 36 · a) Historical theories and hypotheses

### Diapositiva 37 · a) Historical Theories and Hypotheses (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> Lukas and Eichel develop their thesis from the German Democratic Republic and replace the term "physical exercise" with "bodily exercise" in order to distance themselves from Western idealist vocabulary; both distinguish involuntary bodily movements, common to man and animal, from bodily exercises properly speaking, of which only man is capable, and argue that the latter arise entirely from work (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 16-18). For Eichel, in the first four hundred and fifty thousand years of humanity there existed no bodily exercise, because neither the handling of picks required prior training nor would the undernourishment of the period have allowed it; the first bodily exercise properly speaking would be the javelin throw, around 70,000 BC, followed by the harpoon and, around 24,000 BC, the bow and arrow (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 18-19).
>
> With the emergence of agricultural and livestock-raising settlements, Eichel places the birth of festive competitions among members of the same community, and with the emergence of the first stratified societies, the transition of those exercises into military training properly speaking, for which he draws on the Homeric Iliad and Odyssey as an example (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 19-20). The library does not locate, for this author, the date of 1969 that the slide attributes to him. For the essay, the sequence traced by Eichel, from hunting to festive competition and from this to military training, is an early development of the function of sport as training for war.

### Diapositiva 38 · Theories on the Origin of Sport (III)

**`Notas del orador`**

> Diem opens his History of Sports with the phrase "every physical exercise had a cultic origin" and argues that prehistoric man lived his whole existence as a gift of higher powers, such that his rhythmic movements, his dances and, later, his funerary competitions, formed part of a system of actions aimed at attracting the favor of the gods or thanking them for their help (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 20-22). Popplow, for his part, places the true birth of physical exercise in the dance of the Lower Paleolithic, prior to hunting activities, documented by footprints and hand molds up to eighty thousand years old, and attributes to it three functions, a magical utility for the success of the hunt, a spiritual function linked to ecstasy, and a social function, since it was the community that fixed its place and its moment (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 14-16).
>
> The American anthropologists Van Dalen, Mitchell and Bennett, working by analogy with peoples who still live at a "primitive" cultural level, attribute to physical education three purposes at once, the physical safety of the individual and the group, group awareness or "conformity", and recreation, and explain that the games and ritual dances, led by chiefs, sorcerers or priests, served to create the emotional climate necessary to instill in the young the spirit of the tribe (Fernández Truan, Tomo I, pp. 25-26), a formulation that coincides with the one on screen except that the source does not call the first of the three "individual safety". For the essay, Diem's reading of the cultic origin of sport is the clearest formulation of its function as rite.

### Diapositiva 48 · Ambush strategies

**`Notas del orador`**

> Desert kites are stone trap structures, with two long walls converging toward an enclosed pen or a pit, distributed from Arabia to Central Asia. An excavation and dating study using radiocarbon and optically stimulated luminescence on three kites of the same chain, in southeastern Jordan, places their construction, with the highest probability, in the late Pre-Pottery Neolithic B, in the second half of the ninth millennium BC, and interprets them as an indigenous development of highly organized hunter-gatherer groups, contemporary with the agropastoral settlement of the Jordanian highlands (Tarawneh et al., 2026). Another study, based on excavations in Armenia, Jordan, Kazakhstan and Saudi Arabia, establishes, in terms the authors themselves call unequivocal and probably exclusive, the function of the kites as mass hunting traps, which is why they call them megatraps (Crassard et al., 2022).
>
> The case of the V-shaped kites of the Timnian sphere qualifies this picture. More than six thousand kites are catalogued in the arid zones of southwestern Asia, but the V-shaped type, characteristic of the Timnian culture of the southern Levantine deserts, appears associated with communities of nomadic pastoralists whose habitation sites, cemeteries and ceremonial complexes preserve very few bones of hunted prey, which has led to the proposal that their function was chiefly social and ceremonial, and not that of supplementing the diet (Nadel et al., 2024, p. 1). For the essay, this latter reading shifts the function of the Timnian kites away from subsistence hunting and toward ritual and the collective identity of the group that built them.

### Diapositiva 49 · Running and walking in the Upper Paleolithic

**`Notas del orador`**

> The site of Engare Sero, in Tanzania, preserves more than four hundred human footprints from the late Pleistocene, the largest assemblage of this kind known in the African fossil record. Analysis of the footprints identifies a group of fourteen adult women walking together at the same pace, between 1.2 and 1.5 meters per second, accompanied by only two adult men and one child, and a second group of footprints oriented in a different direction, among which one, the so-called trackway B, corresponds to a run at 2.91 meters per second, about 10.5 kilometers per hour (Hatala et al., 2020, pp. 1, 3 y 5). On this last point, one clarification is needed: the source attributes that running footprint, with a probability of fifty-two percent, to an adult woman, and not to a group of men as the slide suggests.
>
> The authors propose, drawing on ethnographic records of present-day hunter-gatherer societies, that the predominantly female group walking together may have been engaged in a cooperative foraging activity with a division of labor marked by sex, without ruling out that the men adjusted their pace to that of the women in the group (Hatala et al., 2020, p. 8).

### Diapositiva 50 · Running and walking in the Upper Paleolithic (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Australian site of Willandra Lakes, in New South Wales, brings together more than a hundred footprints dated to around 20,000 BC. In 2006 Webb, Cupper and Robins estimated that the trackway known as T8 corresponded to an individual moving at 10.3 meters per second, a speed typical of a sprint and comparable to that of an Olympic hundred-meter finalist, a figure that fueled a debate over whether the physical condition of prehistoric humans could be compared to that of today's best athletes.
>
> Ruiz and Torices later recalculated that same trackway using an equation experimentally validated on runners on a stadium track and on beach sand, and obtained a speed of 7.15 meters per second, about 25.7 kilometers per hour, with a confidence interval of between 6.22 and 8.23 meters per second (Ruiz y Torices, 2013, p. 34). Their conclusion, as they present it in their discussion, is that this recalculated speed falls clearly below that of a trained athlete and is more plausible for a person of ordinary physical condition running on a soft mud substrate (Ruiz y Torices, 2013, pp. 34-35). For the essay, this contrast between the initial estimate and the revised one is a good example of how the historical discipline itself corrects, over time, its most spectacular readings of the physical capacity of prehistoric humans.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Webb, S., Cupper, M. L., y Robins, R. (2006). Pleistocene human footprints from the Willandra Lakes, southeastern Australia. *Journal of Human Evolution*, *50*(4), 405-413.
> Ruiz, J., y Torices, A. (2013). Humans running at stadiums and beaches and the accuracy of speed estimations from fossil trackways. *Ichnos*, *20*(1), 31-35.

### Diapositiva 51 · Has physical fitness declined over time?

**`Notas del orador`**

> Ruff and his team analyzed in 2015 the strength of the long bones, femur and tibia, in a sample of one thousand eight hundred and forty-two European skeletons spanning from the Upper Paleolithic to the twentieth century. The results show a pronounced decline in anteroposterior bending strength that begins around 5000-7000 years before present, that is, around 5000-3000 BC, and continues until the Iron Age and Roman period, at which point it stops, with no significant change registered afterward until the most recent samples (Ruff et al., 2015).
>
> The interpretation the study offers is that the bones become more rounded, that is, less prepared to withstand the bending associated with running or walking long distances over uneven terrain during hunting or foraging, as sedentarization reduces those everyday mechanical loads. The relevant point for the course is that this skeletal change stops in the Iron Age and the Roman period, so that neither later industrialization nor urbanization leaves a comparable mark on the skeleton, which places the transition from hunting to farming, and not the industrial revolution, as the decisive moment of that bodily change. For the essay, this finding supports the function of sport and exercise as hygiene and health, insofar as it documents which historical moment is associated with the onset of the loss of physical fitness linked to lifestyle.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Ruff, C. B., Holt, B., Niskanen, M., Sladek, V., Berner, M., Garofalo, E., Garvin, H. M., Hora, M., Junno, J.-A., Schuplerová, E., Vilaça, R., y Whittey, E. (2015). Gradual decline in mobility with the adoption of food production in Europe. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *112*(23), 7147-7152.

### Diapositiva 52 · Has physical fitness declined over time? (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> Macintosh, Pinhasi and Stock compared in 2017 the limb bones of Central European women from the Neolithic to the Iron Age with those of living women, elite athletes and sedentary women, and found that during the first five thousand five hundred years of agriculture, the rigidity of the humerus of these prehistoric women notably exceeded that of most of the modern athletes compared in the study, including semi-elite rowers, with a repetitive upper-body workload comparable, according to the authors themselves, to that of rowing (Macintosh et al., 2017).
>
> By contrast, the legs of these same women were less taxed than those of present-day runners and football players, which the authors explain by the fact that Neolithic women's work was concentrated in upper-body tasks, among them grinding grain on manual stone mills, documented as a particularly repetitive and inefficient task because of its high muscular expenditure per kilogram of grain milled. The result that the slide accurately summarizes, a Neolithic body that loses demand in the legs and gains it in the arms, thus describes a change in the direction of women's physical effort with the arrival of agriculture, not necessarily a decrease in its total amount. For the essay, this finding refines the function of sport and exercise as hygiene and health by showing that its interpretation depends on the sex and the task being compared, and not only on the historical period.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Macintosh, A. A., Pinhasi, R., y Stock, J. T. (2017). Prehistoric women's manual labor exceeded that of athletes through the first 5500 years of farming in Central Europe. *Science Advances*, *3*(11), eaao3893.
