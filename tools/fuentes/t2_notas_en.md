### Diapositiva 6 · Imagen izquierda · Historical introduction

**`Notas del orador`**

> The development of Egyptian civilization extends, in the conventional dating, from 3150 to 30 BC, the year in which the Roman annexation puts an end to the Ptolemaic dynasty following the defeat at Actium the previous year. Egypt constitutes the first civilization for which continuous written documentation survives, spanning three millennia with no possible comparison in the ancient world, and that same documentary continuity partly explains why it is also the civilization from which the oldest sporting evidence with reasonably reliable information derives (Guttmann, 2004, p. 12). Around 3100 BC the Nile valley is organized as a unified territorial state, with administration, taxation and an army, and that same bureaucratic machinery produces much of the written record that documents the country's physical practices.
>
> Among the contributions that survive to the present day are hieroglyphic writing and papyrus, the Great Pyramid as the tallest building on the planet for close to three thousand eight hundred years, and the solar calendar of three hundred sixty-five days, whose status as the first documented solar calendar is upheld by Lull (2006, pp. 66-67, cited in Caravaca, 2014) and which Julius Caesar would adopt in 46 BC. The attribution to Egypt of the oldest surviving surgical treatise, which the slide mentions, has not been located with a precise reference in the source material reviewed for this topic, although by description it corresponds to the surgical papyrus known in the literature as the Edwin Smith papyrus.

### Diapositiva 7 · Dos imágenes · Rosetta Stone and Luxor prosthesis

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Rosetta Stone, dated to 196 BC and held at the British Museum, is a fragment 1.12 meters high and weighing about 760 kg found by Napoleon's troops in 1799, which reproduces a single priestly decree in three scripts, hieroglyphic, demotic and Greek. That triple rendering allowed Champollion to complete the decipherment of Egyptian writing in 1822, after almost fourteen hundred years of silence since the last dated hieroglyphic inscription, that of the Temple of Philae, from AD 394, drawing also on the obelisk of Philae and on Coptic. Every claim made in this topic about Egyptian names, titles and texts ultimately rests on that nineteenth-century decipherment.
>
> The wooden prosthesis found at Luxor, dated to around 600 BC and now held at the University of Basel, belonged to a noblewoman between fifty and sixty years of age who had lost her big toe, probably amputated owing to complications associated with diabetes, and is considered the oldest known functional prosthesis. The object documents, long before a medical vocabulary exists to name it, an intervention on the body aimed at restoring its function, a matter relevant to a degree devoted to the sciences of physical activity.
>
> For the essay, hygiene and health, insofar as the prosthesis bears witness to a bodily intervention aimed at restoring the body's function, two thousand six hundred years ago.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Shaw, I. (Ed.). (2000). *The Oxford history of ancient Egypt*. Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 8 · Imagen izquierda · The gift of the Nile: geography and agricultural cycle

**`Notas del orador`**

> Egypt occupies a habitable strip barely a few kilometers wide along a river course of more than a thousand kilometers, hemmed in between two deserts that protected it from sustained invasions for centuries. The Nile's annual flood deposits the silt that fertilizes the land and organizes the year into three seasons, Akhet the inundation, Peret the sowing and Shemu the harvest, so that the river itself determines when agricultural labor is available and when time is left for festivity. From that constant agricultural surplus arises a centralized state, with its priesthood, its administration of scribes and, from the New Kingdom onward, a professional army (Shaw, 2000).
>
> During the months of flooding the land lies underwater and much of the peasantry is employed in rotation on state works. Herodotus (1992, book II, 124) puts at one hundred thousand men, organized in three-month shifts, the labor force employed in building the Great Pyramid, a figure that should be read as an order of magnitude rather than as an exact census, given the time elapsed between the event and the account. It is this rotational organization, documented by Herodotus, and not the image of the whip-driven slave handed down by later tradition, that the Egyptian record allows us to affirm about state labor.
>
> For the essay, marker of distinction, insofar as the surplus that sustains a class that does not work with its hands is the condition of possibility for the elite sport that occupies the rest of the section.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Shaw, I. (Ed.). (2000). *The Oxford history of ancient Egypt*. Oxford University Press.
> Heródoto. (1992). *Historia: Libros I-II* (C. Schrader, Trad.). Gredos.

### Diapositiva 9 · Imagen derecha · The time of the Egyptians

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Egyptian civil year consisted of twelve months of thirty days, to which five festive epagomenal days were added, and it was organized into three seasons linked to the cycle of the river. This is the earliest solar calendar of which there is record (Lull, 2006, pp. 66-67, cited in Caravaca, 2014, p. 44), and worship and ceremonies were distributed across that calendar in a festive cycle that measured, controlled and renewed the cosmic order (Caravaca, 2014). This fact ceases to be merely chronological once one reaches the royal jubilee festival, whose thirty-year periodicity turns a counting convention into the trigger for the country's most important physical rite.
>
> Egypt dated by the regnal year of the reigning monarch and had no continuous era, so that chronology is reconstructed by adding up reigns from king lists. The slide puts the number of Egyptian dynasties at thirty-one, to which it adds two foreign ones, the Macedonian and the Ptolemaic. The library qualifies that count with a fact that does not contradict the figure but does affect its reading: several of those thirty-one dynasties were already of foreign origin before Alexander, given that the XV is Hyksos, the XXV is Kushite and the XXVII and XXXI are Persian, so that foreign rule over Egypt long predates the Macedonian conquest.
>
> For the essay, rite, given that the calendar organizes the festive cycle on which the periodicity of the royal jubilee, which occupies much of the following block, depends.

> Lull, J. (2006). *La astronomía en el antiguo Egipto*. Universitat de València.

### Diapositiva 10 · Imagen derecha · The social pyramid

**`Notas del orador`**

> Egyptian society was organized into a pyramid headed by the pharaoh, an incarnate god and guarantor of the order of the world, the *maat*, head of the State, of the army and of worship, and nominal owner of the land. Beneath him were the vizier, who directed the administration in his name, the clergy, which turned the temples into major owners of land and labor, and the nomarchs, provincial governors who gained autonomy when central power weakened (Lloyd, 2014). Next came the scribes, the only class capable of reading and writing and responsible for censuses and taxes, along with specialized craftsmen and, from the New Kingdom onward, a professional army paid in land; access to writing constituted the main avenue of social advancement. The peasantry sustained the whole, working the land of the king, the temple or the noble, paying in kind and providing compulsory labor during the flood.
>
> Decker (1992) adds a nuance of particular interest to the history of sport: the nobility both practiced and, moreover, watched, and the harpoon fishing scenes of the Old Kingdom must have provided the deceased noble a specific contemplative pleasure. The on-screen claim about the existence of slavery of prisoners of war, without it constituting the productive base, is consistent with what the Egyptian record documents, that is, compulsory labor by shifts rather than a generalized slave system.
>
> For the essay, marker of distinction, given that each stratum practiced, watched or served according to its position, and that position determined which physical gestures were permitted to it.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Lloyd, A. B. (2014). *Ancient Egypt: state and society*. Oxford University Press.
> Decker, W. (1992). *Sports and games of ancient Egypt* (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 11 · Destacado 1 · Egyptian women

**`Notas del orador`**

> Egyptian women crossed the four steps of the social pyramid with a legal position striking for the ancient Mediterranean: before the law they held the same basic rights as men of their own class, they could own and inherit property, buy, sell, litigate and make a will in their own name, and marriage functioned as a contract with division of property and possible divorce (Watterson, 2011). This documented legal capacity, however, corresponds almost always to elite women, since the record that sustains it comes from tombs and administrative archives that required resources to be produced.
>
> The most frequent title in the tombs is "lady of the house". Outside that domestic sphere, priestesses are documented, above all of the cult of Hathor, along with musicians, dancers, mourners, weavers, millers, wet nurses and midwives. Exceptionally, some women reached the throne, such as Hatshepsut in the eighteenth dynasty, whom the next block takes up again in connection with her royal jubilee, or Cleopatra VII at the end of Egyptian history.
>
> For the essay, marker of distinction, insofar as the notable legal position of Egyptian women is documented unevenly according to the social stratum to which they belonged.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Watterson, B. (2011). *Women in ancient Egypt*. Amberley Publishing Limited.

### Diapositiva 12 · Imagen abajo · The sources

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Egyptian sporting corpus brings together nearly two thousand scenes catalogued in iconographic sources alone, spread across three continuous millennia from the end of the fourth millennium BCE (Decker and Herb, 1994, cited in Decker, 1992). Iconography, tomb reliefs and paintings, constitutes the most abundant source and the richest in information because it allows bodies in motion to be visualized, while official text, royal stelae and monumental inscriptions, serves to quantify and to date, with the caveat that kings tended to exaggerate their feats relative to those of their predecessors. Literature is scarce, and texts such as *The Tale of Sinuhe* or the cycle of Horus and Seth provide context rather than verifiable facts. To these three sources are added the sporting materials found in tombs and sites, bows, arrows, balls, hoops, clubs and senet boards.
>
> Regarding places of practice, the on-screen text states that no space built exclusively for sport survives from the whole of Antiquity. The library allows this point to be qualified without entirely contradicting it: the funerary complex of Djoser at Saqqara preserves a track for the ritual jubilee race, the oldest sporting installation that survives standing, although it was built for the dead king to use in the afterlife and not as the setting for a competition among the living (Decker, 1992, p. 29; Decker, 2021, p. 33).
>
> Almost the entire corpus comes from aristocratic tombs, so that whoever paid for the tomb decided which scenes were recorded for eternity, a selection that conditions everything this topic can claim about the social strata that did not have access to that resource.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (1992). *Sports and games of ancient Egypt* (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 13 · Dos imágenes · Principles of sport and physical education in Egypt

**`Notas del orador`**

> Ancient Egyptian lacks a general attested term for what the modern era calls sport. The closest partial term, *śḫmḫ-ib*, pronounced sejemej-ib, literally means "making the heart forget" and designates amusement (Cannuyer, 2002, cited in Decker, 2021, p. 33). The coincidence with the late Latin root of the word *sport*, *disportare*, "to carry away", is striking and should be read as a semantic coincidence between two languages that separately arrive at naming physical activity by what it suspends, everyday occupation, and not as a genealogy between the two terms.
>
> The on-screen text divides Egyptian physical activities into two broad categories. The first brings together dances, music and acrobatics intended to enliven sacred festivals in temples and religious precincts in honor of the principal gods. The second corresponds to military exercises, whose physical education served to prepare the army for war through structured training camps. The library allows this scheme to be expanded with two functions that underlie much of the topic and that are not covered by those two categories: the legitimizing function, proper to the pharaoh, whose physical prowess is displayed in order to govern and is never practiced in order to improve, and the recreational function named by the word *śḫmḫ-ib* itself.
>
> For the essay, rite and training for war, functions to which the two categories stated on screen correspond respectively.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (2021). Sport in ancient Egypt and the ancient Near East. En A. Futrell y T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 31-46). Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 14 · Imagen izquierda · Principles of sport and physical education in Egypt (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Egyptians showed a concern for health and personal well-being consistent with the sense of *śḫmḫ-ib*, "making the heart forget", an expression that refers to a healthy and satisfied life. Athletic competitions are not documented as disorderly combats: some reliefs show referees intervening in combat sports, although the Egyptian referee figure lacks any distinctive attribute and is identified only by the hieroglyphic text written beside it, except in the frieze at Medinet Habu, where the referee holds a trumpet with which he presumably announced the beginning and end of the bout. There is also testimony of an earlier referee, in the tomb of Neheri, at el-Bersheh, already in the Middle Kingdom, who positions himself between the two contenders and pronounces the formula "do as you wish".
>
> Sport was also used as a means of political propaganda: in visual representations the Egyptians always appear defeating Nubians, Libyans and Asiatics, staging their military superiority over their enemies. Decker (2021, p. 37) notes that these scenes do not prefigure the spirit of a modern international competition, they aim to demonstrate, before the very foreign ambassadors present among the audience, the superiority of Egypt.
>
> Women took part in disciplines of agility, rhythm and flexibility, acrobatic gymnastics, juggling, dance and swimming, documented above all at Beni Hasan (Elhosary, 2025). They do not appear represented in combat sports. The on-screen claim about queens and princesses who received physical and even military training in private has not been located in the working material reviewed for this topic, and what the record clearly documents is the absence of female representation in those disciplines, an absence that does not amount to a prohibition stated by any source.
>
> For the essay, spectacle, insofar as combat refereed and observed by a foreign audience formed part of the program of state ceremonies.

> Futrell, A., y Scanlon, T. F. (Eds.). (2021). *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world*. Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 15 · Dos imágenes · The social division of Egyptian sport

**`Notas del orador`**

> Access to physical-sporting practice was conditioned by social stratification, which encouraged or restricted an activity depending on birth (Decker, 2017). The pharaoh was subject to the principle that the topic's working material calls *victor perpetuus*, a term Decker himself (2021, p. 31 and p. 33) uses: as guarantor of cosmic order, he could not be defeated in public, and for that reason he was forbidden to compete in open contests of uncertain outcome, since any unforeseen event would have violated the belief system that sustained the kingdom. His physical activity was limited to solo demonstrations, horse taming, hunting, handling the war chariot, archery at copper targets or the heb-sed ritual.
>
> The aristocracy and the noble elite hunted as a pastime in private reserves stocked with waterfowl, gazelles and antelopes, with the help of trained dogs, unlike the peasantry, who hunted out of necessity. Nobles also attended sacred celebrations as spectators, or held their own banquets with dancers, musicians and acrobats, and their children received, in courtly education or *kap*, physical instruction in swimming, wrestling, archery and weapons handling oriented toward future military service.
>
> Common people had little time and energy after long working days, although diversions such as boat jousting derived from that same work. On special occasions, after the completion of great public works, the pharaoh could organize athletic competitions among his workers, with prizes for the winners in archery, wrestling or fencing.
>
> For the essay, marker of distinction, given that the same physical gesture, shooting an arrow, hunting or rowing, changes meaning according to the social stratum of whoever performs it.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (2017). Some aspects of sport in ritual and religion in ancient Egypt. *Arys: Antigüedad: Religiones y Sociedades*, (15), 11-20.
> Decker, W. (2021). Sport in ancient Egypt and the ancient Near East. En A. Futrell y T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 31-46). Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 17 · Imagen izquierda · The royal dogma

**`Notas del orador`**

> The pharaoh is, according to the Egyptian conception of history, the very guarantor of life: as protector of the country he disarms his enemies, condemned to impotence and defeat, and that role presupposes in advance an existence distinguished by power and strength, essential conditions of his rule. It is precisely there that his sporting character originates (Decker, 2021, p. 33).
>
> From that dogma derives the *victor perpetuus*, "perpetual victor" (Decker, 2021, p. 31): the pharaoh is always victorious by theological definition, competes alone, without rival, accumulates records, displays feats and orders them recorded for posterity, so that across three millennia not a single reference to a royal defeat survives. The exact formulation of the taboo is limited to competitions whose outcome was uncertain, since the mere theoretical possibility of defeat was incompatible with the dogma of a king who has no equals (Decker, 2021, p. 33).
>
> The only explicit exception to that taboo corresponds to Amenhotep II (1428-1397 BCE), whom Decker (2021, p. 33) calls "the athlete on the royal throne" for the abundance of testimonies attributed to him. The taboo affects only the pharaoh: the ordinary Egyptian could compete, as shown by the strata of the army and the common people discussed in the previous section.
>
> For the essay, collective identity, insofar as the king's undefeated body embodies and guarantees, before his subjects, the continuity of the order of the world.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (1992). *Sports and games of ancient Egypt* (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.
> Decker, W. (2021). Sport in ancient Egypt and the ancient Near East. En A. Futrell y T. F. Scanlon (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (pp. 31-46). Oxford University Press.

### Diapositiva 18 · Destacado 3 · The Heb-Sed festival

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Heb-Sed is Egypt's most important jubilee festival, and it served to renew the pharaoh's power. *Heb* means "festival" according to Gardiner's translation (1926, p. 145, cited in Caravaca, 2014), while the meaning of *Sed* has not been established by Egyptology. It was ideally celebrated in the thirtieth year of a reign and repeated thereafter every three years, although the periodicity varied over time: Osorkon II celebrated his first *sed-festival* in the twenty-second year of his reign, around 865 BCE (Galán, 2000), and Ramesses II celebrated as many as fourteen jubilees during his sixty-six years of reign, between 1279 and 1213 BCE (Caravaca, 2014, p. 47). Far from being an isolated episode, the rite has 314 documented instances across the thirty-one dynasties, and also in the Ptolemaic and Roman periods (Decker, 2021, pp. 33 and 35).
>
> The king's physical aging compromised his role as guarantor of the order of the world, and the jubilee restored that figure's fullness: Decker (2021, p. 33) holds that the king "demonstrated the physical powers he still retained" and "proved his continued qualification to be king", so that the rite requalifies and renews rather than subjects him to an examination with the possibility of suspension, which is not documented in the sources reviewed. During the celebration, moreover, the gods descended to Earth to renew the pharaoh's strength and vitality (Caravaca, 2014, p. 45).
>
> The ceremony included the double coronation with the crowns of Upper and Lower Egypt, the symbolic union of the papyrus and the lily, the shooting of arrows toward the four cardinal points to exemplify dominion over the whole kingdom, and the raising of a Djed pillar, associated with the spine of Osiris and a symbol of the resistance of the State (Caravaca, 2014, p. 58), an element that regains prominence in the stick and fist fighting contests that the festival hosts in later periods.
>
> For the essay, rite, given that the entire ceremony reenacts the king's coronation and symbolically renews his bond with the cosmic order.

### Diapositiva 19 · Imagen izquierda · The Heb-Sed festival (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> The central element of the festival was the pharaoh's race, obligatory in its performance, which was run in the southern court of the sacred precinct of the pyramid of Dyeser, at Saqqara. The track consisted of a straight stretch of fifty-five meters, about one hundred Egyptian cubits, capped by two semicircular boundary markers, with a complete circuit of one hundred and forty meters that the king covered from north to south and from south to north, symbolizing the union of the Two Lands (Decker, 1992, p. 29; Decker, 2021, p. 31). The pharaoh is depicted running with wide strides, dressed in a kilt, crown and scepter of power, at times accompanied by the sacred bull Apis, whose presence, according to different readings, served to mark the pace or to transmit to the king its magical power.
>
> Queen Hatshepsut, of the Eighteenth Dynasty, is depicted accompanied by Apis performing the ritual jubilee race, dressed as a pharaoh and carrying the flail, the *mekes* and the white crown of Upper Egypt, in a relief from the Red Chapel of the temple of Karnak (Caravaca, 2014, p. 55). Sources centered on her political biography place that celebration in the sixteenth year of her reign, around 1485 BCE, a chronology that Egyptologists explain by assuming that the queen added to her count the years in which she served as great royal wife and regent before being proclaimed pharaoh, when she was forty years old (Camilo, 2024). The scene documents that a female ruler, too, had to display her physical strength in the same terms as a male king, although Guttmann (2004, p. 12) points out that it is questionable whether she, or any of the male pharaohs, actually performed the feat that the image represents.
>
> For the essay, collective identity, insofar as the ritual race, run alone before a sacred precinct, stages the link between the monarch's body and the territory he governs.

### Diapositiva 20 · Jerarquía de Viñetas · Funerary complex of Dyeser, Saqqara

**`Notas del orador`**

> The funerary complex of Dyeser, at Saqqara, dates from the middle of the third millennium BCE, between 2690 and 2670 BCE according to the dating given by Decker (2021, p. 33), and constitutes the oldest sports installation still standing. The caveat that Decker himself (1992, p. 29; 2021, p. 33) states is that this is a stone replica, built so that the dead king could celebrate his jubilees eternally, and not the actual setting of the festival, which was erected with perishable material and dismantled after each celebration. The Great South Court, indicated in yellow in the image, preserves the two semicircular boundary markers that close off the jubilee race track, some fifty-five meters apart according to Decker's measurement (1992, p. 29). The north-south orientation of the course reinforces the symbolism of a race to the extremes of the pharaoh's dominion.
>
> For the essay, rite, given that the best-preserved sports installation of all pharaonic Egypt was built for funerary use and not for a competition among the living.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (1992). *Sports and games of ancient Egypt* (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 21 · Jerarquía de Viñetas · Archery against copper targets

**`Notas del orador`**

> The favorite discipline of royal sport was archery against ingots of raw copper used in eastern Mediterranean trade, between two and four centimeters thick and around twenty kilos in weight. This practice flourished in the New Kingdom with the introduction of the composite bow (Decker, 2021, p. 35), which the Hyksos manufactured and which the Egyptians began to produce after the wars of liberation against them.
>
> Unlike the simple bow, carved from a single piece of wood, generally acacia, the composite bow was made by combining a wooden core reinforced with animal horn and sinew joined with organic glue, whose drying could take up to a year. It represented a military revolution by being lighter and more manageable in war chariots, and allowed arrows to be shot up to three hundred meters, compared with one hundred and fifty for the simple bow (Cook, 2018). Nearly three dozen bows come from the tomb of Tutankhamun, among them one with a spare double string and another of reduced size, resembling a child's weapon.
>
> The literature introduces a decisive nuance regarding the meaning of this feat: the copper discs that New Kingdom kings supposedly pierced with their arrows did indeed exist, although it was impossible to penetrate them with an arrow, since not even the composite bow, for all its elasticity, produced sufficient force to do so (Decker and Klauck, 1974, cited in Decker, 2021, p. 35). Only the ideal king, and not a real archer, was capable of that feat, so that the trial documents less a ballistic limit than an ideological obligation.
>
> For the essay, collective identity, insofar as the king's impossible feat certifies to his subjects that the order of the world remains guaranteed.

### Diapositiva 22 · Jerarquía de Viñetas · The impossible records

**`Notas del orador`**

> The table of royal records of the New Kingdom documents that each king surpasses the previous one without any of them ever failing, with the sole exception of Amenofis II, who matches exactly the record of his father Tutmosis III, thereby devaluing it through that equality (Decker, 2021, p. 35). Decker and Klauck (1974, cited in Decker, 2021, p. 35) simulated the shot with modern bows and arrows against copper plates, and the arrows penetrated only a few millimeters, confirming that the Egyptian feat was materially unachievable with the available technology. It follows that a source can be formally impeccable in its wording and materially false in its content, and that this evidence attests that the notion of a record, understood as standardized material, precise measurement and witnesses, was present in Egyptian culture, even though the record itself did not correspond to an event that actually occurred (Decker, 2021, p. 35).
>
> Amenofis II (1428-1397 BCE) holds the largest number of records in this series: the stela itself states that the target was three fingers thick and that the king pierced it so that several handspans of the arrow shaft protruded from the back, before a large crowd of spectators (Decker, 2021, p. 36). An inscription found at Medamud, near Luxor, in a sanctuary of the war god Montu, further reports that the monarch surpassed his own record by having seven ninths of the shaft pierce the plate on the first attempt, in a competition open to anyone who wished to repeat it, with none of those present succeeding. It is the only occasion in three millennia on which a pharaoh appears as challenger in an athletic contest, and it confirms the dogma of invincibility precisely through the incapacity of the rivals summoned.
>
> For the essay, collective identity, insofar as the Egyptian record certifies, irrespective of its material veracity, the king's superiority before his court and his subjects.

### Diapositiva 23 · Destacado 6 · The chariot and the horses

**`Notas del orador`**

> Egyptian chariots, light, flexible and fast, with a frame of ash or acacia wood and a body covered in goat hide, wheels one meter in diameter with four or six spokes and a total weight of around twenty-four kilos, reached, when drawn by two horses, a maximum speed of thirty-eight kilometers per hour (Serrano, 2021). The light two-wheeled spoked chariot arrives with the Hyksos and becomes a status symbol at the royal court, although Decker (2021, p. 35) points out that its importance as an instrument of war is usually overestimated, and that its popularity is also explained by the breeding of fine horses, decisive for the warrior who fought from the chariot.
>
> The Sphinx Stela documents that Amenofis II, while still crown prince, adjusted the harness at Memphis and rode the horses in circles beside the Great Sphinx, gazing at the pyramids, determined to know their nature and to become expert in controlling them, horses that Tutmosis III had given him from his own stable (Decker, 2021, p. 35). The chariot was used chiefly for war and for hunting: Tutmosis III is recorded as having hunted one hundred and twenty wild bulls in one hour and seven lions in the blink of an eye, and Amenofis III, one hundred and two lions in his first ten years of reign and fifty-six wild bulls in a single day.
>
> The absence of chariot races noted on the screen agrees with the literature: no source documenting chariot races in Egypt has yet been located, and as far as the pharaoh is concerned, the same taboo against competitions of uncertain outcome that governs the rest of his physical activity must be assumed (Decker, 2021, p. 35). What is solidly documented is driving, the training of horses and shooting from the chariot.
>
> For the essay, training for war, given that handling the chariot requires mastering both the bow and the control of horses in motion at the same time, a skill of directly military application.

### Diapositiva 25 · Hunting in the marshes

**`Notas del orador`**

> The documentation of aristocratic hunting comes almost entirely from the stone chambers of the nobility, which withstood the passage of time better than modest tombs; Decker (1992), in the introduction to his chapter on private individuals, notes that a complete social history of this stratum remains impossible owing to the lack of prior studies, a limitation that conditions everything that can be said about non-royal sport in Egypt. ### Diapositiva 26 · Courtly education and swimming

**`Notas del orador`**

> The term kap designates both the palace precinct where the king's children were raised together with those of the high nobility and the instruction, physical training included, that they received there. The tomb biography of Khety, nomarch of Siut at the end of the third millennium BCE, is the central textual evidence: the king himself allowed him to take swimming lessons (nbj) together with his own children, and Decker (1992) stresses that this mention is unique in the corpus of tomb biographies, precisely what makes it reflect a historical reality rather than a repeated rhetorical formula. The comparison that Decker opens with is instructive: swimming did not occupy in Egyptian education the place that Plato reserves for it in the Laws (3.689d) as knowledge that every educated person had to master, although the Egyptian testimony suggests that mastering it was associated with a higher cultural level. The lessons were given by a noble recognized for his skill, a procedure comparable to the one followed by the young Amenofis II when the nomarch Min of This taught him archery.
>
> Regarding styles, Decker (1992) notes that the alternating-arm propulsion visible in hieroglyphs and vessel finials of the Old Kingdom cannot simply be equated with the modern crawl, because that same alternation is what any untrained swimmer produces; the attribution of the breaststroke, first proposed and later revised by E. Mehl toward the butterfly, receives from Decker the objection that the discovery of a technique in the twentieth century proves nothing about its existence millennia earlier. It is likely that a large part of the population living along the Nile knew how to swim out of practical necessity, particularly fishermen and boatmen, without this implying competition: swimming trials were, both in Egypt and in Greece, extraordinary exceptions.
>
> For the essay, the slide supports the function of distinction: exclusivity lies in the formal lesson received from an instructor in a courtly setting, not in the mastery of the skill, which was common among those who lived off the river.

> Decker, W. (1992). Sports and games of ancient Egypt (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 30 · Combat sports

**`Notas del orador`**

> Decker (1992) organizes Egyptian combat into three contexts that operate simultaneously rather than in succession. The first is military training, with wrestling painted above scenes of an assault on a fortress in tombs 15 and 17 at Beni Hasan. The second is ceremony before the king, with combats held at tribute receptions, in the Amarna block and beneath the window of appearances of Ramesses III. The third is religious cult: at the third Sed festival of Amenhotep III, depicted in the tomb of Kheruef, numerous pairs fight with papyrus stalks while the Djed pillar, symbol of the resurrection of Osiris, is raised, so that combat serves the ritual regeneration of the king.
>
> The three disciplines the screen lists, wrestling, stick fencing (tahtib) and boxing, have very uneven bodies of evidence: wrestling dominates the record owing to its abundance at Beni Hasan, fencing gains popularity in the New Kingdom and often appears associated with wrestling in the same programs, and boxing is reduced to a single source, the tomb of Kheruef. Poliakoff (1987) qualifies Decker's picture by arguing that the Egyptians came to hold formal fencing competitions, with crowds of spectators documented in the iconography.
>
> For the essay, the slide functions as a map of the training-for-war function and the ritual function, which the following block unfolds discipline by discipline.

### Diapositiva 31 · d) Combat sports (II)

**`Notas del orador`**

> Wrestling is, of all the Egyptian disciplines, the one with the best visual documentation: representations of pairs survive from the beginning of the third millennium to the end of the New Kingdom, in the eleventh century BC, which for Decker (1992) suggests the high prestige of the discipline. Of the four tombs at Beni Hasan decorated with wrestling scenes, that of Baqet III gathers, on the east wall, two hundred and nineteen pairs painted at almost forty centimeters and distinguished by a color code ranging from dark brown to light, a device without which it would scarcely be possible to disentangle the holds. The documented techniques, shoulder throws, hip rolls, pulls and trips, contrast with the systematic absence of punches, bites or severe joint locks, which points to an unwritten limit on the combat. The percentage of ground combat that appears on screen is not found with that figure in the bibliography consulted for these notes.
>
> On refereeing, the screen presents two distinct scenes back to back that are worth distinguishing. At Beni Hasan and in the tomb of Neheri, at el-Bersheh, the referee bears no attribute marking him out: he is a figure standing beside the pair, identifiable only by the hieroglyphic text written next to him, and it is there that the phrase "do as you wish" is documented, which Decker (1992) interprets as the permission given to the wrestler in an advantageous position to exploit it as best he can. The referee with a trumpet belongs to a different and much later scene, that of Medinet Habu, under the reign of Ramesses III, and is the only case in which refereeing is seen as well as read. On how victory was decided, Decker (1992) admits that the information still eludes us, while Poliakoff (1987) proposes that the Egyptian fall required only a touch of the back or shoulder, based on scenes in which one wrestler turns his rival face up.
>
> For the essay, the slide documents at once the training-for-war function, through the dress and the mural arrangement that link it to the army, and the regulated-spectacle function, through the confirmed presence of refereeing.

### Diapositiva 33 · Combat sports (III)

**`Notas del orador`**

> Stick fencing, which the Egyptians felt to be related to wrestling to the point of including both in the same programs, is documented exclusively among soldiers, as the military kilt present in every representation reveals (Decker, 1992). The surviving sticks measure around one meter, with small guards to protect the hand and leather protectors on the forehead, chin and forearm; some variants show the stick held with both hands or a weapon in each. Besides its instructional function, Decker documents ritual combats at court before the pharaoh that symbolized the youth and regeneration of the monarch, such as the one that frames the raising of the Djed pillar at the jubilee of Amenhotep III, and combats offered as part of the ceremonial before foreign ambassadors.
>
> Boxing has a much more marginal presence: the only indisputable evidence comes from the tomb of Kheruef, in western Thebes, with six pairs of bare-knuckle boxers also dressed in the soldiers' kilt. Decker (1992) observes that four of those figures reproduce fairly accurately the postures of the dancers depicted opposite them, from which he concludes that the artist, otherwise skilled, was not familiar with boxing, and that the discipline was probably occasional in Egypt; the reading of the high guard shown on screen, somewhere between archaic head strikes and a stylization close to dance, captures that same ambiguity in the source.
>
> For the essay, fencing supports the training-for-war function and, in its courtly aspect, the ritual function; boxing, owing to the scarcity and ambiguity of its single source, does not allow us to go beyond confirming its occasional existence.

### Diapositiva 35 · The long-distance race

**`Notas del orador`**

> The stela, dated with unusual precision between December 6, 685 and January 5, 684 BC, comes from the sixth regnal year of Taharqa and was erected in the western desert, in a place called "Way of the Camp." Decker (1992) regards it as his single most important source on running in ancient Egypt, precisely because it rarely offers quantified results, something uncommon in Egyptian sport: the elite troops, organized into five sections that trained daily, covered the hundred kilometers of the trial in about nine hours of effective running, not counting the mandatory two-hour rest at the turning point, figures that Decker compares with the marks of professional British runners of the late nineteenth century.
>
> That Taharqa stepped down from his chariot and ran a stretch alongside the troops, a detail noted on screen, is documented by Pruett (2026) as an episode at odds with the pharaoh's usual behavior in sporting iconography; if confirmed by a reading of the original text, it would constitute a second exception to the principle that the king does not compete directly with his subjects, alongside the already known case of Amenhotep II's archery at Medamud. Decker (1992) adds a political reading of the episode: Taharqa, of Kushite origin, reigned over native Egyptian troops, and the recruitment of those light troops for the trial may have reflected an integration strategy on the part of a king aware of his foreign status.
>
> For the essay, the slide documents with the greatest precision available anywhere in the topic the training-for-war function, and secondarily the collective-identity function, through the reading of the race as an instrument of cohesion between native Egyptians and the reigning Nubian house.

### Diapositiva 38 · Fishermen's joust

**`Notas del orador`**

> The contest brought together two to four crews in papyrus-reed canoes with three to six men each, naked or wearing a short kilt: those at the stern kept the fragile platform balanced while those at the bow used the navigation poles to unbalance their rivals, resorting to direct grappling with pushing and pulling when the boats came too close together. Decker (1992) describes the challenge as beginning with playful intent and often ending in bloody duels, to the point that even the calmest bystanders found it hard to separate those who grew heated; the cry «open his skull» recorded in the inscriptions comes precisely from this marsh context. There was no fixed set of rules, and the rules were revised from one occasion to another according to the mood of the participants; Decker further rejects L. Klebs's hypothesis of a joust conducted under fixed rules, a hypothesis supported by an unequal number of crew members between the two boats.
>
> The single scene in which one crew declines the challenge of the neighboring boat constitutes, for Decker (1992), the first documented instance of a sporting withdrawal. Guttmann (2004) regards the practice as one of the best examples of how everyday labor can naturally develop into a team sporting discipline, arising spontaneously as amusement or rough play to measure strength among young men.
>
> For the essay, the slide illustrates a practice that preceded institutionalization, sustained by improvised spectacle rather than by the patronage of any authority.

### Diapositiva 41 · The body as trade: dance

**`Notas del orador`**

> Egyptian dance was practiced almost entirely by professionals, and served at once to enliven the sacred festivals held in honor of the gods, the private banquets of the elites, and, in funerary contexts, to stimulate the resurrection of the deceased. Diab (2021) documents the lute players of the Eighteenth Dynasty tombs as professional musicians who played and danced at the same time, a regular presence at banquets and depicted naked or nearly naked, a nudity the author explains as an artistic device of religious connotation linked to the regeneration of the deceased rather than as a sign of degraded social standing.
>
> The point sustaining the slide is social mobility: artistic activities were carried out by members of the common people who, thanks to their motor skills, could advance within a closed society, something that in Egypt only writing and the body could offer. Belonging to the dance, acrobatic, music or juggling troupes of temples and palaces secured for whoever achieved it a better diet, home and security for himself and his family, so that the entertainment of the elite became, for the performer, a vehicle of advancement.
>
> For the essay, the dominant function is that of distinction, understood in a sense inverse to the usual one, as a narrow door that a bodily skill allows one to pass through rather than as a barrier that excludes.

### Diapositiva 42 · The body as trade: acrobatics and juggling

**`Notas del orador`**

> The documented acrobatic scenes, with the round-off and the front flip as the most common movements, are concentrated in the New Kingdom, when this exercise was incorporated as a regular part of dance; Decker (1992) notes that, although some representations seem to show simply a bridge, what is probably shown is a front flip, executed exclusively by female dancers whose slender, trained bodies were dressed only in a skirt. ### Diapositiva 44 · Games: senet

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Egyptian term snt, meaning to pass or to cross, gives its name to the most documented game in the whole of Egyptian history by the number of references across time; Decker (1992) also calls it the «game of the thirty squares», after the board of thirty squares in three rows of ten along which the pieces move in a zigzag, with the capture of isolated pieces and blocking when several pieces in a row close off the path. It was played across all social classes and in all periods, on supports ranging from simple incisions on a flat stone to the elaborately worked chests with two playable surfaces from the tomb of Tutankhamun, and with four sticks or two lamb astragals used in place of dice.
>
> The claim on the slide regarding equality between the sexes finds specific support in Arnette (2022), who studies the representations of female players in the Theban region of the New Kingdom as a singular case within Egyptian iconography. In that same period the board acquires, in addition to its function as everyday entertainment, a religious meaning: it becomes a habitual funerary object and symbolizes the deceased's journey through the underworld, so that success in the game played against an imaginary opponent guaranteed rebirth, a precondition of eternal life (Decker, 1992). The mhn serpent from the game of the same name eventually became associated with senet in the Nineteenth Dynasty, and a spell from the Book of the Dead confirms the protective sense of the game won against it.
>
> For the essay, the slide supports at once the function of ritual, owing to the funerary meaning of the board, and that of distinction, given that this is the only practice in which the Egyptian woman appears documented on an equal footing with the man.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (1992). Sports and games of ancient Egypt (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.
> Arnette, M. L. (2022). Representations of female senet players in ancient Egypt (Theban region, New Kingdom): A proposed interpretation. Clio, 56(2), 127-141.

### Diapositiva 45 · Other games

**`Notas del orador`**

> The balls preserved in Egyptian tombs, of varied sizes and materials, appear in the iconography practiced almost exclusively by women and young girls, documented at Beni Hasan in registers that share a wall with spinning and weaving; Herrador (2021), citing Gardiner (1930), notes that it is difficult to assume that these games were not also popular among young people of both sexes, even though boys had, in parallel, a repertoire of their own. The four documented types, juggling with up to three balls, the piggyback game with throws between mounted pairs, rhythmic passes with clapping, and high throws caught in the air, sometimes while balancing on one leg, form the most complete catalogue of children's and women's play in the entire Egyptian corpus.
>
> Regarding the material of the balls, the literature adds a relevant qualification: the solid pieces or those filled with leather, reed or straw were the ones intended for play, while the hollow faience balls, more fragile, were used above all as votive offerings to the gods (Herrador, 2021).
>
> The hoop game, of which a single representation exists in the whole of documented Egyptian history, has been mistakenly compared to hockey, as Decker (1992) points out: it is not played with a ball and there is no indication that it was a team game, so the comparison cannot be sustained beyond the superficial resemblance of the curved sticks.
>
> For the essay, the slide supports a function of gender distinction within the common people itself: among the practices of the popular stratum, ball play is marked as territory that is almost exclusively feminine.

> Decker, W. (1992). Sports and games of ancient Egypt (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 46 · Why does pharaonic sport fade out?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The argument that closes the Egyptian section links two ideas that run through the whole topic: the pharaoh's sport depends on his status as guarantor of cosmic order and on a state apparatus capable of recording his feats with the precision demanded by the logic of the record, precision that Decker (1992) documents extensively with regard to the archery and running stelae. When Egypt passes into Persian, Macedonian and Roman hands, and definitively when the annexation of 31 BCE turns it into a province of the Roman Empire, the ideological and religious structure that sustained these physical demonstrations disappears along with the king, and with it the type of sport that depended on the royal person. This causal formulation is a synthesis built on the whole of Decker's work rather than a literal quotation from him, and in class it is preferable to present it as one's own interpretation rather than as a textual conclusion of the source.
>
> What survives is, precisely, what never depended on the palace: stick fencing reaches contemporary Egypt turned into a folk act with no military attribution, and the children's games documented in the tombs of Beni Hasan and Saqqara find, as the slide notes, equivalents in the streets of present-day Egypt. The asymmetry that the slide sums up, the practices that power invents last only as long as power lasts, and those born of work and play last much longer, closes the whole block on the court, the army, the people and games: the more a practice depends on state patronage, royal dogma, a professional army, state festivals, the more fragile it turns out to be when that state changes hands, and the more it is born of everyday labor, fishermen's contests, soldiers' fencing turned into custom, children's play, the longer its historical continuity.
>
> For the essay, this slide works as a cross-cutting close to both the function of distinction and that of collective identity: pharaonic sport identified the king with the order of the world, and its disappearance marks the exact limit of that identification.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Decker, W. (1992). Sports and games of ancient Egypt (A. Guttmann, Trad.). Yale University Press.

### Diapositiva 50 · Space and empire

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Iranian plateau borders to the northeast on the steppes of Central Asia, where the horse was domesticated millennia before Persia existed, and from there comes the most enduring trait of its sporting culture. The Achaemenid empire begins in 550 BCE, when Cyrus II defeats the Medes, and within three generations extends from the Aegean to the Indus, becoming the largest state known up to that time. Four elements sustain it: cavalry, the bow, the education of the nobility and the management of distance, organized through provinces called satrapies and a network of roads. Herodotus (5.52-53) describes the Royal Road between Sardis and Susa, running some two thousand five hundred kilometers with a hundred and eleven relay stations, and calculates ninety days of travel on foot.
>
> The library material specifies a point that the slide does not cover: those royal roads were not paved. What distinguished them from secondary roads was their maintenance, the regularity of the relay stations and their width, needed to admit carts and baggage trains, so that the image of the paved road comes from Rome and is transferred to Persia by contagion (Henkelman y Jacobs, 2021, pp. 726 y 729). The standing corps of ten thousand soldiers accompanying the king, the so-called Immortals, receives that name only in Greek sources, specifically in Herodotus (7.83), and no Persian inscription uses it.
>
> For the essay, the monarch's divine legitimacy, which obliges him to travel in person through the corners of the empire and constantly demonstrate his physical capacity, already sustains the function of ritual that will run through the whole section devoted to royalty.

### Diapositiva 51 · How many Persias are there?

**`Notas del orador`**

> Between the empire of Darius and Xerxes, that of Thermopylae, and the last Persia lie two thousand five hundred years, so that speaking of Persian sport without specifying the period is like grouping the Iberians, Roman Hispania, Al-Andalus and the Spain of Charles V under a single label. The specialized literature distinguishes six successive layers, Achaemenid, Seleucid and Parthian, Sasanian, Islamic, Safavid and Qajar, and contemporary, and much of what textbooks popularize as ancient Persian sport actually belongs to the layers after the fall of the Achaemenids in 330 BCE.
>
> What remains stable across the six layers comes down to three elements, the horse, the bow and wrestling, as is confirmed convergently by studies devoted to the different periods of Iranian sport (Chehabi, 2003; Chehabi y Guttmann, 2002; Krawietz, 2013). What changes in each period is who practices these three things, to what end and what they mean, and that change orders the whole block.

### Diapositiva 52 · The problem of the sources

**`Notas del orador`**

> No description of ancient Iranian sport written by a Persian survives, although Persian sources of another kind do exist: administrative tablets from Persepolis, royal inscriptions that speak of the monarch's body, and reliefs that show him hunting. What is missing is an account of how a young man was trained, and that gap is filled by Greek authors. Herodotus (1.136) writes that Persian youths were trained between the ages of five and twenty in riding, shooting the bow and telling the truth, and Strabo (15.3.18) expands on this three centuries later with details of military instruction and extends it to the age of twenty-four. The most extensive source is the *Cyropaedia*, which Xenophon, an Athenian soldier and disciple of Socrates, wrote around 370 BCE about the life of Cyrus II, king between 559 and 530 BCE. It runs to eight books in which hunting expeditions, hygiene, life on campaign, athletic contests, training for combat and court life all appear.
>
> It is, however, a work of fiction with a political and pedagogical purpose, written a century and a half after the death of Cyrus and addressed to Greek readers, to whom it offers a model of the ruler, so that the image of the Persian body that has come down to the present was constructed by Greeks, in Greek, for Greeks, after decades of military hostility. A documented example of that distance is offered by Onesicritus, cited by Strabo (15.3.8), who read the funerary inscription of Darius I as if it said "as a hunter I prevailed," a phrase the text does not contain and which Almagor (2021, pp. 1107-1120) attributes either to an interpreter's error or to a deliberate Greek embellishment.
>
> For the essay, this slide warns that much of what is known about the Persian body comes from an external and interested gaze, a condition that runs through the reading of every practice in the block.

### Diapositiva 54 · The king and physical-sporting practices

**`Notas del orador`**

> Persian sport begins at the top. The king rules by divine designation, and that legitimacy requires being demonstrated through the body, which explains why the monarchy turns the bow and hunting into a matter of state. Both practices share three traits that set them apart from any other: they are performed before a crowd, they demand years of training and, above all, their outcome depends solely on the king himself, so that no one can defeat him.
>
> The specialized literature offers an interpretive key that the slide does not develop and that underpins the whole section. Jacobs (2021, pp. 758-759) observes that Achaemenid royal images, even where not serene, lack dramatic tension and systematically leave out situations of trial, such as war or the hunt, because legitimizing the ruler through those activities was not their purpose. Royal art shows capability and never shows competition, so that the king is legitimized not by winning but by being capable. That principle explains both the Achaemenid layer, to be studied in the following two slides, and its reappearance eight hundred years later under the Sasanians.
>
> For the essay, this slide sustains the function of distinction in its most radical form, because it constructs a body whose only possible audience is victory, given that losing is excluded by definition from the royal repertoire.

> Kyle, D. G. (2015). *Sport and spectacle in the ancient world* (2.ª ed.). Wiley Blackwell.

### Diapositiva 55 · The king's body, written in stone

**`Notas del orador`**

> At Naqsh-e Rostam, near Persepolis, Darius I (522-486 BC) had a text known as DNb carved on the facade of his own tomb, written in Old Persian, Elamite and Akkadian, in which he speaks of himself in the first person: his body is strong and trained of hands and feet, as a horseman he is a good horseman, as an archer he is a good archer and as a spearman he is a good spearman, on foot and on horseback. The text itself makes clear that these capacities come from Ahura Mazda, so that the sovereign's physical superiority is presented as a divine gift, in a scheme that repeats point by point the Egyptian one, an enumeration of bodily feats, attribution to the divinity and an inscription on a funerary monument meant to endure.
>
> There is an omission that the slide itself points out and that the specialized literature confirms. The king appears as horseman, archer and spearman, and never as wrestler, because wrestling is the contest whose outcome remains uncertain until the end, and a king cannot expose himself to losing before his subjects. This is the same taboo as the pharaoh *victor perpetuus*. The literature adds a further point about the text's intended audience that the slide does not include: Jacobs (2021) notes that the DNb expressly names the successors to the throne as its addressees, and that a good part of the royal inscriptions were placed at heights that made them practically illegible, so that Darius's catalogue of feats functions more as a training program for the heir than as propaganda aimed at the people.
>
> For the essay, the inscription supports at once the function of distinction, because it closes off to any subject the possibility of equaling the king in that bodily repertoire, and the function of rite, because it presents the capable body as a gift bestowed by the divinity.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Alcock, S. E. (Ed.). (2001). *Empires: Perspectives from archaeology and history*. Cambridge University Press.
> Jacobs, B. (2021). Achaemenid art: Art in the Achaemenid Empire. En B. Jacobs y R. Rollinger (Eds.), *A companion to the Achaemenid Persian Empire* (Vol. I, pp. 758-759). Wiley Blackwell.

### Diapositiva 56 · Royal hunting and the paradeisos

**`Notas del orador`**

> The Assyrian kings had turned lion hunting into a state liturgy, and the Persians inherit the practice, though they introduce an institution of their own, the *paradeisos*, an enclosed park stocked with animals whose name, from the Old Persian *paridaida*, "enclosed space", passes into Greek and reaches the English word "paradise". Hunts took place several times a month with bow, short sword, shield and two spears, and the practice served three functions at once, military training, a display of power before embassies and subjects, and symbolic control over nature. The royal hunt carried real danger, as the episodes of Artaxerxes II, who nearly died in one, of Darius I, who injured his foot dismounting in haste, and of Smerdis, killed during a hunt, all show, so that the absence of hunting episodes in the later sources has been read as a sign of the monarchy's decline.
>
> The literature qualifies a point that the slide does not develop and that proves significant for understanding Persian royal art. The hunting motif is conspicuously absent from the palace reliefs and Achaemenid royal inscriptions, and appears instead on objects of restricted circulation, seals, gems from Asia Minor and coins from the western satrapies (Almagor, 2021, pp. 1107-1120), so that the king who hunts the most is also the one who least allows himself to be depicted hunting in the official medium. The episode of Megabyzus, recorded on the slide, illustrates the same principle as wrestling: during a hunt with Artaxerxes I, the courtier speared the lion before the king did, and the king, furious that he had killed the animal before him, ordered his beheading.
>
> For the essay, the royal hunt supports the function of training for war through its tactical and physical component, and at the same time the function of distinction, because the right to strike first belonged exclusively to the monarch.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Almagor, E. (2021). Hunting and leisure activities. En B. Jacobs y R. Rollinger (Eds.), *A companion to the Achaemenid Persian Empire* (Vol. II, pp. 1107-1120). Wiley Blackwell.

### Diapositiva 58 · Persian physical education

**`Notas del orador`**

> Herodotus (1.136) writes that Persian youths were educated between the ages of five and twenty in horseback riding, archery and telling the truth, and Strabo (15.3.18-19), three centuries later, extends the period to twenty-four years and adds the detail that the boys were grouped fifty at a time under an instructor, ran distances of thirty or forty stadia, and learned to withstand heat, cold and rain, to ford rivers while keeping their clothes and weapons dry, and to sleep in the open feeding on wild fruit. Xenophon (*Cyropaedia*, 1.2.2-8) adds the existence of public schools devoted chiefly to justice, with teachers appointed by the state and frugal meals.
>
> The literature firmly qualifies the three-stage system that the slide presents, with the span from six to sixteen years of age and the endurance test at twelve. No ancient source mentions that age or that test: what exists in Strabo is the formative content, heat, cold, rivers forded with weapons kept dry, with no age or test superimposed on it, and current scholarship further notes that there is no record of any properly speaking Persian school institution, it being more likely that instruction took place within the family, so that the state system that Xenophon describes could be a construction of his own (Niesiołowski-Spanò, 2021, pp. 1305-1314). Herodotus, Strabo and Xenophon, moreover, give three calendars that differ from one another, which indicates that each writes from his own particular intent and should not be taken as a univocal description of a single system.
>
> For the essay, Herodotus's threefold teaching, riding, shooting and telling the truth, clearly supports the function of character education, because it places bodily skill and moral honesty on the same plane and within a single enumeration.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Heródoto. *Historia*, 1.136.
> Estrabón. *Geografía*, 15.3.18-19.
> Jenofonte. *Ciropedia*, 1.2.2-12.

### Diapositiva 59 · Pahlavan

**`Notas del orador`**

> Between the end of the Achaemenid empire in 330 BC and the documented appearance of the *pahlavan*, thirteen hundred years pass, and over that span the trained body ceases to belong to the king and the mounted nobility and comes to settle in the Islamic city, around the bazaar and its guilds. The *pahlavan*, "the champion", is a professional fighter who earns his living with his body, performs at festivals and in public squares, protects whoever hires him and answers to a code of loyalty, honesty, humility and protection of the weak.
>
> The specialized literature specifies the social scope of the figure. Chehabi (2003, pp. 238-252) documents that the *pahlavan* formed a guild with a recognized head at the very court, and that they traveled through a region extending from North Africa to Central Asia and from the Balkans to Bengal, which makes the champion a professional institution of imperial scale rather than a merely local figure. In time, as the slide notes, the profession degraded, and not a few *pahlavan* ended up living off petty crime in the service of whoever could pay for it.
>
> For the essay, the *pahlavan* marks the transition from the function of distinction, proper to the royal body of the previous slides, to the function of commodity, since his trained body becomes a means of livelihood within a guild trade recognized by the court.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

### Diapositiva 60 · Zurkhaneh

**`Notas del orador`**

> The zurkhaneh, "the house of strength", is a traditional domed Iranian gymnasium, with a central pit of soft clay and a deliberately low entrance door, so that whoever passes through must bend down, a gesture interpreted as a lesson in humility before exercise. UNESCO inscribed it in 2010 as a practice combining elements of Islam, gnosticism and ancient Persian beliefs.
>
> The slide already rightly warns that the legend of a clandestine origin tied to resistance against Arabs and Mongols has no foundation, and the literature confirms this with a specific date and with a formulation worth citing as it stands: Krawietz (2013, pp. 144-166) writes that the historical background of the zurkhaneh lacks clear evidence prior to the seventeenth century, and that it became conventional wisdom that they arose from the clandestine resistance of Iranian patriots, without there being historical evidence for it. The earliest dated testimony is that of the traveler Chardin, in the 1670s, and the oldest surviving building is a Safavid one, in Bam, Kerman province. Between the Achaemenid king who never appears wrestling and this guild gymnasium, the distance is more than two thousand two hundred years.
>
> For the essay, the zurkhaneh already supports the function of collective identity that will become explicit at the close of the block, because an urban guild gymnasium ends up, centuries later, turned into heritage inscribed by an international body.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

### Diapositiva 61 · The session

**`Notas del orador`**

> The zurkhaneh session follows a fixed order, directed from the center of the pit by the house's most accomplished athlete and marked by live music: warm-up, lifting wooden boards while lying face up, push-ups to the beat of the drum, mace swinging, rapid spinning in place and swinging of the iron bow, the *kabbadeh*, strung with rings. Wrestling closes the session and was its culminating act until the 1940s, and it was practiced in pairs set apart from the rest to avoid the public shame of defeat.
>
> That manner of managing defeat proves significant for the whole block. Krawietz (2013, pp. 144-166) records that the contenders were frequently separated before the decisive throw, precisely to spare one of the two the humiliation of losing before the others, a practice already documented in Chehabi's (1995) account of the contemporary zurkhaneh.
>
> For the essay, the separation of the wrestlers before the decisive bout repeats, nearly two thousand one hundred years after the Achaemenid king who never appears wrestling in official stone, the same discomfort before an uncertain outcome, and it supports the function of distinction in its dimension of honor, because losing in public remains intolerable regardless of the social class of the one competing.

### Diapositiva 64 · The chatir

**`Notas del orador`**

> The *shatir* were the royal foot couriers of the Persian court, tasked with running ahead of the monarch's horse to warn of dangers. In the Safavid period they also formed a guild with its own entrance test, the *chatir* or *shater*: the candidate had to carry twelve arrows on foot, one by one, from a pillar located a league and a half from the gate of the Isfahan palace, that is, twelve trips there and twelve back, some hundred and seventy-four kilometres between sunrise and sunset. The French merchant Chardin witnessed the test on 26 May 1667 and recorded that the winner that year took close to fourteen hours, which yields an average pace near 4:50 min/km, and he also mentions another runner who, in earlier times, had covered the same distance in twelve hours.
>
> This is the best-documented physical entrance examination in the whole history of Persia, and it belongs to the Safavid layer of the seventeenth century, not the Achaemenid one, and the comparison between the fourteen hours of one year and the twelve of another constitutes, over an identical distance and a fixed route, an early example of comparing performances without a stopwatch.
>
> For the essay, the chatir combines the function of distinction, because entry into the corps of royal couriers constituted an honour reserved for whoever passed the test, with the function of spectacle, because the day was declared a public holiday and the whole city followed the candidate and offered him fruit and water along the way.

### Diapositiva 65 · Equestrian sports

**`Notas del orador`**

> The horse is the only element present across the six historical stages of Persia, and each society devised on it practices of a different function, as the slide itself summarises: the courtier plays chovgan to be seen by the king, the army practises jereed to train its cavalry, and the local lord pays for buzkashi to demonstrate that he holds sufficient authority to organise an event. The precondition on which all these practices depend is owning a horse, and a war horse or a games horse requires selective breeding, fodder, stabling and staff, a cost that restricts access to those who can afford it.
>
> For the essay, this slide clearly sustains the function of distinction, because the cost of maintaining a horse acts as a social filter separating the aristocracy from the rest of the population in the three practices that follow.

### Diapositiva 66 · Chovgan

**`Notas del orador`**

> Chovgan is the first team sport played on horseback with a ball and a mallet from which modern polo derives. Knauth (1976) concluded that the Achaemenids did not know it, and Chehabi and Guttmann (2002, pp. 384-400) attribute it with probability to the Parthians, placing the earliest surviving mention in the *Karnamag i Ardashir*, where the future founder of the Sasanian dynasty wins the favour of the last Parthian king by playing better than the king's own sons. The rules were not fixed, as the slide notes, and the figures vary from one source to another: five players per side in the sixteenth century, fifteen or twenty in the seventeenth, according to the accounts of travellers such as Membré and Chardin, against the four players per team of present-day polo, which is a nineteenth-century British codification.
>
> The literature clarifies a detail that the slide includes without dating it, the night-time variant with a lit ball. That practice exists and is documented, although it belongs to the Mughal emperor Akbar (r. 1556-1605), a contemporary of Shah Abbas but not Persian, who played at night with luminous balls made of wood from the *palas* tree (Chehabi and Guttmann, 2002, p. 392). The ravines, the tribute to the sun and the prizes of slaves sometimes associated with this variant are not supported in any source.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Chehabi, H. E., y Guttmann, A. (2002). From Iran to all of Asia: The origin and diffusion of polo. *The International Journal of the History of Sport*, *19*(2-3), 384-400 (en particular p. 392).

### Diapositiva 67 · Chovgan as military training

**`Notas del orador`**

> Chovgan served as training for war, aimed at developing agility, skill and rapid decision-making under conditions that replicated a shifting battlefield, so that riders would grow accustomed to controlling their mount in combat. Several treatises recommended playing it with prudence to avoid fatal collisions, a recommendation also recorded in the eleventh-century *Qabusnama*, a manual on the education of princes that advises the young man to play at most once or twice a year and to position himself cautiously at one end of the field (Chehabi and Guttmann, 2002, pp. 384-400). The passion for the game led Shah Abbas I to order the construction, in 1598, of the Naqsh-e Jahan square in Isfahan, conceived at once as a main square and a playing field, with a palace balcony from which to watch the matches.
>
> For the essay, this slide clearly sustains the function of training for war, and qualifies it with the warning of moderation that Persian literature itself incorporated against the risk of the game.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Chehabi, H. E., y Guttmann, A. (2002). From Iran to all of Asia: The origin and diffusion of polo. *The International Journal of the History of Sport*, *19*(2-3), 384-400.

### Diapositiva 68 · Jereed

**`Notas del orador`**

> From the eleventh century onward another equestrian sport of a military nature developed, jereed, a javelin game on horseback designed to train attack and retreat in combat. Two teams of eight to ten riders face each other in open ground: one rider approaches the opposing side, throws his javelin and rides off at a gallop, while his rivals chase him to strike him with blunted javelins, and points are earned by hitting the body of the pursued rider, driving him off the field or catching him, while he in turn can score points by catching a javelin in mid-air. Today it is practised chiefly in Turkey under the name *cirit*.
>
> The literature confirms and reinforces the geographical distribution the slide suggests: Chehabi and Guttmann (2002, pp. 384-400) note that the Seljuk Turks and, later, the Ottomans also played chovgan, although they preferred *cirit*, which corroborates that jereed constitutes the equestrian practice characteristic of the Turkic world, as against chovgan, proper to the Persian sphere.
>
> For the essay, jereed sustains the function of training for war, because it directly reproduces the sequence of attack and retreat that cavalry combat demands.

> Ubaidulloev, Z. (2015). The history and characteristics of traditional sports in Central Asia: Tajikistan. *Bulletin of the Faculty of Health and Sport Sciences, University of Tsukuba*, *38*, 43-58.

### Diapositiva 69 · Buzkashi

**`Notas del orador`**

> Buzkashi pits riders against each other in a contest over the body of a headless goat or calf carried to a goal, and in its traditional form it lacks a delimited field, teams and a fixed number of participants, so that each rider plays for himself, held only by a prior oath of fair play that excludes from the match whoever does not swear it. What is distinctive about the game is who organises it: matches are held at the invitation of a lord who bears all the costs, and what he seeks by doing so is to demonstrate his authority by ensuring that a chaotic and ungovernable event turns out well, risking public failure. In the 1960s the Afghan Olympic Committee promoted a regulated version, with a bounded field and teams, and today it is the national sport of Afghanistan, with leagues in Mongolia, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan.
>
> For the essay, buzkashi sustains the function of distinction of the sponsor rather than of the riders, because whoever organises the match risks his public prestige on a game without a referee and without written rules reaching an orderly outcome, a mechanism that connects with the absence of a visible referee already noted in the Egyptian section.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Azoy, G. W. (2011). *Buzkashi: Game and power in Afghanistan*. Waveland Press.

### Diapositiva 73 · Historical introduction

**`Notas del orador`**

> Phoenicia extended over a coastal strip of some two hundred kilometres between the Lebanon mountain range and the sea, occupied by independent and often rival city states, among which Byblos, Sidon and Tyre stood out, sharing language, religion and ethnic traits without ever forming a unified state. Its period of expansion, between 1200 and 539 BC, arises from a weakness that was not its own, the collapse of the great empires of the late Bronze Age, which left the eastern Mediterranean without fleets capable of contesting the sea with them. The territory shapes the resulting economy, because the arable strip proves narrow and agriculture never sustained the population, a deficiency that acts as the engine of later expansion. Against that limitation they had three resources available, the cedar and cypress timber that neither Egypt nor Mesopotamia produced, a set of processing industries that turn raw material into a product of high value, among which purple dye extracted from the murex mollusc stands out alongside glass and ivory, and a coastline furnished with sheltered harbours and defensible islands.
>
> On that basis a maritime expansion is organised whose ultimate destination is the silver of the southwest of the Iberian Peninsula, situated some four thousand six hundred kilometres from the mother city, which Tyre needed in order to satisfy the tribute demanded by the Neo-Assyrian empire (Díes Cusí, 1994, pp. 311-336).
>
> For the essay, the imbalance between an insufficient agricultural territory and commercial resources of high value already sustains the function of merchandise as the most determinant in Phoenician history, a condition that will become explicit in the slides devoted to work at sea.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> López-Ruiz, C. (2022). *Phoenicians and the making of the Mediterranean*. Harvard University Press.
> Díes Cusí, E. (1994). Aspectos técnicos de las rutas comerciales fenicias en el Mediterráneo Occidental (s. IX-VII a.C.). *Archivo de Prehistoria Levantina*, *21*, 311-336.

### Diapositiva 74 · Colonial expansion

**`Notas del orador`**

> Phoenician expansion reached North Africa, Malta, western Sicily, Sardinia, the Balearic Islands and the south and southeast of the Iberian Peninsula, through settlements that functioned as trading posts and safe harbours for exchange, always sited next to an islet or an easily defended point, a river mouth or a sheltered anchorage. Carthage, founded by settlers from Tyre in present day Tunisia, occupies the midpoint of the route between the mother city and Cadiz, and literary tradition places its founding in 814 BC. When Tyre falls under subjection, Carthage inherits the network of trading posts thanks to its wealth and its central position, and goes on to direct it autonomously, to the point of contesting the western Mediterranean with Rome.
>
> The library adds precision to the archaeological controversy surrounding that date, which the screen does not develop, the oldest Greek materials found on the Byrsa hill initially pointed toward 760 BC, while recent radiocarbon dating of faunal remains in the lower levels has produced a range between 850 and 730 BC, which brings the material record closer again to the date transmitted by tradition (Docter et al., 2008, pp. 379-422). Six hundred years later, around 227 BC, Hasdrubal the Fair founded on the site of Mastia a second *Qart-ḥadašt*, which Rome would take in 209 BC and translate as *Carthago Nova*, from which the name Cartagena derives, so that the toponym means "new city" twice over.
>
> For the essay, the founding of Carthage sustains the function of merchandise, because the entire network of Phoenician colonies responds to the logic of a commercial trading post oriented toward the acquisition of silver and not to a project of agricultural settlement.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

### Diapositiva 75 · Phoenician society

**`Notas del orador`**

> Each Phoenician city was governed by a hereditary king whose power was limited by a council of elders drawn from the great merchant families and by an assembly of citizens. Society revolved around trade and the temple, an aristocracy of shipowners and merchants occupied the summit, a broad body of specialised artisans extended beneath it, and the temple functioned at once as sanctuary, bank and guarantor of treaties. The most lasting cultural contribution of the Phoenicians was the alphabet, a system of twenty two consonantal signs derived from a repertoire of Egyptian signs simplified around 1800 BC by Semitic workers in the mines of Sinai, which the Phoenicians fixed and distributed across the Mediterranean on their own ships, and which the Greeks would later adopt, adding vowels to it.
>
> The library adds a detail that reinforces the political balance described on screen, Aristotle analyses that Phoenician constitution in his *Politics* (II, 11) and praises it, placing it alongside those of Sparta and Crete among the well governed regimes, which constitutes one of the few occasions on which a Greek author speaks favourably of the Phoenician cities.

### Diapositiva 76 · The problem of the sources

**`Notas del orador`**

> No description of physical activity written by a Phoenician has survived, since their literature has been almost entirely lost, so that whatever is known of them comes from their rivals and commercial competitors, the Greeks and the Romans. The bias is easy to trace, in the *Iliad* they are barely named and only to praise their craftsmanship, while in the *Odyssey* they appear as good sailors also described as greedy and deceitful pirates, a stereotype later reinforced by the Punic Wars against Rome. To this is added a material problem, wood, papyrus and cloth do not withstand the climate of Lebanon, the cities remain inhabited and built over themselves, which prevents extensive excavation, and much of the record remains underwater, in a discipline that has barely sixty years behind it. The principal Phoenician sources come, as a result, from coins and metal bowls, from which it follows that the Phoenicians' work has reached us before their sport.
>
> The library illustrates that bias with an instructive case that the screen does not include. Rawlinson (1889), professor of ancient history at Oxford, deduced the physical character of the Phoenicians from the dress conventions of their iconography, concluding that they were "for orientals" unusually tough because they seldom wore sandals, a procedure that a recent study of a coin from Sidon dismantles in detail from the very same kind of argument (Nitschke, 2024, pp. 29-46). It is likewise relevant that the recent reference work on ancient sport, *A Cultural History of Sport in Antiquity*, expressly excludes the Near East despite its title, which confirms that absence of study does not equate to absence of practice, it equates to absence of a favourable source.
>
> For the essay, the very structure of the evidence already sustains the function of merchandise as the most documentable of Phoenician practices, since what has survived most richly is the record of their commercial activity and not that of their bodily practices.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Quinn, J. C. (2019). Phoenicians and Carthaginians in classical literature. En C. López-Ruiz y B. R. Doak (Eds.), *The Oxford handbook of the Phoenician and Punic Mediterranean* (pp. 671-683). Oxford University Press.
> Rawlinson, G. (1889). *History of Phoenicia*. Longmans, Green, and Co.

### Diapositiva 78 · Rowing

**`Notas del orador`**

> The reliefs of the palace of Sargon II at Dur-Sharrukin, around 710 BC, depict small oared vessels transporting cedar timber, towing logs along the coast, with a horse's head carved on the prow, to the point that the Greeks called those ships *hippoi*, "horses." That figurehead is, of all the nautical innovations attributed to the Phoenicians, the only one that withstands verification through three independent lines of evidence, the textual, the iconographic and the onomastic, given that it came to lend its name to an entire class of vessel (Casson, 1971, p. 66). Rowing is also documented in other types of ship, the war galley moved by continuous rowing, with the whole crew pulling at once, and raised sail only on long crossings, while the cargo ship sailed under canvas and kept three or four oars per side for harbour manoeuvres or for an emergency.
>
> The library adds a clarification about who did the rowing, a question the screen does not address and one relevant to this block. The image of the rower chained to his bench belongs to the early modern period, and as a significant naval institution it does not date back further than the fifteenth century, in Athens the rowers of war fleets were free citizens who were paid to row, while in the Roman merchant marine it was indeed common for the crew, and even the captain, to be enslaved (Casson, 1971, pp. 322-328). Regarding Phoenician crews properly speaking there is not sufficient data to affirm one thing or the other.
>
> For the essay, the collective and synchronised effort of the war galley, with the cadence imposed from outside and no room for the rower to regulate his own rhythm, sustains the function of training for war in its naval aspect.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Casson, L. (1971). *Ships and seamanship in the ancient world* (pp. 66 y 322-328). Princeton University Press.

### Diapositiva 79 · An approach from experimental archaeology

**`Notas del orador`**

> Thucydides, the Greek historian and military commander of the fifth century BC, wrote that the crew of a warship fed on barley cakes and wine and slept in shifts while another group rowed, and that a Greek ship could cover three hundred and forty kilometres in twenty four hours at a sustained speed close to fourteen kilometres per hour. To test that testimony, in 1987 a team of archaeologists launched a full scale reconstruction of a Greek trireme, the *Olympias*, with one hundred and seventy rowers on three levels, oars weighing twelve kilograms and benches set eighty centimetres above the floor. The results yielded sustained speeds of between six and nine kilometres per hour depending on sea conditions, with acceleration from a standstill to fourteen kilometres per hour in thirty two seconds, and an endurance of up to five hours of rowing with rests of twenty minutes for every forty of activity.
>
> It should be noted that the reconstruction documents the Athenian model, not the Phoenician one, whose internal arrangement of benches differed from the Greek in seeking greater beam to house the three levels of rowers within the hull (Casson, 1971, pp. 94-95), so that the experiment serves to establish what the technique of trireme rowing demands physically, not to attribute the invention to a particular culture. Casson further notes that simply asking how fast an ancient ship sailed makes little sense, because speed depends above all on wind direction, to the point that a hundred miles against the wind could cost the same as two hundred with it (Casson, 1971, pp. 281-282).

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Morrison, J. S., Coates, J. F., y Rankov, N. B. (2000). *The Athenian trireme: The history and reconstruction of an ancient Greek warship* (2.ª ed.). Cambridge University Press.
> Rankov, B. (Ed.). (2012). *Trireme Olympias: The final report*. Oxbow.
> Krivec, R. (2016). Rowing times from Athens to Mytilene. *The International Journal of Nautical Archaeology*, *45*(1), 199-201.
> Casson, L. (1971). *Ships and seamanship in the ancient world* (pp. 94-95 y 281-282). Princeton University Press.

### Diapositiva 80 · Navigation and life at sea

**`Notas del orador`**

> Navigation was the activity par excellence of Phoenician culture, practised without compass, without nautical charts and without any instrument of measurement, with one technical advantage over their competitors, the Greeks steered by Ursa Major, while the Phoenicians steered by Ursa Minor, closer to the fixed point around which the sky turns and, for that reason, more stable throughout the night. Crews ranged between six and twenty men on cargo vessels and between two and five on the *hippoi*, including a captain, rowers and a carpenter for repairs, and sleeping took place on deck or directly on the cargo, with food consisting of nuts, pine nuts and olives together with what was fished. Navigation was concentrated between spring and early autumn, and outside that season wind and bad weather halted activity for months, which determined when fleets departed, when they returned and when an entire port city went hungry.
>
> The library adds a curious detail that illustrates well the technical recognition, even a hostile one, that the ancients granted to Phoenician seafaring skill, they called the Pole Star *Phoinike*, "the star of the Phoenicians" (Díes Cusí, 1994, pp. 311-336), which contrasts with the literary hostility noted in the sources slide.
>
> For the essay, the calendar of navigation sustains the function of merchandise in its most literal form, because it determines when income is generated, when it is collected and when the sustenance of an entire urban economy dependent on the sea runs short.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Ruiz, J. A. M., y Carretero, J. R. G. (2024). La vida cotidiana a bordo de un barco mercante fenicio. *ISIMU. Revista de Oriente Próximo y Egipto en la Antigüedad*, *27*(27), 119-148.
> Díes Cusí, E. (1994). Aspectos técnicos de las rutas comerciales fenicias en el Mediterráneo Occidental (s. IX-VII a.C.). *Archivo de Prehistoria Levantina*, *21*, 311-336.

### Diapositiva 81 · The Mazarrón shipwrecks

**`Notas del orador`**

> On the beach of the Isla de Mazarrón two small sunken coastal merchant ships were located. Mazarrón 1 appeared in 1988, of which mainly the keel is preserved; Mazarrón 2 appeared in 1995 with the hull almost entire, measuring 8.15 metres in length by 2.25 in beam and 0.90 in height, it was raised from the water between September and November of 2024 and is today held at the Museo Nacional de Arqueología Subacuática, in Cartagena. The hull combines different types of timber according to the function of each piece, cypress, pine, fig and olive, with the planking joined by mortise, tenon and peg, while the frames are stitched together with esparto cord. Inside it carried some 2,800 kilograms of lead ingots from the mines of Cartagena and Mazarrón, an anchor of wood and lead, a hand mill and an amphora for fresh water.
>
> The library adds precision through carbon 14 analysis, which places the vessel in the first third of the sixth century BC. The cultural attribution of the wreck, which the screen does not discuss, is moreover open, Negueruela reads it as Phoenician, relying on the fact that the mortise and tenon system is attributed to the Semitic sphere, while Pomey and Guerrero maintain that it was built by local carpenters who knew that technique, a reading they consider more plausible for a small vessel engaged in regional coastal trade, so that the wreck documents less a closed ethnic affiliation than a technical contact between shipbuilding traditions.
>
> For the essay, the wreck sustains the function of merchandise with particular force, because its cargo of litharge proves that the object that has survived from the Phoenician presence on this coast is, literally, the residue of the silver industry that sustained the entire commercial system described in the previous section.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> De Juan, C., y Sabio, R. (2025). El pecio Mazarrón 2 (Murcia): la extracción y conservación de una embarcación de época fenicia. *PH: Boletín del Instituto Andaluz del Patrimonio Histórico*, *115*, 244-265.

### Diapositiva 84 · The Children of Kharayeb

**`Notas del orador`**

> The only set of objects that shows Phoenician bodies in playful activity comes from the sanctuary of Kharayeb, inland from Tyre, and consists of fired clay figurines, sixteen thousand four hundred fragments in total, of which children account for thirty-five percent and women for twenty-two. The figurines allow a sequence to be arranged by age: between two and four years old, children appear playing with birds and dogs; between five and eight, the dog is replaced by a ball, a change interpreted as the shift from free play to rule-governed play; and toward twelve or fourteen they appear with a strigil and an oil flask, elements proper to the Greek gymnasium. The figures are interpreted as sacred offerings linked to rites of passage, in which each piece symbolizes the stage and the toy that the child left behind on facing the next one (Castiglione, 2020, pp. 97-126).
>
> The library adds a chronological nuance that the screen does not develop and that proves important for situating the finding. Although the sanctuary functioned between the seventh and first centuries BCE, nearly all of the figurines related to play, sport and school already belong to the Hellenistic phase, so that what the assemblage documents is the adoption of a Greek educational model in an already Hellenized Phoenicia, and not a properly Phoenician playful practice in its earliest phase (Castiglione, 2020, pp. 97-126). Within that Hellenistic assemblage, moreover, children with a strigil represent only 0.03% of the total figurines, the first properly sporting piece to appear in the whole Phoenician corpus, albeit in a minimal proportion. In the same sanctuary and in that of Eshmun, at Bustan esh-Sheikh, numerous astragals and glass counters have also been collected, which functioned at once as toy and as offering.
>
> For the essay, Kharayeb supports the educational function, because it documents the ritualized transition from free play to the discipline of the gymnasium at the precise moment in which the Phoenician cities enter the Greek cultural orbit.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> Castiglione, M. (2020). Pleasure before duty: Playing and studying in the cultic context of Kharayeb. *Pallas*, *114*, 97-126.

### Diapositiva 85 · Female Practices

**`Notas del orador`**

> The female bodily practices documented in the Phoenician sphere are scarce and are limited to the funerary domain. Women took part as musicians or dancers in the procession that accompanied the deceased to the necropolis and during the burial itself, and they also appear represented as mourners, tearing their garments in grief at the loss. Their presence in music and dance is overwhelming compared with any other bodily record of the corpus, which has been interpreted as an acknowledgment of a role proper to women in the funerary space, and it is speculated that professional musicians took part in the funerals of elite families.
>
> The library adds evidence that reinforces and refines what the screen presents. Herodotus (2.79) documents the so-called "song of Linus" as a Phoenician-Cypriot funerary lament, and the sarcophagus of Ahiram, king of Byblos, from the tenth century BCE, already depicts mourners tearing at their hair. The evidence is not only literary: in the tombs of Puig des Molins, the necropolis of Ibiza in use from the seventh century BCE, and in those of Carthage, bronze cymbals and bells of a function interpreted as prophylactic have been recovered, together with figurines of women playing a hand drum, also found at Tharros between the seventh and second centuries BCE (López-Bertran and Garcia-Ventura, 2012, pp. 393-408).
>
> For the essay, this slide clearly supports the ritual function, because the only bodily domain acknowledged to Phoenician women is the one that accompanies death and the passage to the afterlife, and it is precisely there that their presence appears overwhelmingly represented against their near absence in any other record of the corpus.

**`Fuente`** (8 pt)

> López-Bertran, M., y Garcia-Ventura, A. (2012). Music, gender and rituals in the ancient Mediterranean: Revisiting the Punic evidence. *World Archaeology*, *44*(3), 393-408.

### Diapositiva 86 · Why Do We Not Have Phoenician Sport?

**`Notas del orador`**

> The closing on Egypt had shown that the practices power invents last as long as that power lasts; Phoenicia poses the inverse question, what happens when no one has an interest in setting them down in writing. The Phoenician cities lacked a state to record feats, a king who needed to display his body to legitimize himself, and a common sanctuary in which to inscribe victors, so that what is missing is a type of source, a circumstance that in no way proves the absence of practice. When a record that captures bodies does appear, that of Kharayeb, children with balls, trained dogs and boys with a strigil turn up. The evidence from the final, already Hellenized stage points to organized structure: the Greek records note Diotimus of Sidon winning the chariot race at the Nemean Games around 200 BCE, and Aurelius Helix, "the Phoenician," winning at wrestling at Olympia in the early third century CE.
>
> The library expands these two episodes. Diotimus's epigram, composed in the manner of Pindar, does more than celebrate a victory: the inscription calls him "judge," a Greek term that probably translates the Phoenician office of suffete, so that the victor was the chief magistrate of his city, and the text invokes the house of the Agenorids and addresses "Thebes, the holy city of Cadmus," reminding the Greeks that the alphabet they use, the *phoinikeia grammata*, comes from the Phoenicians (Bonnet, 2019). In the case of Helix, a wrestler and pankratiast whom Philostratus simply calls "the Phoenician," three sources of a different nature confirm the episode, Philostratus himself, the historian Cassius Dio (79.10.2) and a prosopographical entry in the *Prosopographia Imperii Romani*, and it includes the fact that the Elean judges came close to excluding him from the two events he had entered and ultimately allowed him to claim only the pankration (Bowie, 2023, pp. 493-510).
>
> For the essay, Diotimus's epigram supports the function of collective identity in its most elaborate form, because a Sidonian magistrate turns a sporting victory won at Greek games into an argument of Phoenician cultural prestige addressed to the Greeks themselves.
